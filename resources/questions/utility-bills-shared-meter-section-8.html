Reader Question

How can my landlord bill me for utilities when the whole building is on one meter?

Asked September 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“My building runs on one meter, and since utilities stopped being included in my rent, my utility charges are higher than my share of the rent on Section 8. The landlord won't explain how they come up with the amounts, and I barely use anything because I'm on a tight fixed income. How can they bill me when everyone is on one meter?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

It depends on your state's law, your lease and Section 8 rules. Some states let landlords split one meter's bill among tenants by a formula (often called ratio billing), and some limit or ban it. On Section 8, a lease change that moves utilities onto you has to go through your housing authority, and you should get a utility allowance for utilities you pay.

How shared-meter billing works

When a building has one meter, the landlord gets one bill. To charge each tenant, some landlords divide it using a formula based on things like apartment size or the number of people. This is often called ratio utility billing, or RUBS. It's an estimate, not your actual use, which is why your bill can be high even when you barely use anything.

Whether this is allowed depends on your state, and sometimes your city. Some states require a lease term that explains the formula, copies of the bills, or limits on fees. Some restrict or ban it. Check your state page and ask legal aid.

What Section 8 rules say

  • Moving utilities onto you is a big change. If the lease changes who pays for utilities, your Section 8 assistance can't continue unless the housing authority approves the new tenancy and signs a new contract with the landlord (24 CFR 982.308(g)(2)(i)). Any lease change must be in writing, and the landlord must give the housing authority a copy (24 CFR 982.308(g)(1)).
  • You should get a utility allowance. Housing authorities must keep a utility allowance for tenant-paid utilities (24 CFR 982.517). It lowers your share of the rent to help cover those bills. If the landlord charges a flat utility fee, the housing authority can base your allowance on that fee only if the fee is no more than its allowance.
  • The rent gets checked. Before approving a lease, the housing authority must find that the rent is reasonable (24 CFR 982.507). Rent that used to include utilities may not be reasonable once they're taken out.
  • You can dispute the allowance. You can ask for an informal hearing on the utility allowance the housing authority used for you (24 CFR 982.555(a)(1)(ii)).

What to do now

  1. Find your lease and anything the landlord asked you to sign about utilities. Did you sign a new lease or an addendum?
  2. Write the landlord and ask for the formula, the building's actual utility bills, and how your share was figured.
  3. Tell your caseworker in writing that utilities moved from the rent to you. Ask whether the housing authority approved the change and updated your utility allowance and rent share.
  4. If you're behind on the bills, ask about LIHEAP or other utility help, and keep a record of every payment.
  5. Don't stop paying rent over this without talking to legal aid first.

What to write

"Utilities used to be included in my rent and are now billed to me from a shared meter. Please send me the formula you use, the building's utility bills for the last six months, and how my charge was figured."

To your caseworker: "My landlord started billing me for utilities that used to be included in my rent. Did you approve this change? Please review my utility allowance and my rent share."

If they say no

If the landlord won't explain the bills, or the housing authority never approved the change, ask the housing authority to step in. Free legal aid or your state attorney general's consumer protection office can tell you whether the billing is legal where you live.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
