Reader Question

I was locked out with my child because I'm not on the lease. Is that legal?

Asked August 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“They put me and my child out of the apartment and changed the lock, with no notice, because I'm not on the lease. My things are still inside and they'll only let me in tomorrow to get them. I have nowhere to stay. Is this legal?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Almost never. In almost every state, no one can change the locks and put out a person who lives in the home without going to court first, and in many states that protects people who aren't on the lease too. A short-term guest may have fewer rights. Today, call 211 for a place to sleep, the police non-emergency line, and legal aid.

What the law says

In almost every state, a landlord can't remove someone on their own: no changing the locks, taking off the door, shutting off utilities or taking your things out. To evict, they have to go to court and win. If they skip that, it is an illegal lockout.

Not being on the lease doesn't always mean you have no rights. In many states, a person who really lives in the home (gets mail there, keeps belongings there, sleeps there) is protected too. For example, New York law makes it illegal to remove any occupant who has lawfully lived in a unit for 30 days in a row or more without a court order, and it specifically bans changing the lock without giving that person a key.

An important exception: a guest who stayed only a few days may have fewer rights. In some states, a guest can be asked to leave without a court case. It depends on the state and on how long you lived there. See your state's page.

If the person who put you out was not the landlord but the person on the lease (a partner or relative), the rules can be similar. If there was violence or threats, call 911 or the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233.

What to do today

  1. A place for tonight. Call 211, say you have your child with you and nowhere to stay, and ask for a family shelter.
  2. Call the police non-emergency line and say you were locked out without a court order. In some places police will help you get back in; in others, they can at least stand by while you get your things.
  3. Get free legal help today and say "illegal lockout." In many states a judge can order that you be let back in, sometimes within days.
  4. Gather proof you lived there: mail or bills with that address, your ID, texts with the landlord, photos of your things in the apartment, your child's school papers.

Tomorrow, when you go for your things

  • Bring someone you trust, and if you can, ask police to come.
  • Take photos or video of everything before you move it. List anything missing or damaged.
  • Take the most important things first: documents, medicine, your child's clothes and school things.
  • Don't sign anything saying you are leaving by choice unless you understand it. If you run out of time, ask in writing (a text works) for more.

Taking your things doesn't mean giving up your rights. If you want to move back in, tell legal aid first.

What to write

"I have lived at [address] since [month and year]. On [date] the lock was changed and my child and I were put out without a court order. Please give me access right away and do not touch my belongings."

If they say no

Keep everything in writing and keep going with legal aid: in many states, an illegal lockout gives you the right to get back in and to collect damages. If you can't reach legal aid, 211 can give you other contacts.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
