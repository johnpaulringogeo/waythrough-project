Reader Question

What can I do about an old eviction and a balance the landlord says I owe?

Asked October 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I have an eviction on my record from a few years ago. I lost my job and moved out on my own, and I just found out the old landlord says I owe thousands of dollars. Is there any help for this?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Yes, there are steps you can take. First find out whether the balance is a court judgment or a collection account. If a debt collector is involved, you can dispute the debt in writing, your state's time limit may apply, and some states let you seal old eviction records. Free legal aid can help with all of it.

First, find out what you're dealing with

  • The court case. Search your county court's online records, or call the clerk, and see how the case ended: a money judgment, a dismissal, or a move-out agreement.
  • The balance. The landlord may have sent it to a debt collector, and it may show on your credit report or a tenant screening report. Get your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and look for it. Ask for an itemized list of charges, including what came out of your security deposit.

If a debt collector contacts you

Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, a collector must send you a notice about the debt. If you dispute it in writing within 30 days of receiving that notice, the collector must stop collecting until it mails you verification of the debt (15 U.S.C. 1692g(b)). After the 30 days pass, you can still dispute, but the collector doesn't have to pause, so act fast. These rules cover collection agencies; a landlord collecting its own debt usually isn't covered.

Time limits

Every state has a statute of limitations, a deadline to sue over a debt. It's often three to six years but varies by state and type of debt. A collector can't sue or threaten to sue on a debt past that deadline (12 CFR 1006.26(b)). But in some states, a payment or admitting you owe it can restart the clock. Talk to legal aid before you pay anything on an old debt. If the court already entered a judgment, different, usually longer, limits apply.

Reports have limits too. Most negative items, including old lawsuits, judgments and collection accounts, can generally be reported for seven years; a judgment can stay longer if your state's time limit is longer (15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)). A record from a few years ago may still show for a while, so plan to explain it.

Sealing

Some states let courts seal or hide eviction records, especially cases that were dismissed, settled or are old. Others don't. Ask legal aid what your state allows.

What to do now

  1. Pull the court record and your credit reports.
  2. If a collector wrote to you in the last 30 days, mail a written dispute now and keep a copy.
  3. Call free legal aid about the time limit and sealing before you pay.
  4. If you settle, get the deal in writing first, then a letter saying the debt is paid or settled.
  5. On applications, explain briefly: you lost your job, moved out on your own, and have rented responsibly since.

What to write to a collector

"I dispute this debt. Please send verification of the amount, an itemized list of charges, and the name and address of the original creditor. Please stop collection until you do."

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
