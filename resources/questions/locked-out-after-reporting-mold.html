Reader Question

I was locked out after reporting mold and mice, and now my voucher is threatened. What can I do?

Asked October 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I was locked out of my home after I reported mice, mold and water damage. They moved me to a unit with no hot water, and I believe they're using my repair reports to get money. I've gotten sick, my caseworker is threatening my voucher, and legal aid, the health department and the building inspector haven't helped. Who can I contact?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

A landlord can't lock you out without a court order, and in most states can't punish you for reporting repairs. Put everything in writing to your housing authority, ask for an inspection of the unit you were moved to, and ask for a hearing before any decision on your voucher. You can report suspected fraud to HUD's Office of Inspector General at 1-800-347-3735.

What the rule says

  • No lockouts. With a Housing Choice Voucher, the owner "may only evict the tenant from the unit by instituting a court action" (24 CFR 982.310(f)). State law says the same in almost every state. Changing the locks or removing you without a court order is an illegal lockout.
  • No retaliation. Most states forbid landlords from evicting you, raising rent or cutting services because you complained about conditions or called an inspector. The rules and time limits vary; see your state's page.
  • Your new unit must be livable. HUD's standards require hot and cold running water in the kitchen and bathroom (24 CFR 5.703(d)(1)). Mold and a mouse infestation are failing items too.
  • Your voucher has protections. Before a housing authority ends your assistance because of something you did or didn't do, it must offer you an informal hearing (24 CFR 982.555). An owner's failure to make repairs is not a reason to take your voucher. If the owner doesn't fix failed items, the housing authority stops paying the owner and must give you your voucher to move (24 CFR 982.404).

Why your caseworker may be worried

Voucher families must tell the housing authority and the owner before moving out (24 CFR 982.551(f)). Leaving a unit without notice can look like breaking the rules. So tell the housing authority in writing why you can't stay (no hot water, your health) and ask in writing for an inspection and for your voucher to move.

What to do now

  1. Write one timeline: when you reported the problems, the lockout, the move, the hot water problem, and every call you made.
  2. Email or hand-deliver a letter to your caseworker and their supervisor. Ask for an inspection of the unit, a written answer about your voucher, and an informal hearing before any termination.
  3. Keep copies of work orders, texts and photos. Don't sign anything you don't understand.
  4. If the housing authority won't respond, call HUD's Public and Indian Housing customer service line at 800-955-2232. HUD's Inspector General sends voucher, maintenance and eviction complaints there.
  5. If you believe someone is billing for work that wasn't done, or lying to get program money, report it to the HUD OIG Hotline at 1-800-347-3735 or online. The OIG investigates fraud, not everyday disputes, so give dates and documents.
  6. Call legal aid again and use the words "illegal lockout" and "retaliation." If that office can't help, ask for a referral to a law school clinic, a tenant union or a fair housing group.
  7. See a doctor and keep the records.

What to write

"On [date] I was locked out of [address] after I reported mice, mold and water damage. I was moved to [address], which has no hot water. I am asking for an inspection of that unit, a written explanation of any action on my voucher, and an informal hearing before any decision to end my assistance."

If they say no

If you get a notice that your assistance will end, ask for the hearing in writing before the deadline in the letter. If the deadline has passed, ask anyway and explain why you missed it. Bring your timeline and proof. In many states, a lawyer can also ask a court to let you back in or to award money for an illegal lockout.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
