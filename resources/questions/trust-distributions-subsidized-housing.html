Reader Question

Will taking trust distributions affect my subsidized housing?

Asked October 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I live in subsidized housing in Maine on a small fixed income plus part-time work. A family member set up an irrevocable trust, and I'm thinking of asking for monthly distributions for a couple of years. My rent would probably go up, but would I be able to stay eligible for my apartment?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Possibly, but it depends on how the payments count. Under HUD's updated (HOTMA) rules, payments of principal from an irrevocable trust you don't control aren't counted as income, while payments of what the trust earned are. Ask your housing provider in writing how it would count the distributions before you take them.

What the rules say

The trust itself. If a trust can't be revoked by, and isn't controlled by, anyone in your household, its value is not one of your family's assets (24 CFR 5.603, "net family assets"). That matters because HUD programs have an asset limit (24 CFR 5.618).

The payments. Under the HOTMA income rule (24 CFR 5.609(b)(2)), for an irrevocable trust outside your family's control:

  • Payments from the principal (the money put into the trust) are not counted as income.
  • Payments of income the trust earned (like interest or dividends) are counted, unless they pay for a minor's medical care.

The trustee can tell you which part of each payment is principal and which is earnings. Get that in writing.

When these rules apply. For housing authority programs (vouchers and public housing), HUD enforces the HOTMA rules for rent calculations that take effect on or after January 1, 2027 (Notice PIH 2026-15). Some housing authorities and privately owned HUD buildings are on other timelines. Under the older rules, regular trust payments may be counted as income, so ask which rules your provider uses now.

Money you keep. Once a payment is in your bank account, what you haven't spent can count as an asset. If your savings grow large, the asset limit or income figured on your assets can come into play.

Will you stay eligible?

Income limits mostly apply when you move in. After that, more counted income usually means higher rent, not losing your home. But it depends on your program:

  • Section 8 voucher: your share of rent goes up. If your share covers the whole rent for 180 days, your assistance contract ends (24 CFR 982.455).
  • Public housing: if your income stays over 120% of the area median (2.4 times the very low-income limit) for 24 months in a row, the housing authority applies its over-income policy, which can mean a much higher rent or having to move (24 CFR 960.507).
  • Project-based Section 8 and other HUD buildings: your rent usually rises with income, up to the building's full rent.
  • Tax credit (LIHTC) and USDA Rural Development buildings: these have their own rules. Ask your manager.

What to do now

  1. Before taking any distribution, ask the trustee for a written statement that the trust is irrevocable, who controls it, and whether payments would come from principal or earnings.
  2. Write your housing authority or property manager and ask how it would count those payments, both now and after January 1, 2027.
  3. Report the payments the way your lease and program require, usually at recertification or within a set number of days. Not reporting can lead to repaying assistance.
  4. Talk to a tax professional about taxes on the distributions.
  5. For a second opinion, contact legal aid. Our Maine page lists housing and legal help.

What to write

"I may receive monthly distributions from an irrevocable trust that no one in my household controls. The trustee says the payments would come from [principal / trust earnings]. Before I accept them, please tell me in writing how you would count these payments for my rent and eligibility, and whether you are using the current rules or the HOTMA rules."

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
