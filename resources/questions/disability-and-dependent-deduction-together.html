Reader Question

Do I get both the disability deduction and the $480 dependent deduction?

Asked October 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I'm on disability and get the $400 deduction. Do my family member and I also get the automatic standard $480 deduction? Does it matter what state I live in?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

There's no automatic standard deduction. Your household gets the elderly or disabled family deduction ($400 a year under the rules most housing authorities use now) once, plus a separate dependent deduction ($480 a year now) only for each member who counts as a dependent. These are federal amounts, so your state doesn't change them.

What the rule says

Federal rules list the deductions a housing authority subtracts from your yearly income before it figures your rent (24 CFR 5.611). Two of them are set dollar amounts:

  • Elderly or disabled family deduction: one per household, if the head of household, spouse or co-head is 62 or older or has a disability. It's $400 a year under the rules most housing authorities use now. It doesn't matter how many people in the home have a disability.
  • Dependent deduction: $480 a year for each dependent. A dependent is a household member, other than the head, spouse or co-head, who is under 18, has a disability, or is a full-time student (24 CFR 5.603). Foster children and foster adults don't count.

So the $480 isn't automatic. Your family member brings a $480 deduction only if they are a dependent. If they're your spouse or co-head, or an adult who has no disability and isn't a full-time student, there's no dependent deduction for them.

Examples

  • You have a disability and live with your child who is under 18: $400 + $480 = $880 a year.
  • You have a disability and live with an adult relative who also has a disability: $400 + $480 = $880 a year.
  • You and your spouse both have disabilities: $400 total, and no dependent deduction.

Deductions lower your counted income, not your rent dollar for dollar. An $880 deduction lowers your rent by about $22 a month (30% of $880, divided by 12).

The amounts are changing

A federal law called HOTMA raises these amounts. Under Notice PIH 2026-15, HUD will make housing authorities use the new rules for rent calculations that take effect on or after January 1, 2027: $575 for an elderly or disabled family and $525 per dependent. A few housing authorities switched early and used $550 and $500 in 2026. HUD adjusts these numbers for inflation every year. Housing authorities in HUD's Moving to Work program get a later date, and some of them set their own rent rules.

HOTMA also changes the medical expense deduction for elderly and disabled families. Today you can deduct unreimbursed medical costs above 3% of your income. Under HOTMA it's above 10%, with phased-in relief for families who already had this deduction. For many people with disabilities, the medical deduction is worth more than the $400.

Does your state matter?

No. These are federal amounts, the same in every state. What can differ is your housing authority: whether it has switched to the new rules yet, and, for Moving to Work agencies, its own rent policy.

What to do now

  1. Look at your rent calculation or rent notice. Check that it shows the disabled family deduction and a dependent deduction for each dependent.
  2. Report your medical costs, like insurance premiums, prescriptions and co-pays, with receipts.
  3. If a deduction is missing, ask in writing for a correction. If they won't fix it, you can ask for an informal hearing on how your income was figured (24 CFR 982.555(a)(1)(i)).

What to write

"Please explain the deductions used in my rent calculation. I believe my household qualifies for the disabled family deduction and a dependent deduction for [name], who is [under 18 / a person with a disability / a full-time student]. Please correct my rent if either was left out."

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
