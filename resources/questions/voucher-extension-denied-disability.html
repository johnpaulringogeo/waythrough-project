Reader Question

My housing authority won't extend my voucher, even as a disability accommodation. What can I do?

Asked June 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“My housing authority says my voucher expires soon and they won't give me any more extensions, even though I asked for more time as a reasonable accommodation for my disabilities. How do I get more time, and should I ask for an informal hearing? They also sent me to a "landlord" who took a holding fee and turned out not to own the house.”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Usually you can still get more time. When a disability is the reason you need longer to find a place, HUD's rule says the housing authority must extend your voucher as long as reasonably needed (24 CFR 982.303(b)(2)), even if its usual policy is "no more extensions." Put the request in writing before the voucher expires, and if they still say no, file a fair housing complaint.

What the rule says

Most voucher extensions are up to the housing authority (PHA). Its written policy, called the Administrative Plan, decides how many it gives (24 CFR 982.303(b)(1)). A disability changes that. If someone in your family has a disability, needs more search time because of it, and you ask for an extension as a reasonable accommodation, the PHA must extend the voucher “up to the term reasonably required” (24 CFR 982.303(b)(2)). A blanket “no more extensions” policy does not override that.

If the link between the disability and the extra time isn't obvious, the PHA can ask for proof of it. It does not need your diagnosis or your medical records.

Good to know: HUD's rules don't require an informal review when a PHA turns down an ordinary extension (24 CFR 982.554(c)(4)). A denied reasonable accommodation is different. It is a civil rights issue, so you have more ways to push back.

What to do now

  1. Write a new request today, before the voucher expires. Title it “Reasonable accommodation request.” Say how the disability made the search harder (for example, limited energy, needing an accessible unit, or trouble getting to showings) and how many more days you need.
  2. Attach a short letter from a doctor, therapist, caseworker or other provider saying you have a disability and that it slows your search. It doesn't have to name the condition.
  3. Send it in a way you can prove: email, the PHA's portal, or hand delivery with a date-stamped copy. Keep copies of everything.
  4. Ask for the answer in writing, with the reason, and ask for the name of the PHA's Section 504 or reasonable accommodation coordinator.
  5. Ask for an informal hearing or review anyway, in writing. Many PHAs offer one for accommodation denials, and asking costs nothing. Requests are often due within 10 to 14 days of the letter, so don't wait.
  6. Keep searching and keep a log of every unit and landlord you contact. It shows you are trying.

What to write

“I am asking for an extension of my voucher as a reasonable accommodation under 24 CFR 982.303(b)(2). Because of my disability, I need more time to find a unit that meets my needs. I am asking for 60 more days. A letter from my provider is attached. Please answer in writing before my voucher expires.”

If they say no

You can file a housing discrimination complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity, online or by phone at 1-800-669-9777. A Fair Housing Act complaint must be filed within one year (24 CFR 103.35), and a Section 504 complaint usually within 180 days, but file as soon as you can, because your voucher clock keeps running. Free legal aid can also call the PHA or help you at a hearing, and sometimes that alone fixes it.

About the fake landlord

Losing a holding fee to someone who didn't own the home is a crime, and it is not your fault. Report it to:

  • The Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
  • Your local police (ask for a report number)
  • Your state attorney general's consumer protection office

Keep every text, email and payment receipt. Tell the PHA in writing too. It may not repay you, but it creates a record, and if the scam cost you search time, say so in your accommodation request. Next time, before you pay anything, check the county property records to see who owns the home, and be wary of anyone who wants money to “hold” a place before you've seen it and signed a lease.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
