Reader Question

My landlord won't fix my broken fridge, oven and dishwasher. What can I do?

Asked September 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I have three repairs that keep getting worse. My dishwasher broke a year ago and the replacement doesn't work. The refrigerator broke, the replacement didn't work either, and they brought the old broken one back. The oven doesn't work, and nobody has come to look at it.”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Usually the landlord has to fix them if the landlord supplied them. A working refrigerator and a working stove or oven are required for a Section 8 unit to pass inspection, so report them in writing and ask your housing authority for an inspection. A dishwasher isn't on HUD's checklist, but your lease or state law may still require the landlord to repair it.

What the rule says

For a home rented with a Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8), HUD requires a kitchen with a cooking appliance and a refrigerator that work:

  • The older Housing Quality Standards (HQS) require "an oven, and a stove or range, and a refrigerator of appropriate size for the family," all "in proper operating condition."
  • HUD's newer NSPIRE standards say the same. They rate a stove or oven that won't heat as "severe," and a refrigerator that can't keep food safe as "moderate." In a voucher unit, both are failing items, and the owner usually has 30 days from written notice to fix them (24 CFR 982.404).
  • Voucher programs can use either checklist until February 1, 2027. After that date, all must use NSPIRE. Either way, a broken fridge or oven fails.

Who supplied it matters. Your lease, and Part A of the HAP contract between your landlord and the housing authority, list who provides the stove and refrigerator. If the owner provides them, the owner must keep them working. If you brought your own, they are your responsibility.

A broken replacement isn't a repair. Swapping one broken fridge for another, or bringing the old broken one back, still leaves the unit failing.

Dishwashers aren't on HUD's inspection list. But if your lease says the landlord provides one, or your state requires landlords to keep the appliances they supply working, you can still ask for the repair. Check your state's page.

If you don't have a voucher, most state and local housing codes still require landlords to repair the appliances they supply, especially the stove and refrigerator.

What to do now

  1. Make a simple list: each appliance, when it broke, each time you reported it, and what the landlord did.
  2. Take photos or short videos showing each one not working. A thermometer inside the fridge helps.
  3. Send one written repair request that covers all three, and keep a copy.
  4. Send a copy to your housing authority and ask for a special inspection. Say that you have no working oven and that food is spoiling.
  5. If the unit fails and the owner misses the deadline, the housing authority must stop its payments to the owner. Ask your caseworker what deadline the owner was given.
  6. Keep receipts for spoiled food, eating out, or a hot plate you had to buy. Legal aid can tell you whether you can get that money back.

What to write

"Three appliances in my unit don't work: the refrigerator (since [date]), the oven (since [date]) and the dishwasher (since [date]). The replacements you brought also don't work. Please repair or replace them with working appliances by [date]. I am sending a copy to my housing authority."

If they say no

If the landlord says the appliances are yours to fix, ask them to show where the lease or HAP contract says so. If the housing authority won't inspect, ask for a supervisor in writing, then call your city's code enforcement office. Don't stop paying rent to force the repairs without talking to legal aid first; the rules are strict and vary by state.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
