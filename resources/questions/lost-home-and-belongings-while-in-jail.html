Reader Question

I lost my apartment and my belongings while I was in jail. What can I do?

Asked September 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“While I was in jail for about a month, I lost my apartment and everything in it. I never got an eviction notice, nobody knows where my things are, and someone else is already living there. What can I do?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

You may have a claim. In most states a landlord can't end a tenancy, re-rent the unit or get rid of your belongings without following state law, which usually means notice and a court case, or set abandoned-property steps. Act fast: write down what you lost and contact legal aid, because claims have deadlines.

What the rule says

Going to jail doesn't end your lease. To take back an apartment, a landlord usually has to give written notice and win an eviction case in court. Changing the locks and renting to someone else without that is often an illegal eviction.

The big exception is abandonment. Many states let a landlord treat a unit as abandoned when rent goes unpaid and it looks like no one lives there. Even then, most states require steps first, such as posting or mailing a notice and waiting a set time.

Your belongings have their own rules. Many states require the landlord to give written notice and hold your things for a period before selling or throwing them away. In California, for example, the notice must give you at least 15 days to claim them, or 18 days if it was mailed (Civil Code 1983); after that date passes, the landlord may sell or dispose of what is left. Other states use different times, and a few have very few rules. Check your state's page.

If you had Section 8 or public housing, program rules apply too. A voucher family can't be away from the unit for more than 180 days in a row, and each housing authority sets its own, often shorter, policy for absences, including jail (24 CFR 982.312). A voucher landlord may evict only through court (24 CFR 982.310(f)). If the housing authority ended your assistance, you can ask for an informal hearing.

What to do now

  1. Write down the dates: when you went in, when you got out, when you last paid rent, and when you found out.
  2. List everything you lost with rough values. Gather proof you owned it: photos, receipts, bank or card statements.
  3. Check the court's online records, or call the clerk, to see if an eviction case was filed in your name. If there was a judgment you didn't know about, legal aid can tell you if it can be reopened.
  4. Send the landlord a short letter or text asking where your belongings are and asking for them back, or for a list of what was done with them. Keep a copy.
  5. If you had a voucher, call your housing authority now. Ask whether your assistance is still active, and if it isn't, ask in writing for a hearing.
  6. Contact legal aid. Ask about "wrongful eviction" and "conversion" (taking someone's property). Many states also let you sue for smaller amounts in small claims court without a lawyer.

Deadlines matter

Claims for lost property or an illegal eviction must be filed within your state's time limit, often a few years but sometimes less. A housing authority hearing request can be due within days of the notice. Don't wait.

What to write

"I was a tenant at [address] until [date]. I did not receive an eviction notice or court papers. Please tell me in writing where my belongings are, return them, and send me any notice you say you gave me."

If they say no

If the landlord won't answer or says everything is gone, keep their reply and take your list and proof to legal aid or small claims court. While you look for a new place, reentry programs and 211 can help with housing and replacing documents.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
