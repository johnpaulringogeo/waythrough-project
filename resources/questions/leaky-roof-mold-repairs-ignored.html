Reader Question

My roof has leaked for years and now there's mold. What can I do?

Asked August 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“My roof has leaked for more than three years. I've asked for repairs over and over, and now there's heavy mold and mildew and a strong musty smell, and it's making me sick. What can I do?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Yes, you can push for repairs. A leaking roof and spreading mold make a home unsafe, so put your request in writing, ask your local code office for an inspection, and if you have Section 8, ask your housing authority for one too: it can stop paying the landlord until the work is done. Don't stop paying rent on your own; check with legal aid first.

What the rule says

In almost every state, landlords must keep a rental safe and livable. Lawyers call this the "warranty of habitability." A roof that lets water in, and mold that grows because of it, are classic problems a landlord has to fix. Your city or county housing code usually says the same, and a code inspector can order repairs.

If you have a Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8), there is a second layer of protection. The owner must keep the unit up to HUD's quality standards (24 CFR 982.404):

  • HUD's newer inspection standards, called NSPIRE, fail a unit for water coming in from outside and for a damaged roof. Those usually get a 30-day fix.
  • NSPIRE treats mold seriously. More than 9 square feet of mold in one room of your unit is rated "life-threatening," which means the owner must fix it within 24 hours of notice. Smaller patches over 1 square foot are rated "severe."
  • Voucher programs can keep using the older checklist (HQS) until February 1, 2027; after that date, all of them must use NSPIRE. HQS also requires a roof that is "weathertight."

If the owner doesn't fix failed items in time, the housing authority must stop (abate) its payments to the owner, and the owner can't evict you because payments stopped. If repairs still aren't made within 60 days, the housing authority ends its contract with the owner and must give you your voucher to move, plus at least 90 days to find a new place.

What to do now

  1. Take dated photos and videos of the leak, stains and mold. Keep a list of every time you asked for repairs.
  2. Send a written repair request to your landlord (letter, email or online portal) and keep a copy. Name the roof leak and the mold, and ask for a repair date.
  3. If you have Section 8, send the same letter to your housing authority and ask for a special (complaint) inspection. Ask for the results in writing.
  4. Call your city or county code enforcement office (it may be called housing inspection or the building department) and ask for an inspection. This works with or without a voucher.
  5. See a doctor about your breathing and sinus problems, and mention the mold. Keep those records.
  6. Call legal aid and ask what your state allows, such as "repair and deduct," paying rent to the court, or a claim for money back.

What to write

"The roof over my unit has leaked since [month and year], and there is now mold on the [walls/ceiling] in [rooms]. I asked for repairs on [dates]. Please fix the roof and safely remove the mold by [date]. I am sending a copy to my housing authority and asking for an inspection."

Don't withhold rent on your own

Holding back rent can feel fair, but in many states it can get you evicted if you don't follow exact steps. Some states allow it only after written notice and a waiting period; some require you to pay the rent to the court instead. Check your state's page and talk to legal aid before you hold back any rent.

If they say no

If the landlord ignores you, a code inspector's report and a failed housing authority inspection create a record that is hard to ignore. If the housing authority doesn't act, ask for a supervisor in writing. If the unit keeps failing, you can ask to move with your voucher. If the landlord threatens you for complaining, most states have laws against retaliation, so tell legal aid right away.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
