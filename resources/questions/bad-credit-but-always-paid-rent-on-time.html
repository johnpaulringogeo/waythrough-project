Reader Question

Can I rent with bad credit if I've always paid my rent on time?

Asked September 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“What if I have bad credit, but I've paid my rent and utilities on time for years while on Section 8?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Often, yes. Your housing authority usually isn't the one checking your credit, but private landlords may. Years of on-time rent and utility payments are strong proof that you pay, so gather that record and ask the landlord to weigh it alongside your credit score.

Who looks at your credit

With a Section 8 voucher, the landlord, not the housing authority, is in charge of screening tenants. Federal rules say so directly (24 CFR 982.307(a)). The same rule lists what a landlord may consider, and the first item is your history of paying rent and utility bills. That is exactly where you are strong.

Many landlords run a credit check anyway. A low score doesn't have to end it. Plenty of landlords care more about rent history than old medical bills or credit cards. And with a voucher, the housing authority pays most of the rent straight to the landlord, which lowers their risk.

A few places go further. In California, for example, a landlord that checks credit for someone with a rent subsidy must let you show other proof that you can pay your share, like bank statements or benefit letters, and must reasonably consider it (Cal. Gov. Code 12955(o)). Rules vary, so check your state page or ask legal aid whether your state has something similar.

What to do now

  1. Get your rent record. Ask your landlord for a rent ledger, or print your payment history from the online portal. Ask your housing authority for a letter saying how long you've been in the program and that you're in good standing.
  2. Get your utility record. Utility companies can usually print a payment history or a letter of good standing.
  3. Check your credit reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com, from all three bureaus. Dispute anything that's wrong.
  4. Write a short note explaining what hurt your credit and what has changed. A few sentences is enough.
  5. Ask about rent reporting. Some landlords and rent payment apps report on-time rent to the credit bureaus, which can help your score over time. Watch for fees.
  6. Offer something extra if you can: a reference from your current landlord, a co-signer, or a somewhat larger deposit if your housing authority allows it.

What to write

"My credit score doesn't show how I pay rent. I've paid my share of the rent and my utilities on time for [number] years, and I've attached my rent ledger and a letter from my housing authority. Please consider this record with my application."

If they still say no

If a landlord turns you down, asks for a co-signer, or charges a bigger deposit because of a credit or tenant screening report, federal law says they must tell you. They have to give you the name and contact information of the company that made the report and tell you about your right to a free copy if you ask within 60 days, and your right to dispute errors. Ask for that notice, get the free report, and fix anything that's wrong. Then keep applying. Smaller landlords and tax credit apartments are often more flexible.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
