Reader Question

I'm leaving domestic violence and was dropped from the Section 8 waitlist. What can I do?

Asked October 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I'm in a domestic violence situation and trying to get out, and I need help right away. I've been on the Section 8 waitlist for a few years, but the housing authority dropped me because the list for my bedroom size closed. I have my child with me. Can you help us?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

If you are in danger now, call 911. For safety planning and shelter, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788. Then ask the housing authority in writing why you were removed, ask to be put back with your original date, and ask about preferences for survivors.

Safety first

The National Domestic Violence Hotline is free, confidential and open 24/7: call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788. An advocate can help you make a safety plan and find shelter, sometimes the same day. You can also call 211 for local shelter. If someone checks your phone or computer, use a safer device when you can.

What the rules say about the waitlist

  • One list for vouchers. For Section 8 vouchers, a housing authority must use a single waiting list (24 CFR 982.204(f)), and it can only remove names under rules written in its policy (24 CFR 982.204(c)). Being dropped because "your bedroom size closed" is unusual for a voucher list. It's more common for public housing or a specific building, which can keep lists by bedroom size. Ask which list you were on.
  • You can ask for a review. Taking you off a Section 8 waiting list counts as denying assistance (24 CFR 982.552(a)(2)), so you can ask for an informal review (24 CFR 982.554). Public housing applicants can ask for an informal hearing (24 CFR 960.208).
  • Check your bedroom size. Your bedroom size comes from the housing authority's written standards for your household. If you were placed on the wrong size list, ask them to fix it and keep your original date.
  • VAWA. A housing authority can't deny you because you are a survivor of domestic violence if you otherwise qualify (24 CFR 5.2005(b)). If the abuse made you miss mail or move, tell them.
  • Preferences. Many housing authorities give a waiting list preference to people fleeing domestic violence or who are homeless (24 CFR 982.207). Ask whether yours does.

What to do now

  1. Get safe first. Call the hotline, 211 or 911.
  2. Write the housing authority. Ask why you were removed, ask to be reinstated with your original application date, and ask for an informal review.
  3. Ask about a domestic violence or homeless preference and what proof it needs. For VAWA protections, you can choose to give HUD's self-certification form (HUD-5382) when a housing provider asks for documentation (24 CFR 5.2007).
  4. Give them a safe mailing address and phone number.
  5. Apply to every other open list nearby, including other housing authorities and public housing.
  6. Ask the shelter or hotline about rapid rehousing or other rent help while you wait.

What to write

"I have been on your waiting list since [year]. I was told I was removed because the list for my bedroom size closed. Please tell me in writing why I was removed and reinstate me with my original application date. I am a survivor of domestic violence. Please tell me if you have a preference I qualify for, and send mail only to this safe address: [address]."

If they say no

Ask for the decision in writing and request the informal review before the deadline in the letter. If the deadline already passed, ask anyway and explain that the abuse made it hard to respond. Domestic violence advocates and free legal aid often help with these requests. If you believe you were turned away because of the abuse, you can file a complaint with HUD.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
