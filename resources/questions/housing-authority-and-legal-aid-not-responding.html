Reader Question

I was locked out, and now the housing authority and legal aid won't call me back. How do I keep my voucher?

Asked November 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 6, 2026

The question

“After my landlord locked me out, I reported it to the housing authority and turned in my move paperwork, but I don't think it was ever processed. Now nobody there answers the phone or calls back, and I was told I'll lose my voucher if I don't find a place. Legal aid hasn't called me back, and a law clinic couldn't take my case. I've been living in my car for months and got very sick, and the landlord still has my belongings. What can I do?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Stop relying on phone calls. Send the housing authority a written request today for your voucher status, proof your move paperwork was processed, and an extension (as a reasonable accommodation if your health condition is a disability). If there's no answer in about a week, go in person and ask for a supervisor, then call HUD. In many states a landlord who illegally locks a tenant out must pay damages and attorney's fees, which can make a private lawyer willing to help.

1. Protect your voucher: put it in writing today

Phone calls leave no record. A written request does, and the housing authority (PHA) has to deal with it. Email it, send it through the PHA's portal, or hand-deliver a printed copy and ask them to stamp the date on your copy. Ask for three things:

  • Written confirmation of your voucher's status and expiration date.
  • Proof your move paperwork was received and processed. If you turned in a request to approve a new unit, the voucher clock must pause until the PHA answers you in writing (24 CFR 982.303(c)).
  • An extension. If your health condition is a disability and it makes your search take longer, ask for the extension as a reasonable accommodation. The PHA “must extend the voucher term up to the term reasonably required for that purpose” (24 CFR 982.303(b)(2)).

What to write

“My name is [name], client number [number]. On [date] I turned in my move paperwork after my landlord told me to leave. My landlord locked me out on [date], and I have been homeless since then. I have been seriously ill and have ongoing health problems. I am asking for: (1) written confirmation of my voucher's status and expiration date; (2) confirmation that my move paperwork was received and processed; and (3) an extension of my voucher. If my health condition is a disability, I am asking for the extension as a reasonable accommodation (24 CFR 982.303(b)(2)). Please reply in writing.”

2. If they don't answer within about a week

  • Go in person with a printed copy of your letter. Ask to see a supervisor in the voucher (Section 8) department, and ask them to stamp the date on your copy.
  • Call HUD's Public and Indian Housing customer service line at 800-955-2232. Explain that the PHA isn't responding and your voucher is about to expire. Ask how to reach your local HUD field office.
  • If you get a letter saying your assistance is ending, ask in writing for an informal hearing right away. HUD requires the PHA to offer one before it ends a participant's assistance for something the family did or failed to do (24 CFR 982.555). If you miss the deadline in the letter, ask anyway and explain why.

3. The lockout: you may be owed money

  • Many states let a tenant who was illegally locked out recover damages, and some make the landlord pay the tenant's attorney's fees too. For example, Nebraska's law says a tenant the landlord “unlawfully removes or excludes” can “recover an amount equal to three months' periodic rent as liquidated damages, and a reasonable attorney's fee” (Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-1430). When the law makes the landlord pay the lawyer, some private lawyers will take a case like this even when legal aid can't. Search for your state's lockout law, and mention it when you call.
  • Small claims court is another option. You can file yourself, without a lawyer, for money up to your state's limit. The court clerk can explain how to file.
  • If the landlord kept taking housing payments after you were locked out, report it to the HUD Office of Inspector General hotline at 1-800-347-3735.

4. Your belongings

Ask for your things back in writing. List what's there, ask for a date to pick them up, and keep a copy. Many states have rules a landlord must follow before selling, throwing out or keeping a former tenant's belongings, such as a written notice saying where and when you can claim them. Legal aid or the court clerk can tell you your state's rule.

5. Getting legal aid to answer

  • Call during intake hours. Many legal aid offices answer their intake line only at set times. Check the hours on their website and call right when the line opens.
  • Apply online if your legal aid office offers it. Many do, through their own site or your state's LawHelp website.
  • Say the urgent parts first: you were illegally locked out, you are homeless, and your voucher is about to expire.

6. A safer place to sleep, and getting well

  • Call or text 211 for shelter. Ask to be connected to your area's Coordinated Entry, which is how most communities refer people to rapid re-housing and other housing programs. Many shelters are Coordinated Entry access points.
  • Get medical care if you're still sick. 211 can also tell you where to find a clinic that charges based on income.

Keep copies of everything you send and get, and write down the date and name for every call.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
