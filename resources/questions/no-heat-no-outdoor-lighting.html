Reader Question

Our landlord won't turn on the heat or fix the outdoor lights. What can we do?

Asked November 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“Our landlord and the rental office manager won't give us heat inside our apartments, and they won't fix the outdoor lighting on the property either. What can we do?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

Report it today, in writing, to the landlord and to whoever oversees your housing, and call your local code office. Under HUD's newer NSPIRE inspection standards, heat that doesn't work between October 1 and March 31 is rated life-threatening, with a 24-hour fix. Broken outdoor lights are a safety problem with a 30-day fix.

What the rule says about heat

Almost every city and state requires landlords to provide working heat, and many local codes set a minimum indoor temperature. If your home is subsidized by HUD, federal standards apply too:

  • NSPIRE (HUD's newer standards). From October 1 to March 31, if the permanently installed heat isn't working, or a room is below 64°F, it is rated "life-threatening." The owner must fix it within 24 hours of notice. A room at 64 to 67.9°F also fails (a 24-hour fix in public housing and HUD apartment buildings, 30 days in voucher units). From April 1 to September 30, broken heat is a 30-day repair.
  • Vouchers on the older checklist. Housing Choice Voucher programs may keep using the older HQS checklist until February 1, 2027; after that, all must use NSPIRE. HQS requires a safe heating system "in proper operating condition." Under HQS, the housing authority decides whether no heat counts as a 24-hour emergency, so ask them directly.
  • Public housing. If your state or city has no heat rule, HUD's guideline is at least 68°F inside (PIH Notice 2018-19).

Space heaters and the kitchen oven don't count as heat. Don't use the oven to warm your home; it can cause a fire or carbon monoxide poisoning.

What the rule says about outdoor lights

NSPIRE rates a broken, missing or dead outdoor light fixture (walkways, parking lots, entries, outside stairs) as a "moderate" safety problem with a 30-day fix. For voucher units, HUD only inspects the path from your door to the street and the shared areas you use, so the lighting rule for the whole grounds mainly applies to public housing and HUD-funded apartment complexes. Local codes often cover outdoor lighting too.

What to do now

  1. Measure the temperature in each room with a cheap thermometer, 3 feet off the floor, and take dated photos. Write down when the heat stopped.
  2. Write to the landlord and the office manager today. Put "No heat" in the first line and ask for a repair time.
  3. Send the same letter to your housing authority if you have a voucher or live in public housing, and ask for an emergency inspection.
  4. If you live in a HUD-subsidized apartment building, call HUD's Multifamily Housing complaint line at 800-685-8470.
  5. Call your local code enforcement office for a heat inspection. Many cities have a no-heat hotline.
  6. Tell every office if someone in your home is elderly, a baby, or has a health condition. In freezing weather, call 211 to ask about warming centers.

Act as a group

If several apartments have no heat, ask neighbors to send their own letters on the same day, or one letter everyone signs. Group complaints are harder to ignore, and many states protect tenants who complain or organize from retaliation.

What to write

"There has been no heat in my apartment since [date]. The temperature inside is [__]°F. The outdoor lights at [location] are also out. Please restore the heat right away and repair the lights. I am sending a copy to [housing authority / HUD / code enforcement]."

If they say no

If the heat isn't back within 24 hours, follow up in writing with the housing authority or HUD and ask what they are doing about the owner. Don't stop paying rent without checking with legal aid; the rules vary by state.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
