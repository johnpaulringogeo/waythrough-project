Reader Question

My client has a project-based voucher and wants to move after a trauma in their unit. How can I help?

Asked November 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 6, 2026

The question

“I'm a case worker. My client has a project-based voucher, and their lease is coming up for renewal. Something traumatic happened in their unit, and they want to move to a different one. How do I help them?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

It depends on how long they've had project-based help and what happened. After one year, they can ask the housing authority for a voucher that moves with them, before giving notice (24 CFR 983.261). If the trauma involved domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault or stalking, VAWA lets them move for safety without the usual notice and ask for an emergency transfer (24 CFR 5.2005(e)). Otherwise, ask for a transfer within the property, or ask for one as a reasonable accommodation if a disability is involved.

First, two questions

  • How long has your client had project-based voucher (PBV) help? One year is the line that matters most.
  • What kind of trauma was it? Domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault and stalking bring federal VAWA protections. Other trauma uses the regular routes.

If your client has had PBV help for at least one year

HUD gives PBV families a right to move with continued help (24 CFR 983.261):

  1. Contact the housing authority (PHA) first. Before giving the owner notice, your client must ask the PHA for a tenant-based voucher, or the comparable help its Administrative Plan offers. If none is available right away, the PHA “must give the family priority to receive the next available opportunity” (983.261(c)).
  2. Then give the owner written notice to move out, with a copy to the PHA, following the notice terms in the lease (983.261(a)).

The lease renewal is a natural time to do this, but the order matters: PHA first, then notice. Your client keeps their PBV help in the current unit while they wait.

If the trauma involved domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault or stalking

  • No advance notice is needed for a safety move. If the move is needed to protect the family's health or safety, your client doesn't have to give the owner advance notice or contact the PHA first. The same applies if a household member was sexually assaulted on the premises in the 90 days before the request to move (983.261(e)).
  • The PHA can't end their help because of that move. If they had at least one year of PBV help, the PHA must offer continued tenant-based help (983.261(e)).
  • They can ask for an emergency transfer. The PHA must have a VAWA emergency transfer plan, and it must cover PBV families. A transfer can be internal (another unit at the same property, with no new application) or external (a different property, which usually means applying). Your client qualifies if they expressly ask for the transfer and either reasonably believe there is a threat of imminent harm from further violence if they stay, or were sexually assaulted on the premises in the last 90 days (24 CFR 5.2005(e)). The plan must also say how it helps families who have had PBV help for less than a year (983.261(f)).
  • Paperwork stays simple and private. A written request in which the tenant certifies they meet those conditions is enough to show they qualify. The provider may ask for documentation of what happened, and your client can choose HUD's self-certification form (HUD-5382) instead of a police report. All of it must be kept strictly confidential (24 CFR 5.2007).

If it was another kind of trauma, or they've had PBV help for less than a year

  • Ask the owner for an internal transfer to another unit at the same property. Many PBV properties have a written transfer policy. Ask for it.
  • Ask for the transfer as a reasonable accommodation if your client has a disability connected to the trauma, such as PTSD or depression. Include a short letter from a provider who knows them. The letter doesn't need to say what happened, only that the move is needed because of the disability. Owners and PHAs must consider these requests (24 CFR 100.204).
  • Be careful about moving out before one year. Ending the lease before one year of PBV help means the family “relinquishes the opportunity for continued tenant-based assistance” (983.261(d)), unless the VAWA protections above apply.

Practical steps for you

  1. Get your client's written consent (a release of information) before you talk with the owner or the PHA for them.
  2. Put every request in writing and keep copies, with the date each was sent.
  3. Ask the PHA for the PBV section of its Administrative Plan and its VAWA emergency transfer plan. Ask the owner for its own transfer policy. These spell out local steps and timelines.
  4. Check the date PBV help started, so you know which side of the one-year line your client is on.

What to write

“I have received project-based voucher assistance at [address] since [month and year]. I am asking to move to a different unit. [Choose what applies: Under 24 CFR 983.261, I request a tenant-based voucher so I can move with continued assistance. / I request an emergency transfer under your VAWA emergency transfer plan (24 CFR 5.2005(e)). / I request a transfer to another unit as a reasonable accommodation for my disability; a letter from my provider is attached.] Please reply in writing and tell me the next steps and timeline.”

If they say no

Ask for the reason in writing, and ask for an informal hearing or review. If a reasonable accommodation is denied, your client can file a complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity at 1-800-669-9777. For help with a VAWA request, contact a local domestic violence program or the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233. Free legal aid can also step in.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
