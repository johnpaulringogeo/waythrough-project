Reader Question

What can I do if my landlord won't sign a good standing letter?

Asked September 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“My landlord won't sign a letter saying I'm a tenant in good standing. What can I do?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

In most places a landlord doesn't have to write or sign a reference letter. But you can usually prove your rental history in other ways: a rent ledger, receipts or bank records, a letter from your housing authority, or a reference from someone else. Ask whoever wants the letter what other proof they'll accept.

What the rules say

No federal law makes a landlord sign a "good standing" or reference letter, and most states don't require one either. What a landlord can't do is refuse for an illegal reason, such as your race, disability or national origin, or because you have children. Many states also bar landlords from punishing you for asking for repairs. Those retaliation rules vary by state.

If you have a voucher, some help is built in. When you want to rent a new place, the housing authority must give the new landlord your current and prior addresses and the names and addresses of your current and prior landlords (24 CFR 982.307(b)). It may share other tenancy history too, under a written policy it must give you. So a new landlord can check your history even without a letter.

What to do now

  1. Ask who needs the letter and why. A new landlord, a housing program and a court may each accept different proof. Ask them: "What else can I give you to show I paid rent and followed my lease?"
  2. Ask for a rent ledger instead. Many landlords who won't write a letter will print a payment ledger. You can also print your history from the online portal.
  3. Gather your own proof: bank statements, canceled checks, money order stubs, or payment app records showing rent paid each month. Some states require landlords to give a receipt when you ask or when you pay in cash; check your state page.
  4. Ask your housing authority for a letter. If you have a voucher or live in public housing, ask for a letter confirming how long you've been a tenant and that you have no lease violations on file.
  5. Find another reference. A former landlord, a property manager, a caseworker or a longtime neighbor can write a short note.
  6. Write your own rental history listing each address, how long you lived there and your rent, and attach your proof.

What to write to your landlord

"I understand you'd rather not write a reference letter. Could you give me a copy of my rent ledger showing my payments since I moved in? I need it for a rental application."

If they still say no

Use the other proof above. One missing letter shouldn't stop your application. Tell the new landlord or program plainly that your current landlord doesn't write letters, and hand them your ledger, receipts and housing authority letter instead.

If you think the refusal is payback for complaining about repairs, or is because of a protected trait, write down what happened and when. Then talk to free legal aid or a fair housing agency about your options.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
