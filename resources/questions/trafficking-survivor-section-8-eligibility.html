Reader Question

I'm a trafficking survivor and was told I'm not eligible for Section 8. Why?

Asked August 2026 · Answered by Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

The question

“I'm a human trafficking survivor. I've been homeless, and now I'm in a safe house and going to school, but when I met with Section 8 they said I'm not eligible. How is that possible? Are there federal rules for who can get a Section 8 voucher?”

Sent through our Ask a Question form. We removed names, places and other details so no one can be identified.

The short answer

You don't need a job or any income for Section 8, and being homeless doesn't disqualify you. Ask the housing authority for the exact reason in writing and request an informal review. VAWA protections can apply if you also lived through sexual assault, stalking or domestic violence, and trafficking advocates can help you find housing.

What the federal rules say

To get a Section 8 voucher, the federal rules mainly look at three things:

  • Income: generally at or below 50% of the area median income (24 CFR 982.201(b)). Having no income at all is fine. You don't need a job.
  • Citizenship or eligible immigration status. If you aren't a U.S. citizen, the federal Office on Trafficking in Persons can give adult foreign trafficking survivors a certification letter that makes them eligible for federal benefits the same way refugees are. A victim advocate or immigration lawyer can help you get one.
  • A few background rules. Some are required, like lifetime sex offender registration or a conviction for making meth in federally assisted housing (24 CFR 982.553). Others are up to each housing authority.

Being homeless, living in a safe house, or going to school doesn't disqualify you. One student rule to know: if you're under 24, in college, unmarried, not a veteran, have no child and no disability, extra income rules can apply (24 CFR 5.612). Ask if that's the reason.

Often "not eligible" means something else, like the waiting list is closed, you don't fit a local preference, or a program only serves certain people. That's why you need the reason in writing.

How VAWA fits

The Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) protects survivors of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault and stalking in Section 8, public housing and other HUD programs. A housing authority can't deny you because you are a survivor if you otherwise qualify (24 CFR 5.2005(b)). VAWA's housing rules don't name trafficking, but many trafficking survivors also lived through sexual assault or stalking, and those are covered. Being followed and watched at night can count as stalking. If a record or an eviction came from what was done to you, tell the housing authority and ask it to take that into account.

Help made for trafficking survivors

  • National Human Trafficking Hotline: call 1-888-373-7888 (TTY 711) or text 233733. It's free, confidential and open 24/7, and it can connect you with local housing and legal help.
  • Your case manager or victim advocate can write a support letter, help you ask for a waitlist preference, and connect you to homeless programs.
  • Homeless programs: HUD's homeless rules include people fleeing violence or life-threatening danger (24 CFR 578.3). Call 211 to reach your area's homeless system.

What to do now

  1. Ask the housing authority in writing for the exact reason and how to request an informal review (24 CFR 982.554). Watch the deadline.
  2. Ask if it has a preference for survivors of violence, homeless people or people in transitional housing.
  3. Ask your advocate for a support letter.
  4. Apply to other open lists too, including public housing.

What to write

"I was told I am not eligible for a Housing Choice Voucher. Please tell me the specific reason in writing and how to request an informal review. I am a survivor of trafficking and stalking and live in transitional housing. Please also tell me about any preferences I may qualify for."

If they still say no, free legal aid can review the decision with you.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by housing authority. For your own situation, talk to your housing authority, a free legal aid office, or call 211.
