How Recertification Works and How to Prepare

Matt · Updated November 4, 2026

Once you're on Section 8, the work doesn't stop. Every year, your PHA will ask you to recertify — to prove your income hasn't changed in ways that would affect your rent payment. This guide explains what recertification is, why it happens, what documents you need, how it might change what you pay, and what happens if you miss the deadline.

What is Recertification and Why It Happens

Recertification is an annual review of your household's income and composition to recalculate what you should pay toward rent. Section 8 works on a sliding scale: you pay about 30% of your adjusted income (income after HUD's deductions) as rent, and the voucher covers the rest (up to the unit's rent and the PHA's payment standard). Your income changes. Jobs end, wages go up, benefits start or stop. Recertification ensures the PHA is still calculating your rent correctly.

It's also how the PHA confirms you still qualify. At recertification, they verify you still meet income limits, your household is still eligible, and you've been living in the unit as stated. It's a check-in point to make sure everything is legitimate.

You must recertify at least once per year, usually on the anniversary of when you first received your voucher. Some PHAs require you to recertify on a calendar year basis. Some recertify more frequently. Your lease and PHA paperwork will tell you your exact recertification date.

How Your Rent is Calculated

Understanding the calculation helps you prepare and predict what might happen.

The 30% rule. HUD calls your share the Total Tenant Payment. Under 24 CFR 5.628, it is the highest of: 30% of your monthly adjusted income (income after HUD's deductions), 10% of your monthly gross income (before deductions), the welfare rent in the few states that have one, or your PHA's minimum rent. For most families, the 30% of adjusted income figure is the one that counts.

Adjusted income. This is your household's total yearly income minus the deductions HUD allows: an amount for each dependent, one amount for an elderly or disabled family, unreimbursed medical and disability-assistance costs above a threshold (for elderly or disabled families), and childcare costs that let someone work or go to school. These deductions are set by federal rules, not picked by each PHA. Our guide on how your rent is calculated shows the current amounts and the changes coming in 2027.

Example: Your household's gross income is $2,000 a month ($24,000 a year), and you have two children. With the $480-a-year dependent deduction most PHAs use today, your deductions are $960 a year, so your adjusted income is $23,040 a year, or $1,920 a month. 30% of $1,920 is $576. 10% of your gross income is $200, which is lower, so your Total Tenant Payment is $576 a month. If you pay your own utilities, the PHA subtracts a utility allowance from that amount.

When Recertification Happens

Annual recertification. You'll be notified by mail or email. The PHA will give you a specific date to either come in for an appointment or submit documents. This usually happens on the anniversary of your initial lease signing, though some PHAs use calendar year recertification (everyone recertifies between December and February).

Interim recertification. If your circumstances change significantly mid-year, you can request an interim recertification. This is especially useful if you lose your job, get a significant pay increase, have a household member move out, or have a major expense come up (medical bills, childcare changes). Request it as soon as your situation changes.

Documents You'll Need to Provide

The PHA will give you a list of documents they want, but here's what's typically required:

Proof of income for all working household members:

  • Last 30 days of pay stubs (most recent)
  • If self-employed, profit and loss statement or tax returns
  • If recent job change, letter from employer with start date and salary

Proof of all benefits:

  • Current Social Security benefit letter (SSI or SSDI)
  • Current unemployment benefits statement
  • Current TANF or SNAP award letter
  • Child support documentation
  • Pension or retirement statements
  • Any other regular income

Documentation of deductions you're claiming:

  • Medical expenses: receipts or invoices
  • Childcare expenses: receipts or invoices
  • Dependent care: receipts or invoices
  • Disability documentation: Social Security award letter or medical verification

Proof of assets:

  • Last 3 months of bank statements (all accounts)
  • Investment or retirement account statements

Changes to your household:

  • If someone moved out: signed statement or lease showing they don't live there
  • If someone moved in: identification and proof they live with you
  • If you have a new baby: birth certificate

Preparing for Your Recertification Appointment

Mark your recertification date on the calendar. The PHA will mail you a notice with the appointment date. Don't lose it. If you move before your recertification, update your address with the PHA immediately so they send the notice to your new address.

Gather documents early. Start collecting documents 2-3 weeks before your appointment. Don't wait until the last minute. If you need a letter from your employer, request it now. If you need a benefit letter from Social Security, request it now.

Make copies. Make copies of everything. Bring originals or certified copies to the appointment. Keep copies for yourself.

Calculate your probable new rent. Gather your most recent pay stubs or benefit letters. Subtract your deductions, then calculate 30% of that adjusted income. This gives you an estimate of what you might pay after recertification. This is just an estimate — the PHA's calculation is official — but it helps you prepare mentally and budget.

Write down questions before you go. If anything is unclear, write it down. Bring a list to the appointment. You're entitled to understand how your rent is being calculated.

Bring identification. You might need to verify it's really you. Bring your ID.

What Happens at the Appointment

The PHA worker will:

  1. Review household composition. Confirm who lives with you. If anyone has moved in or out, update the file.
  2. Review your documents. Go through your pay stubs, benefit letters, and other documents. They may call your employer or benefits agency to verify.
  3. Calculate income. Add up all household income, subtract applicable deductions, and calculate 30% of the adjusted income.
  4. Determine your new tenant payment. This is what you'll pay in rent starting on your new recertification date.
  5. Explain the calculation. They should explain how they calculated it. Ask questions if you don't understand.
  6. Give you the new amount in writing. You should receive a written notice of your new payment amount.

How Your Rent Can Change

Your rent goes up if your income goes up. This is straightforward. More adjusted income means a higher 30% calculation, so you pay more.

Your rent goes down if your income goes down. Lose your job? Your 30% goes down. Your rent payment decreases.

Your rent might stay the same. If income doesn't change, your payment doesn't change (unless the unit's rent changed).

There may be a minimum rent. In the voucher and public housing programs, a PHA can set a minimum rent anywhere from $0 up to $50 a month (24 CFR 5.630). If you can't pay it because of a financial hardship, like losing your job or a death in the family, ask the PHA for a hardship exemption. The PHA must grant one when the family qualifies.

There's usually a maximum rent. You'll never pay more than the full contract rent of the unit. If your share (usually 30% of your adjusted income) is higher than the gross rent, you just pay the rent.

Interim Recertification: Requesting Changes Mid-Year

If your circumstances change significantly before your annual recertification, you can request an interim recertification. Use this if:

  • You lose your job or stop receiving benefits
  • Your income increases significantly
  • A household member moves out
  • You have a large unexpected medical or childcare expense
  • Your household composition changes

Contact your PHA and ask for an interim recertification. Explain what changed. They'll ask for updated documentation. Some PHAs make this easy; others make you wait. But it's worth asking, especially if you've lost income and can't afford your current rent.

What Happens If You Miss Your Recertification

Missing recertification is serious. Your voucher can be terminated. Here's the timeline:

You receive a notice. The PHA mails you a date and time. Respond immediately.

If you miss the appointment: Contact the PHA the same day or the next day. Explain why you missed it. Ask to reschedule. Respond to all PHA communications promptly. The more you engage, the more likely they'll give you another chance.

If the PHA can't reach you: They'll try calling, mailing, and emailing. If they can't get hold of you after several attempts, they may send a "notice of intent to terminate" your voucher. You'll have time to respond (usually 10 business days), but you have to act fast.

If you don't respond to the intent to terminate: Your voucher will be terminated. You'll no longer be on Section 8. The landlord will lose the voucher payment. You'll be responsible for the full rent.

Preventing Recertification Problems

Keep your contact information current. As soon as you move, change your phone number, or change your email, notify the PHA. If they can't reach you, you lose your voucher.

Open all mail from your PHA. Read every piece. Don't assume they're junk. Recertification notices are critical.

Calendar your recertification date. The moment you get the notice, write it on your calendar. Set a phone reminder. Ask a trusted friend to remind you.

Respond to the PHA before your appointment date. If you get a notice but can't make the appointment, call and reschedule. Don't ghost them.

Bring all documents to the appointment. If you're missing something, bring what you have and explain. Most PHAs will work with you.

Ask for a written copy of your new payment amount. Don't leave without it. Keep it for your records.

After Recertification: Your New Payment Amount

After recertification is approved, the PHA notifies your landlord of any rent change. Your new payment amount becomes effective on a specific date (usually 30 days after the recertification interview). If your rent goes up, you'll pay more starting that date. If it goes down, the landlord gets less. The change is automatic.

When Your Rent Was Calculated Wrong

Mistakes happen: income counted twice, a job you no longer have, a deduction left off, the wrong utility allowance, or someone who moved out still listed in your household. You don't have to just live with it. Here's how to get it fixed.

1. Ask for the calculation

Ask in writing for a copy of the worksheet behind your new rent: the income counted for each person, each deduction, the utility allowance, and your Total Tenant Payment. In the voucher and public housing programs, the PHA records these numbers on form HUD-50058. In a project-based building where the owner does your recertification, the owner uses form HUD-50059. Check every number against your own pay stubs and letters, and against the steps in our rent calculation guide.

2. Ask for a correction in writing

Send a short letter or email to your caseworker (or the property manager) and keep a copy:

"I believe my rent at my [month and year] recertification was calculated wrong. [Explain: for example, you counted $___ a month from a job that ended on ___; a letter from my former employer is attached.] Please correct my rent, send me a new rent notice that shows the effective date, and tell me in writing how you will repay what I overpaid, as a refund or a credit. Please also send me a copy of the calculation."

3. Getting overpaid rent back

  • Voucher holders and public housing residents: When the PHA's own mistake made you pay too much, HUD has told PHAs they must repay families for the overpayment (Notice PIH 2007-27). In the voucher program this is often done by lowering your share in the following months until you're paid back. In public housing it follows the PHA's written policy. Ask how much you'll get back, how, and by when, in writing.
  • Project-based Section 8 and other HUD-assisted buildings: Owners follow HUD Handbook 4350.3, paragraph 8-24. When an owner finds it over-counted your income, it must correct the past rent records and give you a written notice with your new rent and the amount you overpaid. It must also let you choose between a full, immediate refund or a credit toward future rent.

4. If they won't fix it

  • Voucher holders: You have the right to ask for an informal hearing on how the PHA figured your income and rent, and on the utility allowance it used (24 CFR 982.555(a)(1)(i)–(ii)). The PHA must tell you that you can ask for an explanation and a hearing. Its administrative plan sets the deadline to ask, and it is often short, so read your rent notice and ask in writing right away. If the deadline has already passed, ask anyway, explain why you're late, and ask the PHA to correct the error at your next interim or annual review.
  • Public housing residents: Use your PHA's grievance procedure. A dispute about a PHA action under your lease is a grievance (24 CFR 966.53). Ask the office for the grievance policy and its deadline to file.
  • Project-based buildings: Ask the owner for a meeting with someone who didn't do the original calculation. If that doesn't work, call HUD's Multifamily Housing Clearinghouse at 1-800-685-8470 (weekdays, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Eastern). It takes complaints about HUD-assisted properties, including mismanagement.
  • Keep paying while you dispute it. Pay the amount on your rent notice and ask for the difference back. Falling behind can put your housing at risk. Talk to free legal aid and check your state's rules before you hold back any rent.

Recertification Checklist

Use this checklist before your appointment:

  • Recent pay stubs (last 30 days)
  • Benefit letters (SSI, SSDI, TANF, unemployment)
  • Bank statements (last 3 months)
  • Proof of household changes (if applicable)
  • Documentation of deductions (medical, childcare, disability)
  • Identification
  • Written list of any questions
