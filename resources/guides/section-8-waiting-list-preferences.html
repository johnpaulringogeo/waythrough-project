Section 8 Waiting List Preferences: How to Move Up the List

Matt · Updated August 2026

Step 5 of the Section 8 application process. This page covers one step in depth — for the whole process end to end, see How to Apply for Section 8.

Two households can apply to the same housing authority on the same day and wait wildly different amounts of time. The difference is usually not luck — it is preferences: categories of applicant that a housing authority moves ahead of everyone else on its waiting list. Preferences are the single largest lever an applicant controls, they are claimed on the application itself, and most people either don't know they qualify or don't document the one they claim.

Here's the short version: There are no national Section 8 preferences any more. Each Public Housing Authority chooses its own and publishes them in its Administrative Plan. The most common are homelessness, veteran status, disability, being elderly, living or working in the PHA's jurisdiction, fleeing domestic violence, and involuntary displacement. You claim them when you apply, you have to document them when asked, and a single qualifying preference can move you past thousands of applicants.

What a Preference Actually Does

A waiting list is not simply a queue in date order. It is sorted first by preference, and only then by date. Every applicant holding a preference you don't hold sits ahead of you, no matter how long ago you applied.

The practical effect is large. At an oversubscribed urban housing authority, applicants with no preference may effectively never reach the top of the list, while applicants holding two or three preferences are contacted within months. This is why a household that qualifies for a preference and fails to claim it has, in real terms, given up years.

It is also why preferences are worth checking before deciding which housing authorities to apply to. A PHA whose preference set matches your situation may be a far better bet than a closer one whose preferences you don't meet.

There Are No Federal Preferences Any More

Until 1998, federal law imposed national preferences on every housing authority. Congress repealed them, and since then each PHA sets its own. That single fact explains most of the confusion in this area: advice about "the Section 8 preferences" is describing one particular housing authority's list, not a national rule.

What a PHA chooses has to be written into its Administrative Plan, described in its annual PHA Plan, and put out for public comment before it takes effect. Those documents are public. They are also the only authoritative answer to "does this housing authority have a preference I qualify for."

There are limits on what a PHA can adopt. Preferences cannot be designed or applied in a way that has the purpose or effect of excluding people on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability. And a PHA that adopts a preference for working families must treat households headed by someone 62 or older, or by a person with disabilities, as though they qualify for it too.

The Preferences You're Most Likely to Find

The list below covers what most housing authorities actually use, what each one generally requires, and what to have ready when you're asked to prove it.

Homelessness

The most widely adopted preference, and often the strongest. Definitions vary: some PHAs use HUD's "literally homeless" definition (a shelter, a place not meant for habitation, or fleeing domestic violence), while others include people who are doubled up, in transitional housing, or facing imminent eviction.

Documentation: a letter from a shelter, transitional housing provider, street outreach worker, school district homeless liaison, or case manager on agency letterhead. A self-certification is accepted by some PHAs where third-party verification isn't possible — ask rather than assuming you can't prove it. If you're in this situation right now, our guide on emergency housing tonight covers immediate options.

Veteran Status

Service by the applicant or, at many PHAs, a household member. Some limit it to veterans with an honorable discharge; some extend it to surviving spouses.

Documentation: your DD-214. If you don't have a copy, you can request one from the National Archives; start that process early, because it takes time. Note also that HUD-VASH is a separate route entirely, not a preference — it's a dedicated voucher program for veterans experiencing homelessness, accessed by referral through a VA case manager rather than through the ordinary waiting list. See how to apply for HUD-VASH and our guide to VA benefits.

Disability

A household member with a qualifying disability. Some PHAs apply this only when the head of household or spouse has the disability; others count any member.

Documentation: an SSI or SSDI award letter is usually the cleanest proof. Otherwise, a verification form completed by a physician or other qualified professional — PHAs typically supply their own form. The PHA may confirm that a disability exists and that an accommodation is needed; it is not entitled to your full medical records or a specific diagnosis.

Elderly

Usually defined as head of household or spouse aged 62 or older. Some housing authorities operate separate elderly waiting lists rather than a preference on the general list.

Documentation: birth certificate, driver's license, state ID, or passport.

Local Residency (Living or Working in the Jurisdiction)

Extremely common, and the one that most often catches out-of-area applicants by surprise. Note the second half: most residency preferences count working in the jurisdiction as well as living in it, and many also count having been hired to work there. If you commute into a county, you may hold its residency preference without living there.

Documentation: a lease, utility bill, or state ID for residency; a letter from your employer or recent pay stubs showing the work address for employment.

Domestic Violence Survivors

Applicants fleeing or who have fled domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, stalking, or human trafficking. Distinct from the emergency transfer protections that apply to people already in assisted housing, though the same federal framework underlies both.

Documentation: federal law allows self-certification here — you cannot be required to produce a police report or a protective order as the only acceptable proof. A statement from a victim service provider, attorney, or medical professional also works. If you are in danger now, ask the PHA about emergency transfer and confidentiality protections at the same time.

Involuntary Displacement

Displacement by a natural disaster, government action, condemnation of your building, or domestic violence. Some PHAs open dedicated preferences after a specific local disaster.

Documentation: a condemnation or code enforcement notice, FEMA registration, insurance correspondence, or a letter from the agency responsible.

Less Common but Worth Asking About

  • Working families — head of household or spouse employed, often with a minimum hours threshold. Elderly and disabled households must be treated as qualifying.
  • Rent burden — paying more than 50% of income toward rent.
  • Substandard housing — your current unit fails basic habitability standards.
  • Youth aging out of foster care — often paired with a Foster Youth to Independence voucher program.
  • Agency referrals — some PHAs reserve slots for referrals from named partner agencies, which can be the fastest route of all if you're already working with a case manager.
  • Terminal or catastrophic illness, and families participating in self-sufficiency programs — see our guide to the Family Self-Sufficiency program.

How PHAs Rank Applicants Who Hold Preferences

Two systems are common, and knowing which one a housing authority uses changes how much a second preference is worth to you:

  • Tiered date-and-time. Applicants are grouped by preference tier, and within each tier sorted by application date. A single preference moves you into a higher tier; additional preferences may do nothing.
  • Points. Each preference is worth a set number of points, and applicants are ranked by total. Here every additional preference you can document genuinely helps.

A third pattern appears at lottery-based PHAs: a random draw determines who gets onto the list, and preferences then determine the order within it. The Administrative Plan will say which model applies.

How to Find Out Which Preferences Your PHA Offers

  1. Read the waiting list opening notice. Preferences are almost always listed there, and this is the version that governs the current opening.
  2. Look up the Administrative Plan on the PHA's website — usually under "About," "Policies," "PHA Plan," or "Documents." Search it for the word "preference."
  3. Ask directly. Call and ask: "What preferences do you offer, how are they weighted, and what documentation do you require for each?" Those three questions in one call save an enormous amount of guesswork. Our guide on how to talk to your PHA covers getting a useful answer.
  4. Compare across authorities. Because preference sets differ, the same household can be a low-priority applicant at one PHA and a high-priority applicant twenty miles away. Our find your PHA guide and the housing authority directory help you build that list.

Claiming a Preference the Right Way

Claim every preference you qualify for, at the moment you apply. Preferences are usually claimed on the pre-application, and at many PHAs adding one later means taking a new date — which can cost you everything you've waited. If you're unsure whether you qualify, claim it and let the PHA make the determination.

But never claim one you cannot document. Preferences are verified before you're pulled from the list, and an unverifiable claim doesn't simply revert you to no-preference status — it can be treated as misrepresentation and get the whole application denied. The right move when you're not sure is to claim it and gather the proof, not to claim it and hope nobody asks.

Gather documentation at the time you apply, even though you won't be asked for it for years. Shelter letters, employer letters, and case managers are much easier to reach today than they will be three years from now. Our document checklist covers what to assemble and how to keep it.

If Your Situation Changes After You Apply

Preferences are not frozen at your application date. If you become homeless, turn 62, acquire a disability, move into the jurisdiction, take a job there, or leave an abusive household, notify every PHA where you have an open application, in writing, and ask to have the preference added.

Policies on whether a newly added preference keeps your original application date vary by housing authority — ask specifically, and keep a copy of the notification you sent along with the date. It works in the other direction too: if you lose a preference, most PHAs require you to report that as well.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Section 8 preferences?

There is no national list. Since federal preferences were repealed in 1998, each housing authority sets its own and publishes them in its Administrative Plan. The most common are homelessness, veteran status, disability, elderly status, living or working in the jurisdiction, fleeing domestic violence, and involuntary displacement.

How much does a preference actually help?

A great deal. Waiting lists are sorted by preference first and application date second, so every applicant holding a preference you don't sits ahead of you regardless of when you applied. At heavily oversubscribed housing authorities, a preference is often the difference between being contacted and never reaching the top.

Can I add a preference after I've already applied?

Usually yes, if your circumstances have genuinely changed. Notify the housing authority in writing and ask whether adding it preserves your original application date — policies differ, and that answer matters.

Do I need a police report to claim a domestic violence preference?

No. Federal law permits self-certification for domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, and stalking. A statement from a victim service provider, attorney, or medical professional is also acceptable. You cannot be required to produce a police report or protective order as the only proof.

Does a residency preference mean I have to already live there?

Not necessarily. Most residency preferences count working in the jurisdiction as well as living in it, and many count having been hired to work there. If you commute into an area, ask whether that qualifies you.

Can I claim more than one preference?

Yes, and you should claim every one you can document. Whether the extra ones improve your position depends on the system: housing authorities using a points model reward each additional preference, while tiered date-and-time systems may not.
