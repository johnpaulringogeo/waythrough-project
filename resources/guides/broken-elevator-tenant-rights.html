Broken Elevator: Your Rights as a Tenant, and How to Get It Fixed

Matt · Updated November 2026

For many people, a broken elevator is a hassle. For seniors and people with disabilities who live on upper floors, it can mean being stuck at home, missing doctor visits, and not being able to get out in a fire. This guide explains your rights when the elevator is out, how to push for repairs, and what extra rules apply if you have a disability or rent with a voucher or in public housing.

Here's the short version: Report every outage to your landlord in writing. If you have a disability, ask for a reasonable accommodation, like a faster repair or a move to a lower floor. If you rent with a Section 8 voucher, ask your housing authority for a special inspection. Report repeated breakdowns to your city's 311 line or building department. If you are ever trapped or in danger, call 911.

Safety first

  • If you are trapped in an elevator, press the alarm or call button, or use the emergency phone. Stay inside and wait for help. Call 911 if you are hurt, feel unwell, or no one answers.
  • If you can't leave your home and run out of medicine, food, or oxygen, or have a medical problem, call 911.
  • Ask for an evacuation plan. Ask building management in writing how residents who can't use stairs will get out during a fire or power outage. Tell them you may need help, and keep a copy.

Step 1: Report it in writing, every time

A phone call or a word in the hallway leaves no record. A written repair request does. Send it by email, text, the landlord's online portal, or a letter, and keep a copy. Our repair request letter is a good place to start. Include:

  • The date the elevator stopped working, and how often it has broken down before.
  • How it affects you. For example: "I use a walker and live on the 8th floor. I can't leave my apartment when the elevator is down."
  • A request for a repair date, and what the landlord will do in the meantime.

Keep a log of each outage: the date, how long it lasted, and who you told. Take photos of any "out of order" signs.

Step 2: If you have a disability, ask for a reasonable accommodation

The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to refuse "reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services" when they may be needed to give a person with a disability an equal chance to use and enjoy their home, "including public and common use areas" (24 CFR 100.204). An elevator is a common area, so this can apply. Things you can ask for include:

  • Putting your repair first, or calling in a repair company faster.
  • A transfer to a lower-floor or ground-floor unit when one is available.
  • Practical help while the elevator is down, such as having mail or packages brought to your door, or staff help carrying groceries. A landlord doesn't always have to start a new service, but many will agree, and asking in writing creates a record.
  • Notice before planned shutdowns, so you can plan appointments.

If your disability isn't obvious, the landlord may ask for a short note from a doctor or other provider saying you have a disability and why you need the change. It doesn't need to name your condition. Use our reasonable accommodation request letter, and see our guide on how to request a reasonable accommodation for the full steps.

Reasonable modifications

A modification is a physical change, like a ramp at the entrance or a railing. Under the Fair Housing Act, a landlord must allow reasonable modifications you need because of a disability, but in private housing you usually pay for them (24 CFR 100.203). In public housing and other federally assisted housing, Section 504 usually requires the housing provider to make and pay for needed structural changes, unless that would be an undue financial and administrative burden.

Step 3: If you rent with a Section 8 voucher

Your landlord signed a contract with the housing authority (PHA) promising to keep your home up to HUD's standards. Those standards cover the path from your door to the street and the building systems that serve your unit, and elevators are one of those systems (24 CFR 5.703).

  • Ask the PHA for a special inspection. Call and write to the PHA's inspections department. Say the elevator is out, how long, and that you can't use the stairs.
  • What the standards say. HUD's NSPIRE inspection standard for elevators lists "Elevator is inoperable" as a deficiency. For voucher units it is a "Fail," with a 30-day correction time. It also says that if a building has more than one elevator, "all elevators must be in working condition." Housing authorities must switch to NSPIRE by February 1, 2027. Until then, some still use the older Housing Quality Standards, which say "Elevators must be working and safe."
  • Inspection timelines. HUD's current rule says that when you report a problem, the PHA must inspect within 24 hours if it is life-threatening, or within 15 days if it isn't (24 CFR 982.405(d)). Ask the PHA what timeline it follows. If you are trapped upstairs and can't get medicine or food, say so clearly.
  • If the owner doesn't fix it. The PHA can hold back its payments to the owner, and if repairs still aren't made, it can stop paying and end the contract. The owner can't evict you because the PHA withheld or stopped payments (24 CFR 982.404). If it comes to that, the PHA must give you a voucher to move.

Step 4: If you live in public housing

  • Put in a work order with the management office, and write down the work order number and date.
  • The housing authority must keep elevators working. HUD requires public housing leases to say the PHA will keep facilities and appliances "including elevators" in good and safe working order (24 CFR 966.4(e)(5)).
  • Hazardous conditions. If damage makes your home hazardous to your life, health, or safety, the PHA must make repairs within a reasonable time. If it can't, it must offer you another standard unit if one is available, and your rent may be reduced (24 CFR 966.4(h)).
  • Section 504. Housing authorities must run their housing programs so that, as a whole, they are "readily accessible to and usable by" people with disabilities (24 CFR 8.24). They must also change their policies and practices so they don't discriminate based on disability (24 CFR 8.33). In practice, this can mean a transfer to an accessible or lower-floor unit. Ask for the PHA's Section 504 or reasonable accommodation coordinator.
  • If they say no, ask for the answer in writing and use the PHA's grievance procedure.

If you live in a HUD-assisted building run by a private owner (for example, many senior buildings), and the management office doesn't fix the problem, call HUD's Multifamily Housing Complaint Line at 1-800-685-8470.

Step 5: Report it to your city or state

  • Call 311 or your building or code enforcement department. An elevator that keeps breaking down may break local building or housing codes. Ask for an inspection and a complaint number.
  • Find the elevator inspection office. Most states, and some large cities, have an office that inspects and licenses elevators. It is often part of the state's labor, licensing, or building safety department. Look for the inspection certificate, which is often posted in the elevator or the building office. It usually shows who inspected the elevator and when.

Protection from retaliation

Many states and cities ban landlords from punishing tenants who ask for repairs or report code problems. Retaliation can look like an eviction notice, a rent increase, or cutting services soon after you complain. The Fair Housing Act also makes it illegal to threaten, intimidate, or interfere with anyone for using their fair housing rights, such as asking for a reasonable accommodation (24 CFR 100.400). If this happens, keep your records and contact legal aid. Our guide to free legal help for tenants shows how to find it.

Frequently asked questions

Does my landlord have to fix a broken elevator?

In most places, yes. Building and elevator safety codes require elevators to be kept safe and working, and most states require rental housing to be kept in safe, livable condition. In HUD-assisted housing, federal inspection standards also count an elevator that doesn't work as a deficiency that must be fixed.

What can I ask for if I have a disability and the elevator is out?

You can ask for a reasonable accommodation under the Fair Housing Act. Examples include putting your repair first, a transfer to a lower-floor unit when one is available, or practical help such as having mail or packages brought to your door while the elevator is down. Ask in writing and keep a copy.

I have a Section 8 voucher. Who can inspect the elevator?

Report it to your housing authority and ask for a special inspection. Under HUD's NSPIRE standard, an elevator that doesn't work fails a voucher inspection and has a 30-day correction time. Under the older HQS rule, elevators must be working and safe. If the owner doesn't fix it, the housing authority can hold back its payments.

I live in public housing. What does the housing authority have to do?

Put in a work order and keep the number. HUD requires public housing leases to say the housing authority must keep elevators in good and safe working order. Under Section 504, it also has to make its housing program usable by people with disabilities, which can mean a transfer to an accessible unit.

Who do I call to report an unsafe elevator?

Call your city's 311 line or building or code enforcement department. Most states and some cities also have an elevator inspection office. The inspection certificate, often posted in the elevator or the building office, can show who inspects it. If someone is trapped or hurt, call 911.

Can my landlord punish me for complaining about the elevator?

Many states and cities ban landlords from retaliating against tenants who ask for repairs or report code problems. The Fair Housing Act also bans retaliation for asking for a reasonable accommodation. If you get an eviction notice or rent increase after complaining, keep your records and contact legal aid.
