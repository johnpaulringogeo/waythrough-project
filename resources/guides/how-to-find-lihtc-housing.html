How to Find Tax Credit (LIHTC) Apartments

Matt · 7 min read

What Is LIHTC Housing?

LIHTC stands for Low-Income Housing Tax Credit. The name is more complicated than the idea. LIHTC apartments are buildings that were built or renovated with help from the government in the form of tax credits. In exchange, the property owners agree to keep rents affordable for a certain number of years (usually 30 years or more).

LIHTC housing is NOT the same as Section 8 vouchers or public housing. With a voucher, the government subsidizes the difference between what you pay and what the landlord gets. With LIHTC housing, the building itself has reduced rents built in for decades, so everyone who lives there pays lower rent, regardless of whether they get a voucher.

That matters a lot. LIHTC apartments often have shorter waiting lists than Section 8, and you don't need a voucher to live there. You apply directly to the property management company, the same way you would for any apartment. If you're also on waiting lists for Section 8 or other programs, see our guide on strategies for handling housing waiting lists.

LIHTC vs. Section 8: How They Differ

The two get mixed up all the time, and the difference changes where you should apply.

Section 8 (Housing Choice Voucher): You get a voucher that subsidizes your rent. You find a private apartment, the landlord agrees to accept the voucher, and the PHA pays the difference between the rent (up to its payment standard) and your portion (usually about 30% of your adjusted income). Your rent can change if your income changes.

LIHTC: The apartment itself has a reduced rent built in. The property was developed with tax credits that require the owner to keep rents low for a set number of years, not forever. You pay the set rent for that unit. Your rent might increase yearly, but it's predetermined and capped, not tied to your income.

Both are affordable housing programs, and they work very differently. Some people prefer LIHTC because there's no PHA involvement and no recertification process. Others prefer vouchers because the subsidy moves with them if they relocate.

How LIHTC Income Limits Work

LIHTC buildings use income limits to decide who can live there. The limits are based on the Area Median Income (AMI) for your region. AMI is the middle income for your area: half of people earn more, half earn less.

Different LIHTC properties have different income limits. Common thresholds are:

  • 30% AMI: For extremely low-income households (the most affordable)
  • 50% AMI: For very low-income households
  • 60% AMI: For low-income households (most common)
  • 80% AMI or higher: Mixed-income properties (some affordable units, some market-rate)

For example, in a mid-sized city, 60% AMI might be about $35,000 annually for an individual. A property with a 60% AMI limit will rent to you if your income is below that threshold.

Verify the Income Limit Before You Apply

Ask each property what its AMI limit is. Limits vary from one property to the next, so check you're eligible before you spend time on the application.

How to Search for LIHTC Housing

There are several good places to look. You don't need all of them; pick the ones that cover your area well.

Affordable Housing Online (affordablehousingonline.com)

This is one of the largest databases of LIHTC properties in the country. You can search by state, city, or zip code and filter by income limit, unit size, and amenities.

Steps to search:

  1. Go to affordablehousingonline.com
  2. Select your state
  3. Search by city or zip code
  4. Review results: each listing shows the property name, address, contact info, income limit, and opening status
  5. Call the property to ask about availability and the application process

Use the "opening dates" filter to see properties that are accepting applications now, instead of ones with closed lists or future openings.

State Housing Finance Agency (HFA) Websites

Every state has an agency that oversees housing finance and development. These agencies keep lists of LIHTC properties in their state, often as a searchable database. Search "[your state] housing finance agency" to find yours.

State HFA websites sometimes have more detail than the national databases, so check your state's site directly.

Call 211

Dial 211 from any phone (or visit 211.org) to reach a local information and referral service. Tell them you're looking for affordable housing, and they can tell you about LIHTC properties in your area and other housing programs. It's free and confidential.

Local Housing Authority or Community Action Agency

Your local Housing Authority or Community Action Agency often maintains lists of affordable housing properties. Call your city or county government office and ask for housing assistance resources.

LIHTC Waiting Lists and the Application Process

When you find a LIHTC property you're interested in, the application process works like this:

  1. Call or visit the property management office to ask about availability and application requirements
  2. Get an application (usually available in person or by mail)
  3. Prepare your documents (proof of income, ID, references, maybe credit history)
  4. Submit your application according to their process (in person, by mail, or increasingly, online)
  5. Wait for a decision (usually 2-4 weeks, though timelines vary)

Most LIHTC properties maintain waiting lists when they don't have immediate openings. The order you apply matters, because your place on the list is based on your application date. Unlike Section 8, which might close and reopen a list, LIHTC properties typically keep lists open continuously.

When a unit opens, management contacts applicants on the list in order. There's less red tape than with a PHA, which can speed things up.

What Documents You'll Need

LIHTC application requirements vary by property, but generally you'll need:

  • Photo ID (driver's license, passport, state ID)
  • Proof of income (last 2 pay stubs, tax returns, Social Security benefits statement, unemployment letter)
  • Social Security numbers for all household members
  • Proof of citizenship or eligible immigration status
  • Bank statements or proof of assets (sometimes required)
  • References from previous landlords or employers
  • Authorization for credit and background checks

Some properties are more flexible than others. If you're worried about something specific (like a gap in employment or a past eviction), call ahead and ask about their policy. Some properties are more willing than others to work with applicants who have a rough history.

Mixed-Income LIHTC Properties

Some LIHTC buildings are "mixed-income": some affordable units (for people under the AMI limit) and some market-rate units (for anyone, regardless of income). That's usually a plus. These buildings tend to be better maintained and have more amenities because they're renting to both groups.

In a mixed-income building, you're applying for the affordable units specifically, so make sure those are the ones you're looking at. The property should be very clear about which units are income-restricted and which aren't.

What to Watch Out For

Compliance Periods Matter

LIHTC rent limits don't last forever. Federal law requires a 15-year "compliance period" plus an "extended use period" of at least 15 more years, so 30 years in total, and many states require longer. When that commitment ends, the owner can raise rents to market rate. An owner can sometimes get out after year 15 if the state agency can't find a buyer willing to keep the rents affordable, though some states don't allow that. If affordability ends early that way, or through foreclosure, current tenants get 3 more years of protection from eviction without good cause and from rent increases above the LIHTC limits. If you move into a property with a 30-year commitment that has already been around for 20 years, your affordable rent might change in about 10 years. Ask the manager when the property's affordability period ends.

A denial at one property is not a denial everywhere. LIHTC properties sometimes deny applications for credit issues, rental history, or income verification problems. Each property makes its own decisions, so apply to others.

Confirm the income limit by phone. Property websites are sometimes out of date. Call the property manager or leasing office and ask for the current income limits before you spend time on an application.

Ask about lease terms. LIHTC leases are standard, but ask about the lease length, when rent can be raised, and what happens if your income goes over the limit (usually you can stay, but your rent adjusts).

Use LIHTC Alongside Section 8

Don't pick one. Apply to LIHTC properties in your area while you're on the PHA waiting list. LIHTC often moves faster, so you might get housing while you're still waiting for a voucher. And if you do get a voucher later, you can move and use it elsewhere.

Apply to several LIHTC properties, too, not just one building. Each has its own requirements, wait times, and opening dates, so every extra application improves your odds.

Income Limits and Rent: A Real Example

Say you earn $28,000 per year and you're looking at two LIHTC properties:

  • Property A (60% AMI limit, 2-bedroom): AMI for your area is $50,000, so 60% AMI is $30,000. You qualify. Rent is $650/month.
  • Property B (30% AMI limit, 1-bedroom): 30% AMI is $15,000. You don't qualify (your income is too high). Don't waste time applying here.

Check each property's income limit before applying. It's usually in the property listing. If it isn't, call and ask.

Next Steps

Start your LIHTC search today at affordablehousingonline.com or by calling 211. Make a list of properties in your area, write down each one's income limit and phone number, and start calling.

Keep your Section 8 applications and other PHA waiting lists going at the same time. LIHTC works best as one more place you've applied, not the only one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between LIHTC and Section 8?

LIHTC (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit) properties are privately owned apartments with income-restricted rents, while Section 8 is a voucher you use with any willing landlord. LIHTC has no voucher. You just pay the below-market rent directly. LIHTC typically uses a 60% AMI income limit (higher than Section 8's 50%), and there's often no years-long waiting list.

How do I find LIHTC apartments near me?

Search HUD's LIHTC database at HUD's LIHTC database, check your state housing finance agency's website, or search "[your city] affordable apartments" or "[your city] income-restricted housing." Many LIHTC properties list on standard rental sites like Apartments.com with an "income restricted" label.

Do LIHTC apartments do credit checks?

Most LIHTC properties do run credit and background checks, but their criteria are often more lenient than market-rate apartments. Each property sets its own screening policy. If denied, ask what specifically triggered the denial. You may be able to address it or find a more lenient property.
