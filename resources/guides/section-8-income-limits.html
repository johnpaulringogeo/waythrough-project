Section 8 Income Limits (2026): Do You Qualify?

Matt · Updated May 2026

"Do I make too much money to qualify?" is one of the most common Section 8 questions. It depends on where you live and how many people are in your household. HUD sets income limits for every county in the country and updates them every year. Below is how the limits work, what counts as income, and how to look up the exact numbers for your area.

To qualify for Section 8, your household income usually must be at or below 50% of your area's median income (AMI). Some families up to 80% of AMI can qualify in limited cases, such as families who already get federal housing help (24 CFR 982.201(b)(1)). Most vouchers go to people earning below 30% AMI (called "extremely low income"). Limits vary a lot by location. A family of four might qualify at $40,000 in a rural area or $60,000+ in a high-cost city.

How Section 8 Income Limits Work

HUD calculates income limits based on the Area Median Income (AMI) for every metropolitan area and county in the United States. The AMI is the middle point: half of families in your area earn more, and half earn less.

Section 8 uses three income categories:

  • Extremely Low Income (ELI): Earning 30% or less of AMI. This is the highest priority category, and by law, at least 75% of new vouchers must go to families at this level
  • Very Low Income (VLI): Earning 50% or less of AMI. This is the standard eligibility cutoff for Section 8
  • Low Income: Earning 80% or less of AMI. Eligible for some programs but generally NOT for Section 8 vouchers (some public housing and LIHTC properties use this threshold)

For Section 8, the number that matters is 50% of AMI. If your household income is at or below it, you're eligible to apply. A few groups of low-income families (up to 80% of AMI) can also qualify, such as families moving from another federal housing program without a break.

How to Find Your Area's Exact Income Limits

HUD publishes new income limits every year, usually in the spring. There are three ways to find yours.

Option 1: HUD's Income Limits Tool
Visit huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html. Pick your state, then your county or metro area. It shows the exact dollar amounts for each household size and income category.

Option 2: Call your PHA
Your local Public Housing Authority knows the current limits for your area. Call and ask: "What are the income limits for a household of [your size]?" They'll give you the exact number. Find your PHA using our PHA finder guide.

What Counts as Income (and What Doesn't)

HUD has specific rules about what counts toward household income. Some sources don't count at all. Deductions come off after that, and they lower the income your rent is based on, not the income checked against the limit.

Income That Counts

  • Wages, salaries, overtime, tips, and bonuses
  • Self-employment income (net)
  • Social Security benefits (retirement and survivor)
  • SSI and SSDI
  • Pensions and annuities
  • Unemployment compensation
  • Child support and alimony received
  • Regular cash contributions or gifts (from family, etc.)
  • Military pay and allowances
  • Interest, dividends, and net rental income
  • Workers' compensation

Income That Does NOT Count

  • SNAP benefits (food stamps)
  • WIC benefits
  • One-time payments (insurance settlements, inheritance, back pay)
  • Tax refunds and earned income credits
  • Earned income (wages) of children under 18
  • Foster care payments
  • Student financial aid (for tuition, fees, and education costs)
  • Temporary, nonrecurring, or sporadic income (including gifts)
  • Amounts received specifically for medical expenses
  • Live-in aide income

Deductions Lower Your Rent, Not Your Eligibility

Deductions won't get you under the income limit. The PHA checks eligibility against your annual income: everything that counts, before any deductions. Deductions are subtracted after that to get your adjusted income, and your rent share is based on that lower number. HUD allows these deductions from annual income:

  • $500 per dependent (2026 amount): For each household member who is under 18, a full-time student, or disabled
  • $550 for an elderly/disabled household (2026 amount): If the head of household, spouse, or sole member is elderly (62+) or disabled
  • Medical expenses: Unreimbursed medical expenses above 10% of annual income (elderly/disabled households only; families who already had this deduction move up to 10% in steps, 5% and then 7.5%)
  • Disability assistance expenses: Attendant care or assistive equipment costs that enable a household member to work
  • Child care: Child care costs necessary for a household member to work or go to school

HUD adjusts the two flat amounts for inflation every January. These figures come from HUD's updated income rule (HOTMA). HUD gave most housing authorities until January 1, 2027 to switch over, so a PHA that hasn't switched yet may still use the older numbers: $480 per dependent, $400 for an elderly/disabled household, and a 3% medical threshold.

I'm Above the Limit. Am I Totally Out of Options?

Not necessarily. You might still qualify for housing assistance in these situations:

  • Your income recently dropped: PHAs look at your current/anticipated income, not last year's tax return. If you lost your job or had hours cut, your current income may qualify you
  • You're close to the cutoff: Apply anyway. Some income doesn't count at all (see the list above), and the PHA does the official math, not you. Let them run the numbers before you rule yourself out
  • Try other programs: LIHTC housing uses 60% AMI in many cases. Some workforce housing goes up to 80% AMI. Our program comparison tool lays the options side by side
  • Different PHAs have different limits: If you live near a county line, the adjacent county may have higher income limits. Check neighboring areas

What Happens If My Income Goes Up After I'm on Section 8?

Getting a raise or a new job while on Section 8 does NOT automatically disqualify you.

Your rent portion is recalculated at your annual recertification. If your income goes up, you'll pay a higher share of rent (still capped at 30% of your adjusted income in most cases). You only "graduate" from the program when your calculated rent share equals or exceeds the full market rent. At that point you're paying market rate anyway.

Some PHAs also have mandatory minimum rent requirements ($50/month is common) regardless of income. If your income goes up a lot mid-year, you're required to report it, but you won't lose your voucher immediately. The rules are set up so that earning more doesn't cost you your housing.
