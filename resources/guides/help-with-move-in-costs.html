Help With Move-In Costs: Security Deposits, First Month's Rent, and Application Fees

Matt · Updated November 2026

Getting into a new place often costs more than staying in an old one. A landlord may want a security deposit, the first month's rent, sometimes the last month's rent, and an application fee, all before you get the keys. Move-in help is harder to find than help with back rent, but it exists. Most of it comes from a few federal programs run by local agencies, plus charities.

Here's the short version: Call 2-1-1 and ask for "help with a security deposit and first month's rent." If you are homeless or about to be, ask for rapid rehousing or homelessness prevention. If you are a veteran, call 877-424-3838 and ask about SSVF. If you have a voucher, ask your housing authority in writing whether it offers deposit help. Ask the landlord about a payment plan, and check your state's deposit limit.

Before you pay anyone

Never pay a deposit for a place you have not seen, or by wire transfer, gift card, or crypto. Fake landlords target people in a hurry. Our guide to rental scams and fake landlords shows how to check who owns a property before you pay.

1. Call 211 first

211 connects you with local help for housing and bills. Dial 2-1-1 from most phones. 211.org says "every call is completely confidential." Move-in help changes often and funds run out, so 211 is the fastest way to learn which programs near you have money right now. Ask by name for help with a security deposit, first month's rent, rapid rehousing, homelessness prevention, and utility deposits. Write down every name and number, because you will probably need to call more than one.

2. If you are homeless or about to be: rapid rehousing

Rapid rehousing helps people who are homeless move into a rental home quickly. It is paid for mostly by HUD's Continuum of Care (CoC) and Emergency Solutions Grants (ESG) programs. Each area's Continuum of Care decides who gets help, often through a system called coordinated entry.

  • What CoC money can pay: HUD's rule says CoC grant funds "may be used for security deposits in an amount not to exceed 2 months of rent." A program can also pay the last month's rent in advance, "in addition to the security deposit and payment of first month's rent" (24 CFR 578.51(a)(2)).
  • How long help lasts: rapid rehousing can give short-term help (up to 3 months) or medium-term help (3 to 24 months). Each local CoC sets its own limits and may ask you to pay part of the rent (24 CFR 578.37).
  • How to get in: ask 211, a shelter case manager, or a street outreach worker for a coordinated entry assessment. Our emergency housing page explains shelters and rapid rehousing.

3. Emergency Solutions Grants (ESG): deposits, fees, and first and last month's rent

ESG is a HUD grant passed to local agencies. It pays for rapid rehousing for people who are already homeless (24 CFR 576.104) and homelessness prevention for people about to lose their housing. Homelessness prevention is mainly for households with income below 30% of the area median income (24 CFR 576.103).

HUD's ESG rule, 24 CFR 576.105(a), lists move-in costs an ESG program can pay. The money goes to the landlord or utility company, not to you:

  • Rental application fees that the owner charges all applicants.
  • Security deposits equal to no more than 2 months' rent.
  • Last month's rent, "if necessary to obtain housing," paid with the deposit and first month's rent.
  • Utility deposits.
  • Moving costs, such as a truck rental or movers.

The first month's rent is paid as rental assistance. HUD's rule lets a program pay up to 100% of it if you sign a lease and move in before that month ends (24 CFR 576.106(i)(2)). In total, ESG rental help cannot go over 24 months in any 3-year period. Local programs can set lower limits.

4. HOME security deposit programs

Many cities, counties, and states get HUD HOME money. HOME can pay for tenant-based rental assistance (TBRA), and it can also be used just for security deposits. HUD's rule says HOME funds can give "loans or grants to very low- and low-income families for security deposits," whether or not the family gets any other rent help (24 CFR 92.209(j)(1)).

The help can be up to two months' rent, and it can be a grant or a loan that you pay back, so ask which before you sign (24 CFR 92.209(j)(2) and (j)(4)). Only the tenant can apply (24 CFR 92.209(j)(3)). Not every area uses HOME this way. To find out, call your city or county housing or community development department and ask: "Do you have a HOME security deposit program or tenant-based rental assistance?"

5. Veterans: SSVF

Supportive Services for Veteran Families (SSVF) is a VA program run by local nonprofits. The VA says it serves "low-income Veteran families who are experiencing homelessness or are at imminent risk of experiencing homelessness."

  • SSVF can pay security deposits and utility deposits to help you get or keep permanent housing. The deposit goes straight to the landlord or utility company (38 CFR 62.34(c)(3)).
  • You can get security deposit help once every 2 years, and utility deposit help once every 2 years. Each 2-year period starts on the date the provider pays the deposit (38 CFR 62.34(c)(1)–(2)).
  • A provider may ask you to pay part of the deposit (38 CFR 62.34(c)(6)).

Call the National Call Center for Homeless Veterans at 877-424-3838. The VA says it is free, confidential, and open 24/7. Ask to be connected to an SSVF provider near you. Our guides to HUD-VASH and VA benefits cover other help for veterans.

6. TANF emergency help

TANF is the cash help program for families with children. Each state runs its own. Federal rules let states give one-time crisis payments that do not count as ongoing TANF "assistance." The rule calls them "nonrecurrent, short-term benefits" for "a specific crisis situation," and they cannot extend beyond four months (45 CFR 260.31(b)(1)).

Names vary, such as "emergency assistance" or "diversion." Ask your state or county social services office: "Do you have one-time emergency help for a security deposit or first month's rent?" You will usually need a child in the home and to meet an income limit.

7. If you have a Section 8 voucher: ask your housing authority

HUD lets housing authorities use their voucher administrative fees to help families lease a unit. Notice PIH 2022-18 says "the PHA may provide security deposit assistance for the family." It can also help with utility deposits, application fees, holding fees, and broker fees. This is optional. A housing authority can do it only after it adopts a written policy in its administrative plan.

  • The help cannot be more than the deposit the owner actually charges, or more than your state or local law allows.
  • The housing authority can require that the deposit come back to it when you move out.
  • If the money is paid to you, you must sign an agreement to use it for the deposit. Money you don't return becomes a debt to the housing authority and can be grounds to end your voucher, after a chance for an informal hearing or review.

What to send your caseworker (email or letter):

"Hello, my name is [name], and I have a voucher with [housing authority]. I found a unit at [address], and the owner is asking for a [amount] security deposit. Does [housing authority] offer security deposit, utility deposit, or application fee assistance under Notice PIH 2022-18? If so, please tell me how to apply and what the terms are. If not, can you refer me to local programs that help? Thank you, [name, phone, voucher number]."

If you are moving with your voucher, ask both your current and your new housing authority. Our guide on how to talk to your PHA has more tips.

8. Local charities and community groups

Many local groups help with a deposit or first month's rent when they have funds. Ask 211 about community action agencies, the Salvation Army, Catholic Charities, and St. Vincent de Paul, and local churches, mosques, and synagogues, even if you are not a member. If you are leaving an abusive home, ask a domestic violence program; some have funds to help you move. For utility deposits and old balances, see our utility assistance guide. Charities often pay only part, so ask whether two groups can each pay a share.

9. Lower the cost: payment plans, deposit caps, and alternatives

Ask for a payment plan

Many landlords would rather get the deposit over a few months than leave a unit empty, and in some places it is your right. In Washington State, if you ask in writing, the landlord must let you pay deposits, nonrefundable fees, and last month's rent in installments. The landlord cannot charge extra for this. There is an exception when those costs total 25% of the first month's rent or less and last month's rent is not due at move-in (RCW 59.18.610). Here is a simple way to ask:

"I'd like to rent [address] and can move in on [date]. I can pay the first month's rent of [amount] at signing. Would you accept the security deposit of [amount] in [three] equal payments with my rent over the next [three] months? I'm glad to sign a written payment schedule."

Check your state's deposit limit

Some states cap how much a landlord can charge for a deposit. Others have no cap. Our state pages list each state's security deposit limit, how fast it must be returned, and, in some states, limits on application fees. Our guide on getting your security deposit back explains the move-out side.

Be careful with deposit alternatives

Some landlords offer a "deposit alternative," such as a surety bond or deposit insurance, for a smaller fee. Read the terms before you agree. Ask: "Is this fee refundable? Can I still be billed for damage or unpaid rent?" Often the answer is no refund, and yes, you can still be billed. If you get HOME tenant-based rental assistance, HUD does not allow surety bonds or deposit insurance in place of a deposit (24 CFR 92.209(j)(6)).

What to have ready when you ask for help

  • Photo ID, proof of income for everyone in the home, and proof of your situation, such as an eviction notice, a shelter letter, or a DD-214.
  • The unit details: address, rent, deposit amount, and the landlord's name and phone. Some programs want a copy of the lease or a written offer.
  • A landlord who will cooperate. Most programs pay the landlord directly, and many need the landlord to fill out a form. Tell the landlord early that a program may pay part of your move-in costs.

If they say no

  • Ask why, and what would change the answer. A cheaper unit or a smaller deposit may fit.
  • Try the next program, and ask to be put on a list for when new funds come in. A "no" from one is not a "no" from all.
  • Ask for a reasonable accommodation if a disability makes a rule hard for you to meet. See our reasonable accommodation guide.
  • Make a plan for the rest. Our guide to budgeting when money is tight can help you find the gap.

Frequently asked questions

Who helps pay a security deposit?

Start by calling 211. Depending on your situation, help can come from rapid rehousing or homelessness prevention programs paid for by HUD's Emergency Solutions Grants and Continuum of Care programs, a city or county HOME security deposit program, SSVF if you are a veteran, your state's TANF emergency help, your housing authority if you have a voucher, or local charities. Each program has its own rules and money often runs out, so ask several.

Can I get help with first and last month's rent?

Sometimes. Under HUD rules, Emergency Solutions Grants programs can pay the first month's rent and, when it is needed to get the unit, the last month's rent along with the security deposit (24 CFR 576.106 and 576.105). Continuum of Care rental assistance can also pay a last month's rent in advance (24 CFR 578.51). These programs mostly serve people who are homeless or about to be.

Will my housing authority pay my security deposit?

Maybe. HUD lets housing authorities use voucher administrative fees for security deposit help (Notice PIH 2022-18), but it is optional. The housing authority must have a written policy first, and it can make you return the money when you move out. Ask your caseworker in writing whether it offers deposit, utility deposit, or application fee help.

Can I pay my security deposit in installments?

You can always ask. In some places it is a legal right. In Washington State, for example, a landlord must let you pay deposits, nonrefundable fees, and last month's rent in installments if you ask in writing, unless they total 25% of the first month's rent or less and last month's rent is not due at move-in (RCW 59.18.610). Get any plan in writing.
