What Does PHA Stand For? Find Your Local Public Housing Authority

Matt · 6 min read

PHA stands for Public Housing Authority, the local government agency that runs Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers and public housing where you live. Every application for either program goes through a PHA, and each one keeps its own waiting list, its own rules, and its own phone number. Find yours in the directory below, or look it up on HUD’s national list.

What Is a Public Housing Authority (PHA)?

A Public Housing Authority is a local agency that manages public housing and runs Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers in your area. For most rental help, the PHA is the front door. If you want a subsidized apartment, or a voucher to use with a private landlord, they're who you apply to.

Every city and many counties have their own PHA. Your PHA keeps the waiting lists, processes applications, does inspections, and makes sure tenants and landlords both follow the program rules. PHAs are government agencies, but each one runs fairly independently, so applying can look very different, and take very different amounts of time, from one PHA to the next.

Why You Should Find Your PHA Now

Getting on a PHA's waiting list is often the first step toward stable, affordable housing. Many PHAs have long waiting lists (sometimes years long), so the sooner you apply, the sooner your name can come up. You also don't have to stick to your local PHA. You can apply to several, even in different cities or counties, and each one adds a real chance. For practical strategies on dealing with waiting lists, see our guide on how to get through housing waiting lists.

Using HUD's PHA Locator Tool

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) keeps an official list of every PHA in the country. It's the most reliable place to find contact information and see what programs a PHA runs.

Visit: https://www.hud.gov/contactus/public-housing-contacts

On this page, you pick your state from a list or click it on a map. HUD then opens a PDF of every housing authority in that state, sorted by city. For each one it shows:

  • The PHA's official name and its HUD code
  • Physical address
  • Phone and fax numbers
  • Email address
  • Program type: "Low-Rent" (public housing only), "Section 8" (vouchers only), or "Combined" (both)

The list doesn't include websites, and HUD says each PHA keeps its own entry up to date, so if a number doesn't work, search for the PHA's name online.

Finding Your PHA Step by Step

  1. Go to the HUD PHA locator using the link above
  2. Select your state from the list (or click it on the map) and open the page
  3. Find your city in the PDF. It's sorted by city, or press Ctrl+F (Command+F on a Mac) and type your city or county name
  4. Review the results. You may see several PHAs if you live in a larger metro area, such as a city authority and a county or state one
  5. Check the program type to see whether each PHA runs vouchers, public housing, or both
  6. Note the phone number and address. Write them down or save the PDF

What to Do When You Call

Take a few minutes to get ready before you call. PHA phone lines are busy, so make the call count.

Best Times to Call

Call early in the morning (8-9 am local time) on Tuesday through Thursday. Mondays are often busy with people calling after the weekend, and Fridays can have reduced staff. Call early and you'll usually wait less on hold and have a better chance of reaching someone before the office gets slammed.

When you call, you might say something like: "Hi, I'm calling to ask about applying for a Housing Choice Voucher" or "I'd like information about your public housing program." Be direct and polite. Housing staff take these calls all day, and getting to the point helps them help you.

Questions to Ask Your PHA

Have these questions ready when you call. Take notes on the answers, including the name of the person you spoke with and the date.

  • Is your waiting list currently open? (Some PHAs have closed lists and don't accept new applications)
  • What programs do you administer? (Public housing, Section 8 vouchers, VASH, other)
  • What's the current wait time for each program? (Be prepared for long answers; sometimes it's years)
  • What are your local preferences? (Many PHAs prioritize certain groups: homeless individuals, people with disabilities, people being evicted, veterans, etc.)
  • What documents do I need to apply? (Photo ID, proof of income, Social Security numbers for household members, etc.)
  • Do I apply in person, by mail, or online?
  • Have you stopped issuing vouchers or leasing for now? (Some PHAs pause new vouchers when funding is tight, and that affects how fast the list moves)
  • Do you accept applications by mail, phone, or online?
  • When do you expect to start issuing new vouchers? (If the list is closed or frozen)

What Information to Have Ready

When you call, be prepared to provide or confirm:

  • Your full name
  • Current address or phone number
  • Rough household size (how many people will live with you)
  • General income range (you don't need exact numbers yet, but PHA staff may ask if you think you qualify based on income limits)

About Local Preferences

Most PHAs have "local preferences": categories of applicants they move up the list because of local housing needs. Common ones include:

  • People who currently live in the PHA's jurisdiction
  • People experiencing homelessness
  • People with disabilities
  • Victims of domestic violence
  • Veterans and their families (HUD-VASH is separate: it doesn't use a waiting list, and the VA makes the referral)
  • People living in substandard housing
  • People being involuntarily displaced

Ask about local preferences. If you qualify for one, you may get priority and a shorter wait. If you don't, you can still apply, but your place on the list will be different.

Can You Apply to Multiple PHAs?

Yes, and we recommend it. Applying to several PHAs in your region, or in neighboring regions, gives you a much better shot at getting help sooner. Each PHA keeps its own waiting list, and wait times vary wildly. One PHA might have a 2-year wait while another has a 5-year wait, or one might have a closed list while another is taking applications.

You can apply to any PHA, even if you don't live there now. So if your city has a very long waiting list, apply in the surrounding counties and nearby cities too. It's a completely legitimate strategy, and many housing advocates recommend it.

Keeping Track of Your Applications

Keep a Housing Folder

Start a folder (physical or digital) with copies of important documents: your ID, proof of income, list of household members with birthdates, Social Security numbers, and any documentation of local preferences you might qualify for. When you call PHAs or go to apply, everything will be in one place.

Write down the date, time, the person's name, and what you learned on every call. If a PHA tells you something important about how to apply, ask them to put it in an email if they can. That gives you a record if there's confusion later.

Ask for extensions or the names of specific people in the housing assistance program. If one person doesn't call back, you can try someone else next time. Be polite, and keep calling.

After the call, send an email or mail a letter saying you want to be added to the waiting list, with all your information in it. That letter documents your application date, and your application date matters for your place on the list.

What Happens After You Apply

After you submit your application, the PHA checks that it's complete and that you meet the basic eligibility requirements (citizenship, income limits, background checks). If everything is in order, you go on the waiting list. You might get a wait number or a confirmation letter. Keep it.

Then you wait. The PHA will contact you when there's an opening, which could be months or years depending on the list. Meanwhile, apply to other PHAs, look for market-rate housing that fits your budget, and look into other housing assistance programs.

Next Steps

Find your PHA in the directory above or on the HUD PHA locator, and make that first call this week. Nothing else in the process starts until you do.

Once you know which programs they run and how long the wait might be, work on other options at the same time, like applying to more PHAs or looking into LIHTC (tax credit) housing, which sometimes has shorter waits.
