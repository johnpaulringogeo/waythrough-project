How Your Rent Is Calculated in Section 8

Matt · Updated October 2026

Section 8 rent calculation is one of the most confusing parts of the program, but understanding it helps you predict your costs, plan your budget, and catch mistakes. The short version: with a Housing Choice Voucher, your share of the rent usually starts at about 30% of your adjusted monthly income, and the housing authority pays the rest up to a limit. Getting to that adjusted income takes several steps and deductions, and the deduction amounts are changing under a federal law called HOTMA. This guide walks you through the whole formula with worked examples, using both the figures most housing authorities use today and the figures for 2027.

The Core Rule: Your Total Tenant Payment

HUD calls your share the Total Tenant Payment (TTP). It is not simply 30% of what you earn. Under HUD's rule (24 CFR 5.628), your TTP is the highest of these four amounts, rounded to the nearest dollar:

  • 30% of your monthly adjusted income (your income after the deductions explained below). For most families, this is the one that counts.
  • 10% of your monthly gross income (before any deductions). This matters when your deductions are very large compared to your income.
  • The welfare rent, which applies only if a welfare agency pays you assistance and specifically designates part of it for housing costs. If that doesn't describe you, skip it.
  • Your housing authority's minimum rent, which can be anywhere from $0 up to $50 a month in the voucher program.

"Adjusted income" is not the same as total household income. It's your total income minus certain deductions that HUD allows. Let's break down the formula step by step.

The Formula

  1. Add up your household's gross income for the year
  2. Subtract the deductions you qualify for to get annual adjusted income, then divide by 12
  3. Your Total Tenant Payment (TTP) is the highest of 30% of monthly adjusted income, 10% of monthly gross income, the welfare rent (if any), or the minimum rent
  4. The Public Housing Agency (PHA) pays the landlord a Housing Assistance Payment (HAP): the lower of the payment standard minus your TTP, or the gross rent (rent plus the utility allowance) minus your TTP
  5. You pay the rest of the gross rent; if you pay your own utilities, what you pay the landlord is your share minus the utility allowance

This guide covers the Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8) program. Public housing uses the same TTP formula, but there is no payment standard; ask your housing authority how your rent is set.

Step 1: Calculate Total Household Gross Income

Gross income includes the money coming into your household, before taxes. HUD counts income for the year, and this includes:

  • Wages and salary (before taxes and deductions)
  • Self-employment income
  • Social Security (including Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI))
  • Veterans benefits and disability compensation
  • Unemployment benefits and workers' compensation
  • Child support and alimony received
  • Pensions and retirement income
  • Investment income, interest, and dividends
  • Rental income
  • Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) and other public assistance (varies by PHA)

The PHA will ask you to document all income sources. You'll need recent pay stubs, benefit letters, bank statements, and tax returns for self-employed people.

Example: Sarah's Income

Sarah works full-time earning $2,400 per month before taxes. She also receives $200 per month in child support. Her household has no other income sources.

Total Gross Monthly Income = $2,400 + $200 = $2,600 ($31,200 a year)

Step 2: Apply Allowable Deductions

This is where many people don't realize they can lower their rent. The PHA subtracts these deductions from your annual income before calculating rent. There is no flat "standard deduction" that every household gets. Each deduction below has its own rules, so claim only what applies to you and document each one.

Two Sets of Numbers: Today and 2027

The Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act of 2016 (HOTMA) changed these deductions, but HUD delayed enforcement while it updated its computer systems. Under Notice PIH 2026-15 (issued May 14, 2026), HUD will enforce HOTMA sections 102 and 104 starting January 1, 2027: every rent calculation that takes effect on or after that date must follow the new rules. Housing authorities in the Moving to Work (MTW) demonstration and those that use only HUD's Family Reporting Software get a later date that HUD hasn't announced yet. Because annual reexaminations often start about 120 days early, some housing authorities began collecting information under the new rules in September 2026.

HUD adjusts the HOTMA amounts for inflation every year. A housing authority that switched to the HOTMA rules early used $500 per dependent and $550 for an elderly or disabled family in 2026. If you're not sure which rules your housing authority is using, ask it, or look at the deduction amounts on your rent calculation.

HOTMA also adds an asset limit. Under the new rules, a family generally can't be admitted or keep assistance if its net assets are over $109,797 in 2027 (retirement accounts don't count) or if it owns a home it could live in, with some exceptions. The housing authority can wait up to six months before acting on it.

Dependent Deduction

You get a deduction for each dependent: $480 a year under the rules most housing authorities use today, and $525 a year under the HOTMA rules in 2027. A dependent is a household member, other than the head of household, spouse, or co-head, who is:

  • Under 18
  • A person with a disability (any age)
  • A full-time student (any age)

Foster children, foster adults, and live-in aides don't count as dependents. An older relative who lives with you is not a dependent just because of age. You need to document who your dependents are and why they qualify. Birth certificates, disability documentation, and school enrollment letters work.

Elderly or Disabled Family Deduction

If the head of household, spouse, or co-head is 62 or older or has a disability, your household is an "elderly family" or "disabled family" and gets one deduction for the whole family: $400 a year today, and $575 a year under the HOTMA rules in 2027. It doesn't matter how many members are elderly or disabled, and an elderly or disabled relative who is not the head, spouse, or co-head doesn't qualify the family on their own. This is separate from the dependent deduction. You'll need to prove disability or age with a doctor's letter, Social Security Administration (SSA) documentation, or a birth certificate.

Medical Expense Deduction

If you are an elderly or disabled family (as defined above), you can deduct unreimbursed medical expenses for every member of the family. This includes:

  • Doctor and hospital bills
  • Prescription medications
  • Health insurance premiums
  • Dental and vision care
  • In-home care or nursing services
  • Equipment and medical devices

Only the part of these expenses (together with any disability assistance expenses, below) that is above a threshold counts. Under the rules most housing authorities use today, the threshold is 3% of your annual income. Under the HOTMA rules it rises to 10%. Families who were already getting this deduction on January 1, 2024 get phased-in relief: a 5% threshold for the first 12 months, then 7.5% for the next 12, then 10%. A family whose medical or disability costs go up, or who has another change in circumstances that causes a financial hardship, can ask the housing authority for general hardship relief at 5%. You need to itemize expenses and provide receipts or bills.

Example: Walter's Medical Expenses

Walter is 68 and lives alone on $1,400 a month in Social Security ($16,800 a year). He pays $250 a month ($3,000 a year) in medical costs that nothing reimburses.

  • Today (3% threshold): $3,000 − $504 (3% of $16,800) = $2,496 in medical deductions, plus the $400 elderly family deduction. Adjusted income = $16,800 − $2,896 = $13,904 a year ($1,158.67 a month). TTP = 30% = $348.
  • 2027 HOTMA rules (10% threshold): $3,000 − $1,680 (10% of $16,800) = $1,320, plus the $575 elderly family deduction. Adjusted income = $16,800 − $1,895 = $14,905 a year ($1,242.08 a month). TTP = 30% = $373.
  • 2027 with phased-in relief (5% threshold, first 12 months): $3,000 − $840 = $2,160, plus $575. Adjusted income = $14,065 a year ($1,172.08 a month). TTP = $352.

In every case, 10% of Walter's gross monthly income ($140) is lower, so the 30% figure is his TTP. If you have high medical costs, ask your housing authority about hardship relief before your first reexamination under the new rules.

Childcare Expense Deduction

If someone in your household needs to pay for the care of children 12 or younger so they can work, look for work, or go to school, that childcare cost can be deducted. This includes:

  • Daycare center fees
  • In-home daycare providers
  • Summer camp and after-school programs
  • Nanny or babysitter fees

Only reasonable, unreimbursed costs count, and when the care lets someone work, the deduction can't be more than the income that person earns from that work. You need receipts or a letter from the childcare provider documenting the monthly cost.

Disability Assistance Expense Deduction

Any family, not only elderly or disabled families, can deduct unreimbursed costs for attendant care or auxiliary apparatus (for example, a wheelchair, a reading device, or vehicle modifications) for a family member with a disability, when the expense is needed so that a family member (including the person with the disability) can work. These costs are combined with any medical expenses and count only above the same 3% (today) or 10% (HOTMA) threshold, and the deduction can't be more than the earnings of the adults who can work because of it. A letter from a medical professional explaining why the expense is necessary helps.

Earned Income Disallowance (EID) Has Ended

Older guides describe the Earned Income Disallowance, which used to exclude part of a qualifying family's new earnings for up to 24 months. HOTMA eliminated it: no new families could start EID after January 1, 2024, and HUD's guidance says no family is still receiving it after December 31, 2025. Some Jobs Plus programs have a separate disregard of their own, so ask your PHA if you're in one.

Step 3: Calculate Adjusted Income and TTP

Once you've applied all deductions, divide your annual adjusted income by 12. Then compare 30% of that monthly figure with 10% of your monthly gross income and your PHA's minimum rent. The highest one is your Total Tenant Payment.

Example: Sarah's Deductions

Sarah's gross income is $2,600 a month ($31,200 a year). She has two children (ages 8 and 12), and she is not elderly or disabled, so her only deduction is for dependents:

  • Today: 2 children × $480 = $960 a year. Adjusted income = $31,200 − $960 = $30,240 a year, or $2,520 a month.
  • 2027 HOTMA rules: 2 children × $525 = $1,050 a year. Adjusted income = $31,200 − $1,050 = $30,150 a year, or $2,512.50 a month.

Now calculate her Total Tenant Payment. 10% of her gross monthly income is $260, which is lower than 30% of her adjusted income, so:

TTP today = $2,520 × 0.30 = $756
TTP under the 2027 HOTMA rules = $2,512.50 × 0.30 = $753.75, rounded to $754

The examples below use the $756 figure.

Step 4: The Utility Allowance and the Voucher Subsidy

Section 8 recognizes that many tenants pay some utilities (electricity, gas, water, trash) out of their own pocket. The PHA sets a "utility allowance," an estimate of what those utilities cost for your unit size and type. What you actually pay in utilities doesn't change the calculation; it's a fixed allowance. If your landlord pays all the utilities, your allowance is $0.

Utility allowances vary based on unit size, the utilities you pay, and location. Your PHA publishes its utility allowance schedule and must review it every year.

The voucher subsidy works from the gross rent, which is the rent to the landlord plus the utility allowance, and from your PHA's payment standard, the most it will use to calculate your subsidy for your voucher size. The PHA pays the landlord a Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) equal to the lower of:

  • The payment standard minus your TTP, or
  • The gross rent minus your TTP

You pay the rest of the gross rent (your "family share"). If you pay utilities, you pay the landlord your family share minus the utility allowance.

Example: Sarah's Apartment

Sarah's TTP is $756. She finds a 2-bedroom apartment that rents for $950 a month, and she pays the utilities. Her PHA's utility allowance for that unit is $170 a month, and its payment standard for her voucher size is $1,200.

  • Gross rent = $950 + $170 = $1,120
  • HAP = the lower of $1,200 − $756 = $444, or $1,120 − $756 = $364. The PHA pays the landlord $364.
  • Sarah's family share = $1,120 − $364 = $756 (equal to her TTP, because the gross rent is under the payment standard)
  • Sarah pays the landlord $756 − $170 = $586, and pays her utility bills herself

Check: $586 from Sarah + $364 from the PHA = $950, the full rent.

When the Rent Is Above the Payment Standard: The 40% Limit

You can rent a unit whose gross rent is higher than the payment standard, but you pay the difference. To protect families from taking on too much, when you first move into a unit (or move to a new one) with a gross rent above the payment standard, your family share can't be more than 40% of your monthly adjusted income. If it would be, the PHA can't approve the lease unless the rent comes down. The 40% limit applies only at the start of the lease, not at later reexaminations.

For Sarah, 40% of $2,520 is $1,008. If the apartment rented for $1,150, the gross rent would be $1,320. The PHA would pay $444 (the payment standard minus her TTP), and her family share would be $1,320 − $444 = $876, which is under $1,008, so the PHA could approve it. She would pay the landlord $876 − $170 = $706. At a rent of $1,350, her share would be $1,076, over the 40% limit, so the PHA could not approve the unit at that rent.

What You Actually Pay: The Three Amounts

It's important to understand that your Section 8 housing cost has three parts:

1. Your Rent to the Landlord

This is your family share minus the utility allowance (or your whole family share if the landlord pays all utilities). This is what you pay your landlord each month.

2. Your Utility Bills

This is not rent; it's what you pay the utility companies. The PHA's utility allowance is an estimate. If your actual utilities cost more, you pay more out of pocket. If they cost less, you save money. If the utility allowance is ever larger than your family share, the PHA pays the difference as a "utility reimbursement," either to you or directly to the utility company.

3. Housing Assistance Payment (What the PHA Pays the Landlord)

This is the voucher subsidy, calculated from the payment standard and the gross rent as shown above. The PHA pays this directly to the landlord. You don't see this money, but it's part of your benefit.

In Sarah's example: She pays $586 to the landlord, the PHA pays $364 to the landlord, and she pays her utilities separately (the PHA estimated $170, but her actual bills could be higher or lower). Sarah's total out-of-pocket housing cost is her $586 rent plus whatever her actual utilities are.

What Happens When Your Income Changes?

Your rent is recalculated at your annual reexamination (recertification). Between reexaminations, you can ask for an interim reexamination, and the rules for when rent changes mid-year depend on whether your PHA follows the HOTMA rules yet. Always follow your PHA's reporting rules, which are in its Administrative Plan.

Your Income Goes Up

Your TTP and your rent will eventually go up. Before your PHA switches to the HOTMA rules, its Administrative Plan decides whether an increase is processed right away or at your next annual reexamination. Under the HOTMA rules, the PHA generally doesn't count increases in earned income until your next annual reexamination (unless you already got an interim rent decrease earlier in the same year), and it must do an interim reexamination when other income changes would raise your adjusted income by 10% or more. If you don't report a change on time, a resulting rent increase can be charged back retroactively.

Your Income Goes Down (Job Loss, Reduced Hours)

Request an interim reexamination right away. Your income decrease will be reflected in a lower TTP and lower rent. Under the HOTMA rules, the PHA may decline to do an interim if the drop is less than 10% of your adjusted income (or a lower threshold your PHA sets), and a decrease takes effect the first of the month after the change you reported. For example, if Sarah loses her job, her income drops to $200 a month (just the child support). This significantly lowers her rent, often making the difference between staying housed and facing eviction.

Income Becomes Zero (Unemployment, Benefit Loss)

Your PHA may charge a minimum rent of up to $50 a month in the voucher program, and some PHAs set it at $0. If you can't pay even that because of a financial hardship, such as losing your job or waiting on a benefits decision, the PHA must grant a hardship exemption from the minimum rent. See our guide on what to do when you lose your job for strategies.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Not Claiming Deductions You Qualify For

Many people don't claim medical expenses, childcare costs, or disability assistance expenses because they don't know they can. Ask your PHA for a complete list of deductions and claim everything you qualify for. Each deduction lowers your rent.

Mistake 2: Assuming Your Utility Allowance Will Cover Your Utilities

The utility allowance is an estimate. It rarely matches exactly what you actually spend. If you live in a climate with hot summers or cold winters, you might spend much more on air conditioning or heating. Budget for utilities as a separate monthly expense. Review your actual utility bills and plan accordingly.

Mistake 3: Not Reporting Income Changes

If your income drops, report it to the PHA right away and ask for an interim reexamination. Don't wait for your annual reexamination: a rent decrease generally starts based on when you report, so every month you wait can cost you money. If your income goes up, report it as your PHA requires, because unreported increases can be charged back.

Mistake 4: Forgetting That Deductions Are Documented

The PHA doesn't take your word for childcare costs, medical expenses, or anything else. You must provide receipts, bills, or letters from providers. Keep organized records of all deductible expenses. During recertification, bring documentation or the deduction won't count.

How to Review Your Rent Calculation

Your annual recertification paperwork should show how the PHA calculated your rent. Review it carefully:

  1. Check that your income is correct. Verify all sources of income are listed.
  2. Check that all your deductions are claimed, at the right amounts for the rules your PHA uses. Are you claiming all dependents? Medical expenses? Childcare costs?
  3. Verify the utility allowance matches your unit size, type, and the utilities you pay.
  4. Calculate TTP yourself: (Annual Income − Deductions) ÷ 12 × 0.30. Compare it with 10% of your monthly gross income and the minimum rent; the highest wins.
  5. Check the subsidy: the PHA pays the lower of the payment standard minus TTP or the gross rent minus TTP. Your rent to the landlord is the gross rent minus that payment, minus the utility allowance. Does the result match your assigned rent?

If something doesn't match, contact your PHA and ask for an explanation. Errors happen, and the PHA should fix them.

Section 8 Rent Calculation Example (Full Scenario)

Household: Rosa (head of household, age 38, no disability), two children (ages 6 and 9), and Rosa's mother (age 75)

Income: Rosa works part-time earning $1,800/month. She receives $150/month child support. Her mother receives $650/month Social Security.

Total gross income: $1,800 + $150 + $650 = $2,600 a month ($31,200 a year)

Deductions (annual):
Dependents (2 children): $960 today ($480 each), or $1,050 under the 2027 HOTMA rules ($525 each)
Childcare: Rosa pays $200/month for after-school care so she can work = $2,400
Elderly family deduction: none. Rosa's mother is 75, but she isn't the head of household, spouse, or co-head, so the household isn't an "elderly family," and she isn't a dependent.
Medical expenses: none. Her mother's $180/month in medical bills can't be deducted, because only elderly or disabled families can deduct medical expenses.

Total deductions: $3,360 today, or $3,450 under the 2027 HOTMA rules

Monthly adjusted income: ($31,200 − $3,360) ÷ 12 = $2,320 today, or ($31,200 − $3,450) ÷ 12 = $2,312.50 in 2027

TTP (30% of monthly adjusted income, which is higher than 10% of gross income, $260): $696 today, or $694 under the 2027 HOTMA rules

The unit: A 3-bedroom apartment rents for $1,300/month and Rosa pays the utilities. Utility allowance: $210, so gross rent = $1,510. Payment standard: $1,600.

PHA payment to the landlord: $1,510 − $696 = $814 today ($816 in 2027)
Rosa's rent to the landlord: $696 − $210 = $486 today ($484 in 2027)

Without any deductions, Rosa's TTP would be $780 (30% of $2,600), so the deductions save her $84 per month today ($86 under the 2027 amounts). The PHA covers the rest of the rent to her landlord. If Rosa's mother were the head of household or a co-head (an adult who shares responsibility for the family's program obligations), the family would be an elderly family: it would get the elderly family deduction, and her mother's medical bills above the 3% (today) or 10% (HOTMA) threshold would count.
