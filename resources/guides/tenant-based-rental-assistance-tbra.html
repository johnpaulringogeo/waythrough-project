TBRA (Tenant-Based Rental Assistance): What It Is and What to Do When It Ends

Matt · Updated November 2026

TBRA stands for tenant-based rental assistance. It is rent help paid for with federal HOME Investment Partnerships money from HUD. A city, county, or state runs it, often through a nonprofit or a housing authority. TBRA helps you pay rent in a home you pick, and the help can move with you. But unlike a Section 8 voucher, it is built to be short-term: each TBRA contract can last up to 24 months. If your TBRA ended sooner than you expected, or the end date is coming, you still have options. Act now, while more of them are open.

Here's the short version: Ask the program in writing why your help ended (or is ending). Ask for a copy of your TBRA agreement, its written rules, and a review. Tell your landlord right away. Apply for emergency rental assistance now. Get on every Section 8 and public housing waiting list you can. Call legal aid the same day you get any eviction notice or court paper.

If you already have an eviction notice or court papers

When TBRA ends, your lease does not. You still owe the rent in your lease, and the landlord can go to court for unpaid rent. Read every paper for two dates: when you must respond, and when your hearing is. Call legal aid the same day (see our guide to free legal help for tenants) and go to every court date. Pay what you can. Don't hold back rent on purpose without first talking to legal aid and checking your state's rules.

What TBRA is

HUD's HOME rule says TBRA is help where "the assisted tenant may move from a housing unit with a right to continued assistance" (24 CFR 92.2). In plain words, the help follows you, not the building. The main rules are in 24 CFR 92.209:

  • Who runs it: HOME money goes to cities, counties, and states. They can run TBRA themselves or hire a housing authority or another group to run it (92.209(k)).
  • What it pays: monthly rent help. Some programs also pay a security deposit (up to two months' rent) or a utility deposit (92.209(a) and (j)).
  • How much: the monthly help can't be more than the program's rent standard for your unit size minus 30% of your monthly adjusted income. Every program must set a minimum amount you pay. It can lower that amount for financial hardship if its written rules allow it (92.209(h)).
  • How long: a TBRA contract "may not exceed 24 months," but it "may be amended or renewed, subject to the availability of HOME funds" (92.209(e)(2)). Renewal is not automatic. The program must check your income again before it renews (92.209(e)(4)).
  • Who qualifies: only low-income households, with income at or below 80% of the area median income (92.2 and 92.209(c)(1)). At least 90% of the families a program helps with TBRA must be at or below 60% of the area median (24 CFR 92.216). Many programs also focus on one group, such as people leaving homelessness, people with disabilities, or survivors of domestic violence (92.209(c)(2)).
  • Job or self-sufficiency programs: a program can require you to join a self-sufficiency program to get TBRA. But the rule says that if you stop taking part, that "is not a basis for terminating the assistance." It can be a reason not to renew (92.209(c)(2)(iii)). If your help was cut in the middle of a contract because you didn't find a job or finish a class, ask the program about this rule in writing.
  • Where you can use it: the program can limit TBRA to its own city, county, or state, or let you use it elsewhere (92.209(d)).

Many programs use TBRA as a bridge while you wait for a Section 8 voucher or a public housing unit. That's why getting on those waiting lists early matters so much.

Other short-term rent help that gets mixed up with TBRA

  • Rapid rehousing: for people who are homeless. It is paid for by HUD's Emergency Solutions Grants (ESG) or Continuum of Care (CoC) programs and run by local homeless service groups. ESG rent help can last up to "24 months of rental assistance during any 3-year period" (24 CFR 576.106). These programs must use a formal process to end help: a written notice with "a clear statement of the reasons," a chance to object to someone other than the person who decided, and a prompt written final decision (24 CFR 576.402 and 578.91). ESG programs must end help "only in the most severe cases."
  • State or local rental assistance: some states and cities pay for their own rent programs. Each one sets its own rules, so ask for them in writing.
  • Emergency rental assistance: usually a one-time payment or a few months of help with back rent or utilities. See our guide to emergency rental assistance.

Not sure which one you have? Look at your approval letter or the agreement you signed. It should name the program and who pays for it, like "HOME," "ESG," or "CoC." That tells you which rules apply. If it doesn't say, ask your case manager to tell you in writing.

If your TBRA ended early or is about to end: step by step

1. Get the reason in writing and ask for a review

The federal HOME rule doesn't spell out an appeal process for TBRA the way Section 8 does. Your rights come mostly from the TBRA agreement you signed, the program's written rules, and your state's law. So ask for those papers. Ask for:

  • The reason your help ended, in writing.
  • A copy of your signed TBRA agreement (the rental assistance contract). Compare its end date with the date your help stopped.
  • The program's written rules on ending help, renewals, and hardship.
  • How to ask for a review or appeal, and the deadline.
  • Whether you can be renewed or put back on, and whether a hardship rule can lower your minimum payment.

If the letter gives a deadline to ask for a review, send your request before that date. Keep a copy, and send it in a way you can prove, like email, certified mail, or hand delivery with a stamped copy. If the deadline already passed, ask anyway in writing, explain why you're late, and call legal aid. If the reason is linked to a disability, you can also ask for a reasonable accommodation.

2. Talk to your landlord early

Don't wait until rent is late. HUD's rule says the TBRA part of your lease (the "tenancy addendum") ends automatically when the rental assistance contract ends (92.209(g)). Your regular lease keeps going, and so does the full rent. Tell your landlord what happened and what you're doing about it. Ask whether they'll take a payment plan or wait while your rent help application is reviewed. Put any deal in writing.

3. Apply for emergency rental assistance now

Call 2-1-1 and ask about rent help, and look up local programs in our emergency rental assistance guide or your city page. Some programs ask for a late notice or past-due balance. Apply anyway and ask what they need. Money runs out, so apply early.

4. Get on Section 8 and public housing waiting lists

Ask your TBRA program whether it works with a local housing authority or can refer you. Then apply to every list that is open. Our guides on how to apply for Section 8, waiting list strategies, and what to do when you find an open list explain how.

5. Get legal aid before any court date

If you get an eviction notice, call legal aid that day. A lawyer can check whether the landlord followed your lease and state law, and whether the program followed its own rules. Our free legal help guide shows how to find one.

6. Have a backup plan

If you might become homeless, ask 211 how to reach your area's homeless services. That is often the way into rapid rehousing. Our emergency housing guide covers what to do if you need a place tonight. And if you lost income, see what to do when you lose your job.

Sample letter: asking why your TBRA ended and asking for a review

[Date]

To: [Program name and address, or your case manager's supervisor]

Re: Tenant-based rental assistance for [your name], [your address]

My rental assistance stopped on [date]. My TBRA agreement, signed [date], says it runs until [date]. Please send me, in writing:

1. The reason my assistance ended, and the rule or part of my agreement you relied on.
2. A copy of my signed TBRA agreement and the program's written policies on ending, renewing, and hardship.
3. How I can ask for a review of this decision, and the deadline.

I am asking for a review of this decision, and for my assistance to be reinstated or renewed. If I don't qualify, please tell me what other help or referrals are available. [If it applies: My situation is related to a disability, and I am asking for a reasonable accommodation.]

Please reply by [date, about 10 days from now]. You can reach me at [phone] or [email].

Sincerely,
[Your name]

If they say no

  • Ask for the "no" in writing, with the reason and the name of the person who decided.
  • Ask who is above them. That may be a program director, then the city, county, or state housing department that pays for the program. Ask legal aid whether to contact that agency.
  • Keep going on steps 2 through 6. A "no" on your TBRA doesn't stop you from getting emergency rent help, a payment plan, or a spot on a waiting list.

Frequently asked questions

What is TBRA?

TBRA means tenant-based rental assistance. It is rent help paid for with HUD's HOME program money and run by a city, county, or state, often through a nonprofit or housing authority. It helps low-income renters pay rent in a home they choose, and the help can move with them.

How long does TBRA last?

Each TBRA contract can last up to 24 months. The program can renew it, but only if HOME money is available, and it must check your income again first. Renewal is not guaranteed, so plan ahead and get on Section 8 and public housing waiting lists early.

Is TBRA the same as Section 8?

No. A Section 8 voucher comes from a housing authority, has no set end date, and gives you a federal right to an informal hearing before it is ended. TBRA is short-term help from a city, county, or state, and your rights come mostly from your TBRA agreement and the program's written rules.

Can my TBRA be stopped if I don't find a job?

A program can require you to join a self-sufficiency program to get TBRA. HUD's rule says that stopping your participation is not a reason to end your help during the contract, but the program can use it as a reason not to renew. Ask the program in writing for the exact reason and rule.

What should I do if my TBRA ended and I can't pay rent?

Ask the program in writing for the reason and a review. Tell your landlord right away and ask for a payment plan. Apply for emergency rental assistance through 211 or local programs. Get on Section 8 and public housing waiting lists. Call legal aid the same day you get an eviction notice.

Do I have a right to appeal when TBRA ends?

It depends on your program. The federal HOME rule does not set up a hearing process for TBRA, so ask for your TBRA agreement and the program's written rules. Rapid rehousing programs paid for by ESG or CoC must give written reasons and a review by someone else. Section 8 gives an informal hearing.
