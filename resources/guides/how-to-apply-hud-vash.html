How to Apply for HUD-VASH

Matt · Updated May 2026

HUD-VASH (HUD-Veterans Affairs Supportive Housing) is one of the most effective housing programs for veterans who are homeless or at risk of homelessness. You don't apply through a housing authority the way you would for standard Section 8. The whole process goes through the VA. This guide covers each step, from the first screening to move-in and after.

To apply for HUD-VASH, contact your local VA Medical Center and ask for a referral to the HUD-VASH program. A VA social worker will look at your veteran status, your homelessness or risk of homelessness, and your clinical need to decide if you're eligible. There's no separate application form. The VA handles everything, including the housing voucher.

What HUD-VASH Is and How It Works

HUD-VASH is a joint federal program run by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). It pairs a housing voucher (similar to Section 8) with intensive case management (which Section 8 doesn't have). The program is built to move veterans from homelessness into stable housing, and from there toward self-sufficiency.

The voucher part works like this: the federal government pays the landlord the difference between what the veteran can afford (usually about 30% of their adjusted income, meaning income after certain deductions) and the full market rent, up to a local payment standard. The case management part is what sets HUD-VASH apart from standard vouchers. A VA social worker or case manager stays with the veteran through the process and after move-in, working on housing and on whatever led to the homelessness in the first place: mental health care, substance use treatment, employment barriers, benefits enrollment, and more.

The combination works. Studies show HUD-VASH has one of the highest housing retention rates of any homeless services program. Around 85-95% of veterans who get housed stay housed, compared to much lower rates in programs without case management.

Step 1: Determine If You're Eligible

HUD-VASH has three core eligibility requirements, and you have to meet all three. There are no exceptions.

You must be a veteran. You need to have served in the U.S. military and received a discharge other than dishonorable. In some cases that includes an "other than honorable" discharge, because the phrase that matters is "other than dishonorable." With a "bad conduct" or "dishonorable" discharge you typically would not qualify, but the VA has some discretion and may work with you on it. If you're not sure where your discharge stands, contact the VA right away. They can pull your service record and tell you. "Other than honorable" is a gray area: some VA benefits require a review, but ask about HUD-VASH specifically. While you're at it, look at the other VA benefits you may qualify for, including disability compensation and healthcare.

You must be homeless or at imminent risk of homelessness. The VA defines homelessness broadly for HUD-VASH. It includes living on the street or in a shelter, living in a place not meant for human habitation (abandoned buildings, cars), living in transitional housing without a permanent lease, facing imminent eviction with nowhere else to go, or living in a motel or hotel because you have no permanent housing. "At risk of homelessness" can also cover doubling up with someone else (couch surfing), spending nights in unsafe situations, or losing your housing within 14 days with no plan to replace it. What the VA is looking for is housing that is precarious: not permanent, not safe, or about to end.

You must be willing to participate in VA supportive services. That's the "S" in VASH. Your voucher comes with case management, which may include mental health care, substance abuse counseling, employment assistance, healthcare coordination, or other services the VA decides fit your situation. Taking part doesn't mean you have to have a mental health condition or a substance use disorder. It means you agree to work with your case manager on keeping your housing. If the VA decides you need substance abuse treatment to stay housed, taking part includes that treatment. If you need mental health care, you'll be expected to go to the appointments. The point is to help you, not to punish you or watch you.

Common Barriers: Discharge Status and Your History

A lot of veterans worry that their discharge status or something in their past will keep them out of HUD-VASH.

Discharge papers (DD-214). If you don't have your DD-214, the VA can help you get it through the National Personnel Records Center. That takes time, so don't wait on it before contacting the VA. Call first, explain your situation, and they'll walk you through getting a replacement.

"Other than honorable" discharge. This is a gray area for VA benefits, and it's worth pushing on. Contact the VA and talk through your specific discharge. Some facilities are more flexible than others. The worst they can say is no, and many times they say yes or find another program that works for you.

Substance use history. A history of substance use does not disqualify you. Many veterans in HUD-VASH have a history of addiction or are dealing with substance use right now, and the program builds addiction counseling and treatment into case management. What the VA looks at is your willingness to work toward recovery and stability.

What Does "Chronically Homeless" Mean in VA Terms?

You may hear the term "chronically homeless" used in discussions of HUD-VASH. The VA and HUD define it as: an individual with a disability who has been homeless for at least 12 months (either continuously or in four or more episodes in the last three years). However, HUD-VASH is not limited to chronically homeless veterans. Many veterans served by the program are newly homeless or at-risk. The definition matters mainly for program statistics and funding. It doesn't decide whether you can apply.

Step 2: Contact Your VA Medical Center

HUD-VASH applications start at your local VA Medical Center (VAMC), not at a housing authority. Nothing moves until you make that first contact, so make it soon.

Call the VA National Call Center for Homeless Veterans: 1-877-424-3838. This line is available 24/7, 365 days a year. Tell them you're a veteran experiencing homelessness (or at imminent risk of it) and you want to be screened for HUD-VASH. They'll ask about your situation and what you need right now, then connect you with programs in your area. They can also tell you whether your VA Medical Center has HUD-VASH slots open right now.

Go directly to your nearest VAMC. Walk in the main entrance and ask for the Homeless Veterans Coordinator, a social worker, or the Homeless Veterans Program staff. Every VA Medical Center has staff whose job is helping homeless veterans, and you don't need an appointment for this first visit. If you're in crisis (active suicidal thoughts, extreme weather, no shelter), say so right away. They have emergency resources.

Contact a VA Community Based Outpatient Clinic (CBOC). If the VAMC is far from you, many CBOCs can start the HUD-VASH screening and referral too. Find your nearest VA facility at VA's facility locator.

Connect through a veterans service organization or shelter. Many homeless shelters, veterans shelters, and nonprofits serving veterans have VA liaisons on staff who can make the contact with you and help you through the process. If you're in a shelter now, ask a case manager or social worker there. They often work directly with the VA.

Step 3: Complete the VA Screening and SSVF (if applicable)

After you make contact, the VA does an initial screening to decide if you're eligible and how much help you need. It looks at your military service and discharge status, your current housing situation, your income, your health and behavioral health needs, substance use history, employment history, and what level of supportive services would fit you.

Be honest and complete in this screening. The more the VA knows about your situation, including past struggles with mental health or substance use, the better they can match you with the right program and support. Tell them if you've been incarcerated, have felony convictions, or live with mental illness or addiction. Nobody in that room is there to judge you.

SSVF (Supportive Services for Veteran Families). If the VA decides HUD-VASH isn't the right fit (perhaps you're not yet chronically homeless, or you have some barriers to address first), they may refer you to SSVF instead. SSVF is a bridge program that provides rapid rehousing and supportive services for veterans and veteran families experiencing homelessness. It can get you housed quickly while you work on the underlying problems, and you can move to HUD-VASH later if that makes sense. Getting sent to SSVF is not a step down.

The screener may also refer you to other veteran housing programs like GPD (Grant and Per Diem, for transitional housing) or to other VA services. Any of these can lead to stable housing.

Step 4: HUD-VASH Approval and Meeting Your Case Manager

If you're approved for HUD-VASH, you'll be assigned a VA case manager or social worker. This person is your main contact for the whole program and, in practice, your advocate.

Your case manager will:

  • Help you complete all housing voucher paperwork with the local Public Housing Authority (PHA), including income verification, family composition, and eligibility confirmation
  • Assist with your apartment search, including identifying veteran-friendly landlords and dealing with rental history or credit issues
  • Connect you with VA services you need: primary healthcare, mental health counseling, substance abuse treatment, employment assistance, benefits enrollment
  • Conduct regular check-ins once you're housed to help you maintain stability and address any issues (landlord problems, roommate conflicts, health crises) early
  • Help you with recertifications, housing moves if needed, and changes in your program

This relationship is one of the biggest advantages HUD-VASH has over standard Section 8. Your case manager may set expectations (like going to mental health appointments), but the goal is keeping you stably housed. If your case manager isn't a good fit, you can ask for a different one. Lean on this person. They are a big part of why the program works.

Step 5: Voucher Issuance from the PHA

Once the VA approves you for HUD-VASH and your case manager confirms you're ready, they refer you to the local Public Housing Authority (PHA) that runs the vouchers in your area. The PHA verifies your income and your eligibility for the voucher itself. It's a lot like standard Section 8 verification, but it usually moves faster because HUD-VASH vouchers are set aside for veterans and the VA has already screened you.

When your voucher is officially issued, you'll receive:

  • Your voucher paperwork and approval letter
  • Information about the "payment standard" for your area, which is the maximum rent the voucher will cover (this varies by apartment size and location)
  • Your share of rent (generally 30% of your adjusted income, but the PHA calculates this based on your specific income)
  • The timeline for finding housing (usually 60-120 days)
  • A list of unit inspection requirements (Housing Quality Standards, or HQS)

How HUD-VASH Differs from Regular Section 8

If you're deciding between HUD-VASH and regular Section 8, these are the main differences:

  • No waiting list: Regular Section 8 waiting lists often run for years. HUD-VASH is designed to move quickly, so you spend less time in crisis.
  • Case management included: Regular Section 8 is just the voucher, and you're on your own. HUD-VASH gives you a dedicated case manager to help with housing, services, and staying stable.
  • VA handles the referral: You don't deal with the housing authority alone. Your case manager takes you through it.
  • Access to VA services: As part of HUD-VASH, you're connected to the full VA system: healthcare, mental health, substance abuse treatment, and employment assistance.
  • Support after move-in: Regular Section 8 ends once you're housed. HUD-VASH continues to support you with regular check-ins and crisis intervention.

Step 6: Search for Housing with Your Case Manager

With your voucher in hand, you and your case manager look for a unit on the private rental market. Your case manager can help here in ways a standard PHA caseworker usually can't. They may already know landlords, know which properties are veteran-friendly and which landlords won't hold your rental history or criminal record against you, and they can go to viewings with you or negotiate with a landlord for you.

The unit you choose must meet HUD's Housing Quality Standards (HQS), the basic standards for safety, plumbing, heating, electrical systems, and sanitation. The rent also has to fall within the PHA's payment standard for the area and unit size. Your case manager and the PHA set up the inspection and lease signing. After that, the landlord gets their portion of the rent from the PHA each month.

Step 7: Move In and Maintain Your Program

Once your unit passes HQS inspection and you sign the lease, you move in. Your rent share is calculated at approximately 30% of your adjusted income (the PHA determines your exact share), with the voucher covering the rest up to the payment standard.

To maintain your HUD-VASH voucher, you need to:

  • Continue meeting with your VA case manager on the schedule you've agreed to (frequency depends on your individual plan and may decrease over time as you stabilize)
  • Participate in agreed-upon VA services (mental health appointments, substance abuse treatment, healthcare, employment assistance, or other services identified in your plan)
  • Report significant changes in income, household composition, or housing to the PHA promptly
  • Maintain the unit in good condition and comply with your lease (pay rent on time, don't damage the unit, follow house rules)
  • Stay in compliance with your lease and not engage in criminal activity

HUD-VASH has no time limit. As long as you stay eligible, take part in services, and keep your lease in good standing, you can keep your voucher indefinitely. Case management usually tapers off as things get more stable. Some veterans eventually move to a standard Section 8 voucher, which frees their HUD-VASH slot for another veteran, but there's no pressure to do that until you feel ready.

What Documents to Bring to Your First VA Appointment

To speed things up, gather these documents before your first VA appointment. If some are missing, contact the VA anyway. They will help you get what you need.

  1. DD-214 or discharge papers: proof of military service. If you don't have a copy, the VA can help you obtain one through the National Personnel Records Center (this can take 4-6 weeks).
  2. Government-issued photo ID: driver's license, state ID, passport, or military ID
  3. Proof of homelessness or housing instability: eviction notice, shelter letter, letter from someone you're staying with, or a self-declaration (you can simply state your housing situation in writing)
  4. Proof of income (if any): VA disability compensation letter, SSI/SSDI award letter, pay stubs from employment, pension statement
  5. Disability rating letter (helpful if you have one, but not required)
  6. Medical records related to service-connected conditions (helpful but not required for initial screening)

Overcoming Common Obstacles

Missing DD-214. Call the National Personnel Records Center at 1-314-801-0800 or request your DD-214 online at archives.gov. In the meantime, contact the VA with what you have (your service number, unit, dates of service). They can often start the process while the official copy is on its way.

Other-than-honorable discharge. Ask the VA directly whether your discharge status allows HUD-VASH. If it doesn't, ask what other VA benefits and programs you might qualify for. Some facilities and regions have more flexibility than others, so it's worth asking more than once or asking for a review by someone with more authority.

Substance use history or active substance use. The VA is not a police force. Their job is getting you housed and stable. If you're using and worried about admitting it, know that HUD-VASH can include substance abuse treatment as part of your case management. Many veterans in HUD-VASH have a history of addiction, and the program is set up for that. Be honest about where you are now.

Criminal history. A criminal record does not automatically disqualify you from HUD-VASH. The VA considers the nature of the offense, how long ago it was, your conduct since then, and whether you pose a safety risk. Certain violent felonies or sex offenses may create barriers, but many veterans with criminal histories are housed through HUD-VASH. Ask the VA before you decide you're ineligible.

Your Case Manager After Move-In

Your case manager's job doesn't end with the application, and it isn't to collect rent or enforce rules. If you're having trouble with your landlord, facing eviction, having a mental health crisis, using again, or struggling to get to work, tell your case manager. They have resources, can often step in quickly, and can help keep you from losing your housing.

What you tell your case manager is confidential, except where someone's safety is at risk. Your case manager won't report you to the police for past behavior. They will work with you on whatever is going wrong so you stay housed.
