Renting with Poor Rental History

Matt · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

Your rental history is one of the first things a landlord checks. But "poor rental history" means different things to different landlords, and what you think is on your record may not be what shows up when they search. A bad rental history doesn't lock you out for good. With the right information and a plan, people get past it.

What Counts as "Poor Rental History"

Landlords and screening companies look for problems with your past rentals. Some of what counts against you is obvious, and some of it may surprise you.

Evictions

An eviction is the biggest red flag on a rental record. But "eviction" can mean two different things.

A formal eviction is a court-ordered removal. The landlord filed in court, got a judgment, and you were removed or left before removal. Formal evictions show up on public court records and on tenant screening reports.

An informal eviction is when a landlord tells you to leave and you go, without a court case. It may not show up as a formal eviction. But if you ended your lease early and the landlord is unhappy, they may report it to tenant screening companies or mention it to future landlords.

A "no-cause" eviction is a different thing. It means the landlord ends your tenancy without giving a reason, usually with a written notice that the lease is ending or won't be renewed. Not every state or city allows it. If you move out by the date on the notice, there's usually no court case. If you don't, the landlord can file in court, and then it becomes a formal eviction like any other.

So what do landlords actually see? A formal eviction is on the public court record and on most tenant screening reports. With an informal one, it depends on whether the landlord reported it to a screening company, or whether a future landlord calls your old one to ask about you.

Broken Leases

If you broke your lease and left early without the landlord's permission, that's a mark on your record. Even without a formal eviction, the landlord may report it. They might also report that you owe money for the rest of the lease (though they're legally required to try to re-rent and keep your debt down).

Owing Back Rent

If you owe a previous landlord money (whether for unpaid rent or damages), that can appear on tenant screening reports, especially if it's been reported to a collection agency or the landlord pursued it through small claims court.

Damage Charges

Landlords report damage charges to screening companies. It shows up as a negative mark whether it was real damage or normal wear and tear that got called damage (which happens a lot). If the charge turned into a small claims judgment or went to collections, it's more serious.

Noise Complaints and Lease Violations

If your landlord had to contact you about noise complaints, unauthorized occupants, pets you weren't supposed to have, or other lease violations, they may have made a note of it, and a reference from them might bring it up.

What You Think Is There vs. What's Actually There

Many people assume their rental record is worse than it is. Leaving on bad terms with a landlord doesn't mean anything was formally reported, and something that happened may not show up the way you expect. The only way to know for sure is to get your own records.

What Landlords Actually Check

Most landlords now check more than one source. If you know what they'll see, you can prepare for it.

Tenant Screening Reports

Tenant screening companies (TransUnion SmartMove, RentPrep, MyRental, etc.) build databases of eviction records, payment history, and rental violations reported by landlords. They don't work like credit bureaus, so there's less consistency. Each company has its own database and may have different information about you.

What's in these reports:

  • Formal evictions (pulled from court records, vary by state)
  • Reported breaking leases or non-payment
  • Information voluntarily reported by previous landlords
  • Collection accounts related to rental debt

Eviction Records (Court Records)

Formal evictions are public record. Landlords can (and do) search your county court records online or through court repositories. If you have a formal eviction, a careful landlord will find it. How long it stays on the public record depends on the state: 7 years, longer, or permanently.

Credit Reports

Some landlords run credit checks. If a landlord sued you for unpaid rent or damages and got a judgment, that might show up on your credit report. If rental debt went to a collection agency, it will definitely show up.

Landlord References

Many landlords call your previous landlords directly. That's where the smaller details come out: noise complaints, lease violations, how you treated the place, whether they'd rent to you again. Previous landlords don't have to be honest, but most are. If you left on bad terms, expect the reference to say so.

Get Your Own Records First

Before you apply anywhere, find out what's actually on record about you. You'll know what you're dealing with, and you'll be able to dispute anything that's wrong.

Pull Your Credit Report

Go to annualcreditreport.com and get your free annual credit report from all three bureaus (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion). Look for collections accounts, judgments, or late payments related to rental housing.

If you see inaccurate information, you have the right to dispute it. Contact the credit bureau in writing and send them documentation of the error (for example, a letter from your landlord saying the debt was settled, or proof that you paid).

Check Your County Court Records

Search your county courthouse online (most counties now have searchable databases) to see if there are any eviction judgments, small claims judgments, or other housing-related lawsuits against you.

Request Your Tenant Screening Report

You have the right under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) to request your tenant screening report for free. Contact the screening companies:

  • TransUnion SmartMove
  • RentPrep
  • RentSpree

Each company has a process for requesting your report (usually through their website). Get your report and check for errors.

Dispute Errors

If you find inaccurate information on your credit report or tenant screening report, you have the right to dispute it under the FCRA. Send a written dispute to the agency explaining the error and include any documentation (letters from your landlord, receipts, etc.). The agency must investigate and correct errors within 30 days.

Evictions on Your Record

An eviction is serious, but it doesn't last forever, and the law gives you some protection.

How Long Do They Stay?

On tenant screening reports: Typically 7 years from the date of the eviction.

On court records: This varies by state. Some states purge court records after a certain period (7-10 years); others keep them permanently. Even if they're "purged," they may still be findable through archive systems.

Sealing or Expunging Eviction Records

Some states allow you to seal or expunge eviction records, especially if:

  • You were wrongfully evicted
  • You won an eviction case (the eviction was dismissed or you won at trial)
  • The eviction happened long ago and you've since had stable housing
  • You're a victim of domestic violence and the eviction was related to your abuse

Sealing or expunging an eviction record removes it from public view and from most tenant screening reports. That's a big deal. Check your state's rules with a legal aid attorney or your county court.

If You Were Wrongfully Evicted

If you were evicted illegally (the landlord didn't follow proper procedures, retaliated against you for reporting code violations, violated fair housing laws, etc.), consult a legal aid attorney immediately. You may have a case to overturn the eviction or seal the record.

If You Owe Money to a Previous Landlord

Owing money is a serious problem, but it can often be fixed.

Negotiate a Settlement

Contact your former landlord and offer to settle. Many landlords will take a partial payment to close the account. Even if you can't pay it all, offering a settlement shows you're serious about clearing the debt.

Get a Written Settlement Agreement

Once you agree on a settlement, get it in writing. The agreement should say that once you pay, the landlord considers the debt settled. Keep it with your records.

Get a Letter Confirming Resolution

After you pay, ask the landlord for a letter saying the debt is resolved, and include it when you apply for housing. It shows you took responsibility and cleared it up.

Ask About Neutral References

Some landlords will give a "neutral" reference after you pay: not glowing, but not negative either. Ask: "Now that we've settled this debt, would you be willing to give me a neutral reference to future landlords?" Some will; some won't.

Building a Positive Record Going Forward

Landlords care more about your recent history than about what happened years ago, so what you do from here counts.

Pay Current Rent On Time

If you're renting now, pay on time, every time. Nothing else you can do matters as much. Look into rent reporting services that report your on-time payments to credit bureaus and tenant screening companies.

Rent reporting services:

  • RentReporters
  • Esusu
  • RentTrack

These services can send your rent payments to the credit bureaus. That builds positive credit history and gives future landlords proof that you pay.

Keep a Rental Ledger

Keep a record of every rent payment: photos of checks, bank statements, receipt confirmations, etc. Write down that you paid, when, and how much. If there's ever a dispute, that record is your proof.

Be a Good Neighbor

Stay clear of noise complaints and lease violations. When you need a landlord reference down the road, you want your current landlord to have only good things to say.

Get Written References

Ask your current landlord (or past landlords who had a good experience with you) for a written reference letter. Ask them to say that you were reliable and that you took care of the property and got along with neighbors. Use these letters in future applications.

Proving Good Rental History When a Landlord Won't Write a Reference

In most places, a landlord doesn't have to write or sign a reference letter, even if you always paid on time. A past landlord saying no doesn't mean you have no proof. First, ask the landlord or program that wants the reference: "My last landlord won't sign a reference letter. What other proof of rental history do you accept?" Then gather what they name. These usually work:

  • A rent ledger or portal history. If you paid through an online portal or app, download or print your payment history. You can also ask the management office for a printout of your ledger. Some landlords will give a ledger even when they won't write a letter.
  • Bank or money order records. Bank statements that show rent checks or transfers, canceled checks, or money order receipts made out to the landlord. Black out account numbers before you share them.
  • A letter from your housing authority. If you rented with a Section 8 voucher, ask the housing authority for a letter confirming your address and the dates of your assisted tenancy. HUD's rules say the housing authority must give a new voucher landlord your current and prior addresses and your landlords' names, and may share other information about your tenancy history under a written policy that applies the same way to every family (24 CFR 982.307(b)). If you lived in public housing, the housing authority was your landlord, so ask it for your rent ledger.
  • Utility payment history. A record of on-time electric, gas, or water bills in your name shows you pay your bills. Ask the utility company for a payment history.
  • Rent-reporting records. If a rent-reporting service sent your rent payments to the credit bureaus, they may show on your credit report. The CFPB says "positive rental payments can help build your credit," and suggests asking your landlord whether it uses a rental reporting program. Check for fees first (CFPB).
  • Other references. An earlier landlord, a caseworker, a housing counselor, shelter or program staff, or an employer who can speak to how reliable you are.

Put it together in a short packet: one page listing each address, your move-in and move-out dates, and the rent, followed by your proof. If a landlord still says no only because you have no reference, ask whether a co-signer or other steps below would change the answer.

Strategies for Getting Approved Despite Poor Rental History

These steps can improve your chances.

Be Upfront About Your History

Don't hide it. If you know you had an eviction, broken lease, or other issue, write a short explanation letter (one paragraph at most) to hand landlords up front. Own the mistake, say what happened, and show what's different now.

Example: "I had a difficult period in 2021 that resulted in an eviction, due to job loss. Since then, I've been employed steadily for three years and have been renting successfully for the past two years. I take my housing stability seriously and am committed to being a reliable tenant."

Being straight with a landlord goes a long way. They'd rather hear it from you than find it in a background check.

Offer a Larger Security Deposit

Offer to pay a larger security deposit. Many landlords will loosen their screening if you're putting down extra money. Check your state's rules on maximum security deposit amounts first. If you need help paying a deposit, see our guide to help with move-in costs.

Provide Personal References

Go beyond your landlord references. Provide references from:

  • Your employer (showing employment stability)
  • A case manager or social worker (showing you're engaged with support)
  • A religious leader or community mentor (showing community connection)
  • Someone who can vouch for your character and reliability

Find a Co-Signer

A co-signer (usually a family member or close friend with good credit and income) agrees to pay the rent if you don't. Landlords like that, and it improves your chances a lot. Make sure your co-signer understands what they're signing up for before they agree.

Look for Smaller, Independent Landlords

Big management companies lean on automated screening and are more likely to reject you over your history. A small, independent landlord is more willing to hear your story and make an exception for the right tenant.

Not All "Poor Rental History" Is the Same

A single eviction five years ago when you were homeless is different from three recent evictions. A broken lease due to domestic violence is different from breaking a lease to move closer to a boyfriend. Many landlords will weigh your circumstances if you explain them clearly.

Programs That Can Help

If private landlords keep turning you down, look at subsidized and nonprofit housing. Their screening is often more flexible.

Section 8 (Housing Choice Vouchers)

Federal Section 8 rules leave most rental-history screening up to each PHA (Public Housing Authority), and the PHA's written policy (its administrative plan) sets the rules. Federal rules let a PHA deny you for things like a household member being evicted from federally assisted housing in the last five years, or your family owing money to a PHA, but they don't require it (24 CFR 982.552(c)(1)). One rule is mandatory: the PHA must deny admission for three years from the date a household member was evicted from federally assisted housing for drug-related criminal activity, unless that person finished an approved, supervised drug treatment program or the circumstances no longer exist (24 CFR 982.553(a)(1)(i)). And once you have a voucher, the landlord does its own screening; the PHA doesn't do it for them (24 CFR 982.307(a)). Some PHAs are more lenient than others, so you may qualify even with a poor rental history. See our guide on How to Apply for Section 8.

Public Housing

Like Section 8, public housing leaves most rental history screening to the PHA, and some PHAs are more lenient. The same three-year bar after a drug-related eviction from federally assisted housing applies (24 CFR 960.204(a)(1)). Apply anyway.

LIHTC Properties (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit)

LIHTC properties are privately owned but get tax benefits for providing affordable housing. Each property sets its own screening policy. Some are strict; others barely look at rental history. See our guide on How to Find LIHTC Housing.

Nonprofit Housing Providers

Nonprofit housing groups usually care more about serving people who need housing than about a spotless screening report. They may be more willing to work with you despite a poor rental history, especially if you're working with a support program.

Rapid Rehousing Programs

If you're homeless right now (in a shelter, on the street, in a car, or somewhere else not meant for people to live), rapid rehousing programs help you move into a place quickly. They usually screen very lightly and give you support to stay housed. Federal rapid rehousing money generally requires that kind of homelessness, so if you still have a place but could lose it, ask about homelessness prevention programs instead. Ask your case manager or contact 211 to find programs in your area.

Transitional Housing as a Stepping Stone

If your rental history is keeping you from getting a lease, transitional housing (usually 6-24 months with support services) can be a stepping stone. You get stable housing and a case manager, and you build the positive rental history that makes you eligible for permanent housing later. It's also a chance to put a clean recent record behind you and collect good landlord references.

Your Rights as a Renter with Poor History

Fair Housing Protections

A landlord can consider your rental history, but they cannot use it in a way that discriminates against you based on:

  • Race, color, or national origin
  • Religion
  • Sex
  • Disability
  • Familial status (having children under 18 in the home, which also covers being pregnant)
  • Sexual orientation or gender identity (in many jurisdictions)

For example: A landlord cannot deny you because you're Black and landlords have higher eviction rates in Black neighborhoods. They cannot deny you because you're a woman and women experience more housing instability. If you believe you're facing discrimination, consult a legal aid attorney.

FCRA Protections for Tenant Screening Reports

If a landlord denies you based on a tenant screening report, they must:

  • Tell you they ran a screening report
  • Give you the name and contact of the screening company
  • Let you know you can dispute the report if it's inaccurate

You have the right to dispute errors on your screening report (contact the screening company in writing). You also have the right to request a free copy of your screening report from the company that provided it.

"Fair Chance" Housing Ordinances

Some states and cities have "fair chance" housing ordinances that limit how far back landlords can look at rental history (e.g., only the past 5 years) or require them to consider context and rehabilitation. Check if your state or city has one.

When a Landlord Says No

Ask for the Reason in Writing

If you're denied, ask the landlord for the reason in writing. Depending on your state, they may be required to give it. The reason tells you whether to appeal, try something else, or move on to another property.

Check If It Violates Fair Housing Laws

If the reason for denial is based on your race, disability, familial status, or another protected class, it's illegal. If it's based on information in a screening report, you have the right to know that information and dispute it if inaccurate.

Appeal If It's a Subsidized Property

If you're denied for Section 8 or public housing, you have the right to an informal hearing to appeal. Contact the PHA about their appeal process. You can present your case, answer questions, and provide documentation of your rehabilitation or changed circumstances.

Try a Different Approach

If one approach didn't work, try another: offer a co-signer or a larger deposit, or find a landlord who might be more flexible. You only need one who will work with you.

Special Situations

First-Time Renters with No History

If you've never rented, you have no rental history, good or bad, and many landlords hesitate over first-time renters. What helps: a co-signer, a larger deposit, personal references from an employer or mentor, or looking for landlords who specifically welcome first-time renters.

Returning from Incarceration

See our guide on Criminal Records and Housing. You have the right to individualized assessment, and many programs (Section 8, LIHTC, nonprofit housing) are willing to work with people returning from incarceration, especially if you're engaged in reentry programs or have a case manager.

Leaving Domestic Violence

If you're leaving domestic violence, the law may protect you. Some states let you break a lease without penalty to get away from an abuser, and some let you seal an eviction record if the eviction was tied to the abuse. A domestic violence shelter or legal aid attorney can tell you what applies to you.

Aging Out of Foster Care

Many people aging out of foster care have no rental history and no co-signers. Programs like the John H. Chafee Foster Care Program for Successful Transition to Adulthood (usually just called Chafee) or housing agencies that work with youth aging out may help. Talk to your social worker or contact a housing counselor about programs for young adults.

Getting Help

Housing Counselors

A HUD-certified housing counselor can go over your options with you, help you dispute errors on your screening reports, put together your application materials, and work out how to present yourself to landlords. Many charge nothing or very little.

Find a counselor: HUD's Housing Counselor Locator or call 211.

Legal Aid

If you've been wrongfully denied housing due to inaccurate information, discrimination, or other legal violations, legal aid attorneys can help you dispute denials or file complaints.

Contact: lawhelp.org or your local legal aid office.

Tenant Rights Organizations

Local tenant unions and tenant rights groups can explain your protections, help you push back on unfair practices, and help you deal with the local rental market. Search "[your city] tenant rights" or "[your state] tenant union."

Quick Recap

  • "Poor rental history" includes evictions, broken leases, unpaid rent, damage charges, and lease violations, but what's actually on record might be different from what you think.
  • Pull your credit report, check court records, and request your tenant screening report to know what landlords will see.
  • Evictions can be sealed or expunged in some states if you were wrongfully evicted or meet other criteria.
  • If you owe a previous landlord, negotiate a settlement and get written confirmation of resolution.
  • Build a positive record going forward by paying rent on time, using rent reporting services, and maintaining good neighbor relations.
  • Strategies for approval: be upfront about your history, offer a larger deposit, provide personal references, find a co-signer, look for independent landlords.
  • Consider subsidized housing (Section 8, LIHTC, nonprofit housing) which may have more flexible screening.
  • You have legal protections against discrimination and the right to dispute inaccuracies on screening reports.
  • Don't apply to just one landlord. Apply to multiple properties and programs to find ones that will work with you.
