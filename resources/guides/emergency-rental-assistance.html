Emergency Rent Help Near You: Find Your City's Program

Matt · Updated November 2026

If you're behind on rent, the help that still exists is local: a city or county program, a nonprofit, a church fund. There is no national rent program to apply to anymore. The federal Emergency Rental Assistance money from the pandemic is gone (more on that below). This page gives you the steps to take this week, then a list of our local guides by state. Each guide says who actually pays rent in that city, how to apply, and what's open right now.

What to do this week if you're behind on rent

  1. Call 211. Dial 211 or use 211.org. Tell them your county, how much you owe, and whether you have a notice or court papers. Ask for every rent program that's open, not just one, and write down each name and number.
  2. Tell your landlord in writing. A text or email is fine if that's how you two usually talk. Keep a copy. Say how much you can pay now, when you can pay the rest, and that you've applied for help. Something like: "I'm behind because I lost hours at work. I applied to [program] on [date]. I can pay $[amount] on [date]. Can we set up a payment plan for the rest?"
  3. Apply to your city's program. Find your city below. Apply to every program that serves your address. Most run on limited money and close when it's spent, so the first program you try may say no.
  4. If you have court papers, get free legal help now. A summons, complaint or eviction filing comes with a deadline. Read how to get free legal help as a tenant, call today, and go to every hearing. For the full plan, see how to avoid eviction.

If you could be without a place to sleep before help comes, go to emergency housing tonight.

If your city isn't listed

Most of the country isn't a big city, and help still exists there. It's usually run by the county or a regional agency. Try these, in this order:

  • 211. Call 211 or use 211.org and give your county name. In a small county, 211 is often the only place that knows every fund.
  • Your state's housing finance agency or rental assistance program. Open your state's page. It lists the state housing finance agency and any statewide rent or eviction program.
  • Your local Community Action Agency. These local anti-poverty agencies cover rural counties too, and many have a small emergency fund for rent or utilities. Use the Community Action Partnership's Find a CAP tool to search by ZIP code, county or state.
  • Your local housing authority. Housing authorities usually don't hand out emergency rent money, but they run Section 8 and public housing, and some know which local funds are open. Use our guide to find your PHA. If you already have a voucher and your income dropped, ask your PHA for an interim recertification right away so your share of the rent is recalculated.
  • Charities and congregations. The Salvation Army, Catholic Charities, St. Vincent de Paul, United Way and local churches, mosques and synagogues often have small rent funds. Call several. Each may cover only part of what you owe, so stack them.
  • Your utility company. If the utility bill is what put you behind, ask about hardship or payment programs.

What happened to federal Emergency Rental Assistance

Federal Emergency Rental Assistance (ERA) was the pandemic program. Treasury sent states, local governments and tribes $25 billion in the first round (ERA1) and $21.55 billion in the second (ERA2). It's over. Treasury says the ERA2 period of performance ended September 30, 2025, and that grantees may no longer use ERA2 money to help renters (Treasury ERA page, checked November 18, 2026).

If a website still tells you to "apply for ERA," it's either out of date or it's a local program that kept the name. The programs still running are paid for by cities, counties, states and charities. They open and close as money comes and goes, which is why our city guides say what's open now.

How local rent programs usually work

  • Who qualifies: Most programs cap income at a share of Area Median Income (AMI), often 50% or 80%. You'll usually need to be behind on rent or utilities (or about to be), have a lease or rental agreement, and have a hardship such as a job loss, cut hours, or medical bills. Don't rule yourself out. Apply and let the program decide.
  • Where the money goes: To your landlord or the utility company, not to you. Most programs are grants.
  • What they pay: Back rent first. Some also pay past-due utilities, a month or two of future rent, or (rarely) a deposit to move. Many cap the amount per household. Check your program's limit.
  • How long it takes: Weeks, not days. An initial review often takes 2 to 4 weeks, and the whole process can take 6 to 12 weeks. Answer requests for documents fast, because that's where most delays happen.
  • Follow up: Get a confirmation number when you apply. Call every 2 to 3 weeks and ask, "What's the status of my application, and do you need anything else from me?"
  • If you have a court date: Bring proof that you applied. Some landlords and judges will wait for a pending payment. None have to, so don't skip the hearing.
  • Immigration status: Rules differ by program. Ask the program before you assume you're shut out.

Documents to gather now

Programs vary, but most ask for the same things. Start collecting them before you apply:

  • Proof of identity: A government-issued photo ID (driver's license, state ID, passport).
  • Proof of income (or no income): Recent pay stubs (usually the last 30 days), last year's tax return, or a letter from your employer. If you're unemployed or self-employed: bank statements, unemployment or benefits letters, or a short written statement explaining your income.
  • Your lease and rent amount: A current lease or rental agreement, your landlord's name and contact information, and something showing your monthly rent.
  • Proof of what you owe: A late notice, eviction notice, "pay or quit" notice, landlord letter, or a rent ledger. If there's no formal notice, texts or emails with your landlord about the balance help.
  • Who lives with you: Birth certificates or school records for children. Programs use household size to set your income limit.
  • Proof of the hardship: A layoff letter, medical bills, or a short letter in your own words about what happened.
  • Utility bills: Recent bills, if you want past-due utilities covered.

Copies are fine. If you're missing something, ask whether a signed statement will do. Send everything at once if you can, and keep a copy of all of it.

If you're denied

  • Ask for the reason in writing. Common reasons are income over the limit, missing documents, or the program running out of money.
  • Ask for reconsideration. If you were denied for missing documents, send them and ask the program to look again.
  • Apply to other programs. Different programs have different limits and rules, so one denial doesn't settle it.
  • Check your household size. Income limits go up with household size. If you left someone off the application, adding them raises the limit that applies to you.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get emergency rent help near me?

Find your state and city in the list on this page and open the guide. Each one names the programs that pay rent there, how to apply, and what's open now. If your city isn't listed, call 211 and ask for rent help in your county, then check your state's page for the state housing finance agency and contact your local Community Action Agency. Apply to every program that serves your address, not just one.

Can I get help paying rent before I'm evicted?

Yes, and that's the best time to ask. Many local programs help renters who are behind but haven't been to court, and some only help once you have a notice. Apply as soon as you miss a payment and tell your landlord in writing that you applied. If you get court papers, contact free legal aid right away, go to every hearing, and bring proof of any pending application.

What documents do I need for emergency rent help?

Usually a photo ID, proof of income for everyone in the household (pay stubs, benefits letters, or a written statement if you have no income), your lease, proof of what you owe (a late notice, eviction notice, or rent ledger), your landlord's name and contact information, and any utility bills you want covered. Copies are fine. If you're missing something, ask whether a signed statement will do.

Is there emergency rent help if I'm not in a big city?

Usually, but it's run by the county or a regional agency instead of a city. Call 211 and give your county name. Community Action Agencies cover rural counties too, and the Community Action Partnership's Find a CAP tool will give you yours. Your state's page lists the state housing finance agency and any statewide program. Churches, the Salvation Army and Catholic Charities also help in many small towns.

Is federal Emergency Rental Assistance (ERA) still available?

No. The federal ERA program is over. Treasury says ERA2 grantees may no longer use that money to help renters, and the program's period of performance ended September 30, 2025. Some local programs still use the ERA name, but their money now comes from cities, counties, states and charities.

Do I have to pay emergency rent help back?

Usually not. Most programs pay your landlord directly as a grant. Some charities and a few local programs make loans instead, so ask before you sign anything.
