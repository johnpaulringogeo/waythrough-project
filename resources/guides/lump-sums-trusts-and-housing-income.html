Lump Sums, Trusts, and One-Time Payments: How They Count for Section 8 and Public Housing

Matt · Updated November 2026

If you have Section 8 or live in public housing and you get an inheritance, a settlement, Social Security back pay, or money from a family trust, you may worry about your rent or your home. Most one-time money does not count as income. But money you keep can count as an asset. Large assets can add "imputed" income to your rent math, and very large assets can make you ineligible. HUD changed these rules under a law called HOTMA, and it will enforce them at most housing authorities starting January 1, 2027.

Here's the short version: One-time lump sums, SSI and Social Security back pay, and insurance payouts are generally not income. Whatever you keep counts toward your assets. Payments from a trust you don't control are not income if they come from the trust's principal, but they are income if they come from what the trust earned. In 2027, imputed income starts when net assets are over $54,898, and the asset limit is $109,797. Report changes the way your housing authority requires. Before you take a trust payment, ask the housing authority or owner in writing how they will count it.

Which rules does your housing authority use?

Under Notice PIH 2026-15, HUD will enforce HOTMA sections 102 and 104 at housing authorities starting January 1, 2027. Moving to Work agencies and those using only HUD's Family Reporting Software get a later date. Before an agency switches, older rules can apply, and some were very different: HUD says it used to count all trust payments as income. Privately owned HUD-assisted buildings use the same rules in 24 CFR part 5, but ask the owner which version it uses. This guide describes the HOTMA rules.

Income vs. assets: the basic idea

Income is money you expect to get in the coming year, like wages or Social Security (24 CFR 5.609). Your rent is mostly based on it. Assets are what you own, like savings, stocks, or land, valued at their "net cash value" (24 CFR 5.603). Assets matter in three ways:

  • Real earnings on assets, like bank interest, count as income.
  • If your net assets are over a set amount, the housing authority adds "imputed" income for assets where real earnings can't be figured out.
  • If your assets are over the asset limit, you can lose eligibility.

Inheritances: the rule excludes "lump-sum additions to net family assets, including but not limited to lottery or other contest winnings." A one-time cash inheritance fits that description, but the rule doesn't name it, so ask your housing authority to confirm in writing. Inheriting a house is different. It can count under the real property rule below.

Watch for patterns. In HUD's guidance, a second online fundraiser in two years counted as income because it showed a pattern. Money that keeps coming is income.

SSI and Social Security back pay

HUD excludes "deferred periodic amounts from Supplemental Security Income and Social Security benefits," paid in one lump sum or in monthly catch-up payments, and deferred VA disability benefits (5.609(b)(16)). Your regular monthly benefit still counts. Back pay you keep in the bank counts toward your assets. SSI has its own rules about saving back pay, so ask Social Security.

Trusts: principal vs. what the trust earns

How a trust counts depends on who controls it and where each payment comes from. HUD's HOTMA guidance (Notice PIH 2023-27, section F.4.d) explains it like this:

  • Irrevocable trust, or a revocable trust you don't control (someone outside your household set it up, and no one in your household is the trustee): the trust's value is not your asset (5.603(b)(4)). Payments from the trust's principal are not income. Payments of what the trust earned, like interest, dividends, or gains, are income, unless they pay health or medical costs for a minor (5.609(b)(2)(i)). Earnings the trust keeps don't count.
  • Revocable trust you control (you or someone in your household set it up, is the trustee, or otherwise controls it): the trust's value is your asset. What the trust earns counts as income even if it isn't paid out, and payments to you are not counted again as income (5.609(b)(2)(ii)).

HUD tells housing authorities to be careful to tell principal apart from earnings. Ask the trustee for a written statement for each payment showing how much came from principal and how much from trust income.

Example: Ruth is 77 and has a voucher. Her late father left an irrevocable trust, and she wants to ask the trustee for $1,650 a month for 22 months. If her housing authority uses the HOTMA rules and the trustee pays her from principal, those payments aren't income, and her rent stays based on her Social Security and wages. If the payments come from the trust's earnings, they count, and her rent could rise by about $495 a month (30% of $1,650). Either way, money she saves from the payments becomes her asset. Ruth should get the housing authority's answer in writing first and talk with a tax professional.

Imputed income when assets are over the threshold

If your net family assets are over $54,898 in 2027 ($52,787 in 2026), the housing authority must add imputed income for any asset where it can't figure out real earnings. It multiplies that asset's value by HUD's passbook rate: 0.38% in 2027 (0.4% in 2026) (5.609(a)(2); HUD's 2027 values). At or below the threshold, there is no imputed income (5.609(b)(1)). For example, if a family is over the threshold and owns a vacant lot worth $20,000, it adds $76 a year in imputed income.

If your assets are at or below $54,898, the housing authority can accept your signed statement instead of bank records, but it must check with third parties at least every three years (24 CFR 5.618(b); 982.516).

The asset limit

Under 24 CFR 5.618, a family can be denied or lose assistance, at move-in or at a reexamination, if:

  • its net family assets are over $109,797 in 2027 (the rule's $100,000, adjusted each year), or
  • it owns a home it could live in and has the legal right to sell. Exceptions include a voucher homeownership home, a home a co-owner outside your household lives in, survivors of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking, and a home that is for sale.

Retirement accounts, ABLE accounts, 529 and Coverdell education accounts, and trusts you don't control don't count toward net family assets (5.603(b)). Giving assets away for less than they're worth doesn't get around the limit. The housing authority counts anything you gave away in the past two years. After two years, it no longer counts (5.603(b)(2)).

If you're over the limit: a housing authority or owner can adopt a policy not to enforce the limit at recertification, or to make exceptions (5.618(c)). It can also wait up to six months before starting termination or eviction (5.618(d)). Ask in writing whether it has a policy and how long you have. Use that time to talk to legal aid. After that period ends, the agency can start termination. Section 8 families can ask for an informal hearing (24 CFR 982.555). The notice must state the deadline to ask. If you miss it, ask in writing anyway and call legal aid.

The public housing over-income rule

This applies to public housing only, not vouchers. Under 24 CFR 960.507, the over-income limit is 2.4 times the very low-income limit, about 120% of the area median income. The housing authority must notify you in writing within 30 days of finding you over the limit, and check again at 12 and 24 months. If you're still over after 24 months in a row, it must either charge a higher alternative rent under a new lease or end your tenancy within six months of the 24-month notice. You can ask for a hearing if you disagree. If your income drops below the limit, the clock starts over. Lump sums aren't income, so they usually don't count here. Regular payments of trust earnings can.

With a voucher, higher income raises your share of the rent instead. See My Income Went Up on Section 8. Will I Lose My Voucher?

What you must report, and when

  • Annual reexamination: the housing authority reviews your income and your household at least once a year (982.516; 960.257 for public housing). Be ready to list your assets, any lump sums, and any trust payments.
  • Between reexaminations: each housing authority must have written rules on when you report changes. Look in your lease, your voucher papers, or the agency's Administrative Plan. The agency must do an interim reexamination when your adjusted income will go up 10% or more. Raises in earned income usually wait until your next annual review.
  • Report even money you think is excluded. Let the housing authority decide, and keep copies. If you report late, a rent increase can be charged back to the month after the change (982.516). See how recertification works.

Before you take a payment: ask in writing

  1. Get papers that show the source and type of money, like the trust's type (revocable or irrevocable), who the trustee is, and whether payments come from principal or income.
  2. Send your housing authority or owner a letter like the one below. Ask for a written answer.
  3. Talk to a tax professional. If you get SSI, Medicaid, or SNAP, check those programs' rules too, because they're different. Our guide on how benefits interact can help.

[Date]

To: [Housing authority or property manager]

Re: How a payment would count. [Your name], [address or voucher number]

I may receive [describe it: an inheritance of about $___ / a settlement / SSI back pay / monthly payments of $___ from an irrevocable trust set up by ___, paid from the trust's principal]. Before I accept it, please tell me in writing:

1. Whether you would count it as income, as an asset, or neither, and the rule you rely on.
2. Whether you are using the HOTMA rules in 24 CFR 5.603 and 5.609 for my next rent calculation.
3. How it would change my rent, and when I would need to report it.

I've attached [trust papers / award letter / settlement letter]. You can reach me at [phone] or [email].

Sincerely,
[Your name]

If the answer isn't what you expected

Ask the housing authority to show you the rule and the math in writing. If your rent goes up or your help is ended, ask for an informal hearing (Section 8) or use the grievance process in your public housing lease, before the deadline in your notice. And call legal aid; our free legal help guide shows how to find one.

Frequently asked questions

Does an inheritance count as income for Section 8?

Under HUD's HOTMA rules, lump-sum additions to assets are not income. A one-time cash inheritance generally fits that rule, but ask your housing authority to confirm in writing. Money you keep counts as an asset.

Does SSI or Social Security back pay count as income?

No. HUD excludes deferred SSI and Social Security amounts, whether paid in one lump sum or in monthly catch-up payments. Your regular monthly benefit still counts. Back pay you keep in the bank counts toward your net family assets.

Do trust distributions count as income in HUD housing?

It depends on the trust. For an irrevocable trust or a revocable trust you don't control, payments from the trust's principal are not income, but payments of what the trust earned are income. For a revocable trust you control, the trust is your asset and its earnings count as income.

What is the asset limit for Section 8 and public housing in 2027?

A family can be denied or lose assistance if its net family assets are over $109,797 in 2027, or if it owns a home it could live in, with some exceptions. Retirement accounts don't count. A housing authority can wait up to six months to act.

What is imputed income on assets?

If your net family assets are over $54,898 in 2027, the housing authority adds imputed income for assets where it can't figure out real earnings. It multiplies the asset's value by HUD's passbook rate, which is 0.38% in 2027. At or below that amount, there is no imputed income.

Do I have to report a lump sum to my housing authority?

Follow your housing authority's written reporting rules. It is safest to report any lump sum or new trust payment in writing and let the agency decide how it counts. If you report late, a rent increase can be charged back.
