How to Request a Reasonable Accommodation

Matt · Updated May 2026

If you have a disability and need a change to your housing rules, policies, or physical space to have equal access to your home, you have the right to ask for a "reasonable accommodation" under the Fair Housing Act and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. The right applies in private market housing, public housing, the Section 8 voucher program, and any federally-assisted housing program. It is how people get an animal allowed in a no-pets building, a parking spot near the door, or more time on a voucher search.

What to do: Write a letter to your landlord or housing authority that states your disability-related need and the exact change you're asking for. Include a letter from your doctor or therapist confirming the need. Your landlord must respond and cannot charge extra fees. If denied, you can file a fair housing complaint. Common accommodations include emotional support animals, reserved parking, grab bars, and policy exceptions.

What the law requires

A reasonable accommodation is a change to a rule, policy, practice, or service that allows a person with a disability to have equal use and enjoyment of their home and housing. The Fair Housing Act and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act require housing providers to make these changes unless doing so would create an undue burden or fundamentally alter the nature of the program.

People with disabilities sometimes need an exception to a standard policy to live in their housing on the same terms as everyone else. The goal is equal access. A "no pets" rule applies to everyone equally, for example, but if it keeps someone with a service animal from living there, it discriminates, because it puts up a barrier based on disability.

Examples of reasonable accommodations

Reasonable accommodations fall into two main categories: rule changes and physical modifications.

Rule changes and policy exceptions. These are requests to waive or modify a standard housing policy because it creates a barrier due to disability. Examples include:

  • Waiving a "no pets" policy to allow an emotional support animal (ESA) or service animal
  • Allowing a service animal or ESA in properties that claim "no animals"
  • Allowing a tenant with a mobility limitation to have a reserved, accessible parking spot
  • Permitting a live-in personal care attendant or aide when the lease normally limits occupants to family members only
  • Allowing a tenant with a visual impairment to receive lease notices via email instead of posted notices
  • Extending a housing search deadline for a tenant with a disability that makes apartment hunting more difficult (e.g., chronic fatigue, mobility limitations)
  • Allowing flexible hours for housing inspections for someone with a health condition
  • Waiving a "no smoking" policy for a tenant with a respiratory condition who needs to use a certain medical device
  • Allowing a tenant with a psychiatric disability to have a roommate for emotional support despite an "individual occupancy" rule

Physical modifications. These are changes to the physical space of the unit or building to make it accessible or usable for someone with a disability. Examples include:

  • Installing grab bars in the bathroom
  • Widening doorways or hallways for wheelchair accessibility
  • Installing a ramp at an entrance
  • Lowering light switches or thermostat controls
  • Installing an accessible shower or walk-in bathtub
  • Modifying a kitchen for accessibility (lowering counters, adjusting appliances)
  • Installing visual alarm systems for someone who is deaf or hard of hearing
  • Providing accessible parking spaces

The change has to be necessary because of the disability, and it has to be "reasonable," meaning it doesn't impose an undue financial or administrative burden on the housing provider or fundamentally alter the nature of the housing program. What counts as reasonable depends on the situation. Grab bars usually are. A new parking garage is not.

Who counts as having a disability

The Fair Housing Act defines disability broadly. You can request a reasonable accommodation if you have a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. Major life activities include walking, seeing, hearing, learning, working, caring for yourself, communicating, thinking, concentrating, and more.

This includes, but is not limited to:

  • Mobility impairments: Conditions affecting walking, standing, or movement, such as arthritis, cerebral palsy, spinal cord injuries, stroke, MS, Parkinson's disease, etc.
  • Visual or hearing impairments: Blindness, low vision, deafness, hard of hearing
  • Chronic illnesses: Fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, lupus, HIV/AIDS, diabetes, kidney disease, cancer, etc.
  • Mental health conditions: Depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, OCD, panic disorder, etc.
  • Intellectual and developmental disabilities: Down syndrome, autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disabilities
  • Substance use disorders in recovery: If you're in recovery from addiction, you're protected under fair housing law. This includes both active treatment (like medication-assisted therapy) and sustained recovery.
  • Hidden or invisible disabilities: Chronic pain, traumatic brain injury, learning disabilities. The disability doesn't have to be visible to qualify

You do not need to be receiving disability benefits (SSI, SSDI, VA disability compensation) to have a disability under fair housing law. You also don't need a specific diagnosis. What matters is the functional limitation, and you can request an accommodation based on that alone.

Recovery from substance use is protected. If you have a history of substance use and are in recovery, through treatment, medication, peer support, or on your own, you can request accommodations. People in recovery from addiction are covered as people with disabilities, and housing providers cannot discriminate based on recovery status.

Step 1: Identify What You Need

Before you ask, pin down the exact change you need and how it ties to your disability. A specific request is much easier to approve than a vague one.

Ask yourself these questions:

  • What is the barrier? What rule, policy, or physical space prevents me from having equal use of my home?
  • What is my disability-related functional limitation? (Not the diagnosis, but the actual limitation: e.g., "difficulty walking long distances," "severe anxiety in crowded spaces," "require assistance with daily living")
  • What specific change would address that barrier? (Be concrete)
  • Why does this accommodation relate to my disability? (How does the accommodation address my functional limitation?)

For example: "Due to my mobility limitation, I walk with a cane and cannot safely use stairs. I need a reserved parking space near the building entrance to reduce the distance I must walk and the risk of falling on stairs. I also need the property to modify my assigned parking spot placement from the back lot to the front near the accessible entrance."

Another example: "I have severe PTSD with panic disorder. Noise from hallway traffic and neighbors triggers panic attacks, which interfere with my ability to eat, sleep, and function. I request a unit on the end of the building or upper floor away from high-traffic areas. I also request flexibility in receiving lease notices via email instead of door-to-door notices, as unexpected knocks can trigger panic episodes."

Both examples describe the functional limitation (mobility issues, panic attacks) and tie it to a specific request. Neither names an exact diagnosis. What they make clear is the link between the disability and the accommodation.

Step 2: Make Your Request in Writing

You can make a reasonable accommodation request verbally, but always follow up in writing. A written request gives you a dated record and makes it much harder for the housing provider to claim they never got it or didn't understand it.

Send your request in writing by email (if possible, to create a record), certified mail, or hand-delivery with a signed receipt. Keep copies of everything.

Your letter or email should include:

  1. Your name, address, and unit number (if applicable)
  2. A clear statement that you are requesting a reasonable accommodation under the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604) and/or Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (29 U.S.C. § 794)
  3. A description of the specific accommodation you need (say exactly what help or modification you want; "I need help" is not enough)
  4. An explanation of how the accommodation is connected to your disability-related functional limitation (you can and should describe functional limitations without naming your diagnosis)
  5. A statement that you are willing to provide documentation from a qualified professional if requested
  6. A request for a response within a reasonable timeframe (14 days is standard, but you can request longer if needed)

A template you can adapt:

Dear [Landlord/Property Manager/Housing Authority],

I am writing to formally request a reasonable accommodation under the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604) and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (29 U.S.C. § 794). I am a person with a disability, and I need the following accommodation to have equal use and enjoyment of my housing:

[Describe the specific accommodation in detail. Be concrete. Example: "I request a designated parking space within 50 feet of the building entrance of my unit. This space should be clearly marked as reserved and should not require stairs to access my unit entrance."]

I need this accommodation because [describe how the accommodation relates to your disability-related functional limitation, without necessarily naming your diagnosis. Example: "I have a mobility limitation that makes it difficult and unsafe for me to walk long distances or climb stairs. A nearby parking space would allow me to access my unit safely and independently"].

I am happy to provide supporting documentation from a qualified healthcare professional if needed. I request a response to this request within 14 days. If you need clarification or have questions, please contact me at [your phone number] or [your email].

Thank you for your prompt attention to this request.

Sincerely,
[Your name]
[Your address/unit number]
[Your phone]

Keep a copy of everything you send and note the date. If you send it via email, save the confirmation. If you hand-deliver it, get a signature. If you use certified mail, keep the receipt.

Step 3: Provide Documentation If Requested

If your disability is obvious (for example, you use a wheelchair and are requesting a wheelchair ramp), the housing provider generally cannot require additional documentation. But if the disability or the need for the accommodation isn't apparent, the provider may ask for verification from a qualified professional.

A "qualified professional" includes: your doctor, nurse practitioner, physician's assistant, therapist, psychiatrist, psychologist, social worker, case manager, or other licensed healthcare provider who is familiar with your condition and limitations.

What housing providers CAN and CANNOT ask for:

Housing providers CANNOT ask:

  • For your diagnosis or specific medical condition
  • For detailed medical records or treatment history
  • For your medications
  • For why you have the disability or how you got it
  • For your medical file or records from providers

Housing providers CAN ask:

  • For confirmation that you have a disability-related functional limitation
  • For confirmation that the requested accommodation is necessary because of your disability
  • For a letter from a healthcare provider confirming these two points

A simple letter from your healthcare provider is usually sufficient. It might look like this:

[Date]

To whom it may concern,

This is to confirm that I am a healthcare provider for [client name], and that [he/she/they] has a disability that substantially limits [his/her/their] ability to [describe functional limitation, e.g., "walk without pain" or "manage anxiety in crowded spaces"].

Due to this functional limitation, [client name] requires the following accommodation [describe accommodation] to have equal use and enjoyment of housing.

Sincerely,
[Provider name]
[License/credential]

Give them this letter, not your medical records. If the housing provider asks for your full medical file, they're asking for more than the law allows.

Step 4: Work it out with the provider (the "interactive process")

Fair housing law requires both you and the housing provider to engage in what's called an "interactive process," a good-faith dialogue about the accommodation. The provider doesn't have to grant your exact request. It does have to take the request seriously and talk it through with you.

If the housing provider can't grant your exact request, they should offer an alternative that meets your disability-related need. If you ask for a specific parking spot and it's already assigned, they might offer you a different nearby spot. If you ask for a unit away from hallway noise and they have no units fitting that description, they might offer building modifications to reduce noise or flexibility on which unit you get.

A flat denial with no talk of alternatives and no real explanation is a warning sign. They're supposed to work with you on it.

Keep records of all conversations: who you talked to, when, what was said, and any follow-up emails or letters.

Step 5: When a provider can say no

Housing providers can deny a reasonable accommodation request only if they can show that:

  • The accommodation would impose an undue financial or administrative burden on the housing provider. A grab bar that costs $50-200 is not an undue burden for a property. Building a whole new parking garage is. Burden is measured against the provider's resources and the housing program.
  • The accommodation would fundamentally alter the nature of the housing program. For example, if you request to not pay rent, that might fundamentally alter the nature of the program. But waiving a "no pets" policy for a service animal would not fundamentally alter a rental housing program.
  • You didn't provide requested documentation when the disability or need for accommodation wasn't obvious
  • There's no genuine connection between the requested accommodation and a disability

Service animals vs. emotional support animals

Under fair housing law, both service animals and emotional support animals (ESAs) are protected accommodations, but the rules differ a little.

Service animals are specifically trained to perform a task or job for a person with a disability (e.g., guide dogs for blindness, alert dogs for diabetes or seizures, mobility assistance dogs). Housing providers generally must allow service animals in housing with "no pets" policies. Service animals do not require documentation beyond the disability-functional limitation-accommodation connection.

Emotional support animals (ESAs) provide comfort by their presence but are not trained to perform a specific task. ESAs are protected under fair housing law if they're necessary because of your disability. A housing provider can ask for documentation from a healthcare provider confirming: (1) that you have a mental health or psychiatric disability, and (2) that the ESA is necessary because of that disability. You don't need to disclose your diagnosis; you just need confirmation of disability-related need.

Common ESA documentation questions:

  • "Does the person have a disability-related psychiatric or emotional condition?" (Yes or no)
  • "Is the animal necessary because of that condition?" (Yes or no)

Your provider just needs to confirm these two facts. They don't need your treatment history, diagnosis, or medical records.

Step 6: What to Do If You're Denied

If your reasonable accommodation request is denied, the housing provider should provide a written explanation. Read it carefully. The only legitimate reasons for denial are the four listed above. If the denial doesn't cite one of these reasons, or if the reason doesn't make sense, you likely have grounds for a complaint.

Examples of illegitimate denials:

  • "We don't make accommodations." (Illegal. Fair housing requires them.)
  • "We don't believe you have a disability." (They need documentation, not beliefs.)
  • "We've never done this before." (Not a legal reason for denial.)
  • "It's against our policy." (Policy can't override fair housing law.)
  • "Other tenants might be upset." (Discrimination is illegal regardless of others' opinions.)

If you're denied, these are the places to go:

File a complaint with HUD. You can file a housing discrimination complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO) online at HUD's complaint portal or by calling 1-800-669-9777. You have up to one year from the date of the denial to file. HUD investigates at no cost to you.

Contact a local fair housing organization. Nonprofit fair housing groups can provide free advice, help you file complaints, write appeal letters, and sometimes represent you in proceedings. Search for "fair housing [your city]" or "fair housing [your state]" to find one near you. Some offer pro bono legal representation.

Contact a disability rights organization. Every state has a Protection and Advocacy organization, and the National Disability Rights Network (NDRN) can point you to yours. These are state-run agencies that provide free legal services to people with disabilities.

Contact legal aid. If you qualify for free legal services based on income, legal aid organizations in your state can help with housing discrimination cases. Search "legal aid [your state]" online.

File a complaint with your state or local civil rights agency. Many states have their own fair housing enforcement agencies that can investigate faster than the federal process. You can often file both federally and locally.

Consult a private attorney. Some attorneys take housing discrimination cases on a contingency basis (meaning they don't charge you upfront). Many fair housing organizations can refer you to attorneys who handle these cases.

How long it takes

Housing providers are expected to answer reasonable accommodation requests promptly. The law itself sets no federal deadline, but 14-30 days is considered reasonable. It can take longer if they ask for documentation.

If the provider is dragging their feet (weeks with no response), you can push back: "I submitted my reasonable accommodation request on [date] and have not received a response. Please respond within 7 business days."

For HUD complaints, HUD has 100 days to investigate. In practice, cases can take several months to a year. But once a complaint is filed, housing providers generally cannot retaliate against you (retaliation is illegal under fair housing law).

Special Notes for Section 8 and Public Housing

If you're in a Section 8 voucher program or public housing, your PHA (Public Housing Authority) is also obligated to provide reasonable accommodations. The process is similar to private landlords, but PHAs often have more resources and clearer procedures for accommodations.

Common reasonable accommodations in Section 8/public housing:

  • Extending your housing search time if your disability makes apartment hunting more difficult
  • Allowing an exception to the voucher size (bedroom count) if you need a live-in aide or personal care attendant or a separate room for medical equipment
  • Providing program information in accessible formats (large print, audio, Braille)
  • Making exceptions to program rules that create barriers because of disability
  • Providing phone appointments instead of in-person for those with severe mobility limitations
  • Allowing online payment of rent if you have difficulty with in-person payment due to disability

If your PHA denies your reasonable accommodation request, you have the right to an informal hearing. Ask for the denial in writing and request the hearing within the timeline specified in your PHA's policies (usually 10-20 days from the denial). At the hearing, you can explain why the accommodation is necessary and why the PHA's denial was wrong.

Protections Against Retaliation

Once you request a reasonable accommodation or file a housing discrimination complaint, the housing provider is prohibited from retaliating against you. Retaliation includes: threatening eviction, evicting you, raising your rent, reducing services, making your living conditions worse, or harassing you.

If you request an accommodation and suddenly face eviction proceedings or hostile treatment, that's retaliation, and it's illegal. Document everything: dates, times, what happened, who was involved. If you believe you're being retaliated against, file a complaint immediately.

National Disability Rights Network: ndrn.org, where you can find your state's Protection and Advocacy organization for free legal help

Frequently Asked Questions

What qualifies as a reasonable accommodation in housing?

A reasonable accommodation is any change to rules, policies, or physical space that allows a person with a disability equal access to housing. Common examples include emotional support animals (even in no-pet buildings), reserved accessible parking, grab bars, transfer to a ground-floor unit, extra time to pay rent, and exceptions to guest policies for live-in aides.

Can a landlord deny a reasonable accommodation request?

A landlord can only deny a request if it would cause undue financial or administrative burden, or fundamentally alter the nature of the housing program. They cannot deny simply because they don't want to, because it's inconvenient, or because they don't believe in your disability. If denied, you can file a fair housing complaint.

Do I need a doctor's note for a reasonable accommodation?

Your landlord can request verification of your disability-related need if your disability isn't obvious. A letter from any healthcare provider (doctor, therapist, social worker, counselor) confirming you have a disability and explaining the connection between your disability and the accommodation requested is sufficient. They cannot demand your diagnosis or medical records.
