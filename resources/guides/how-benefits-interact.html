How Your Benefits Interact With Each Other

Matt · Updated November 2026

You're on Section 8. Maybe you're on Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). Maybe you get Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI). You're thinking about taking a job. What happens to everything? Will you lose your benefits? Will you be worse off? This guide explains how the major benefits interact, how the much-feared "benefits cliff" actually works, and why earning more almost always leaves you with more money in your pocket.

The Core Principle: The 30% Rule and Section 8

Understanding Section 8 is the foundation. Your share of the rent, called the Total Tenant Payment, is usually 30% of your adjusted income: your income after HUD's deductions, such as $480 a year for each dependent and $400 a year for an elderly or disabled family under the rules most housing authorities use today ($525 and $575 under the rules that apply from January 1, 2027). Under HUD's rule (24 CFR 5.628), your share is the highest of 30% of your monthly adjusted income, 10% of your monthly gross income, the welfare rent (only in a few cases), or your housing authority's minimum rent (up to $50). The voucher covers the rest, up to the unit's gross rent and the Public Housing Authority's (PHA) payment standard. For a single parent with two children (a $960 yearly dependent deduction):

  • Earning $1,500 a month: adjusted income is $1,420 a month, so your share is about $426. The voucher covers the difference.
  • Earning $2,500 a month: adjusted income is $2,420 a month, so your share is about $726. The voucher covers less.
  • Earning $3,500 a month: adjusted income is $3,420 a month, so your share is about $1,026. The voucher covers even less.

The key: as income goes up, your rent payment goes up, but you keep most of the difference. If you earn an extra $1,000/month, your share usually goes up about $300/month (30% of the increase). You pocket the other $700. You're ahead. If you rent a unit above the payment standard, you also pay that extra amount. Our guide to how Section 8 rent is calculated walks through the full formula.

How SNAP Interacts With Your Income

SNAP (food stamps) has income limits. Under federal rules, your gross income must be at or below 130% of the federal poverty line, and your income after deductions (net income) must be at or below 100% of the poverty line. Some states use a higher gross limit, and households with someone 60 or older or disabled have different rules. How SNAP counts income is important.

The shelter deduction. SNAP allows a deduction for high housing costs. If your rent and utilities are more than half of your income after other deductions, SNAP subtracts the part above half. For most households, this "excess shelter deduction" is capped at $769 a month in fiscal year 2027 (Oct. 1, 2026 to Sept. 30, 2027) in the 48 states and DC. There is no cap if someone in your home is 60 or older or disabled. Many states use a set utility amount instead of your actual bills. This is unusual — most benefit programs don't count housing as a deduction. But SNAP does.

How it works: SNAP looks at your gross income and subtracts deductions: 20% of your earnings, a standard deduction ($217 a month for 1 to 3 people in FY 2027), child care costs, some medical costs for people 60+ or disabled, and the excess shelter deduction. What's left is your net income. Your SNAP amount is the maximum benefit for your household size minus 30% of your net income.

The example (FY 2027 amounts, 48 states and DC): A family of 3 has $2,000 a month in earnings, pays $300 for child care, and pays $800 for rent and utilities. $2,000 minus the 20% earnings deduction ($400) is $1,600. Minus the $217 standard deduction is $1,383. Minus $300 child care is $1,083. Half of $1,083 is about $542, so the shelter deduction is $800 − $542 = about $258. Net income is about $825. The maximum SNAP for 3 people is $808. 30% of $825 is about $248. SNAP: $808 − $248 = about $560 a month.

This means higher housing costs can help your SNAP calculation. If rent and utilities were $1,000 instead of $800, net income would drop by $200 (to about $625), and SNAP would go up by about $60 (to about $620). This is a strange quirk of the system, but it's real. Source: USDA FNS, SNAP cost-of-living adjustments.

SSI/SSDI and How They Interact With Earning

SSI (Supplemental Security Income). This is income-based assistance for disabled, blind, or elderly people with low income. Working lowers SSI, but not dollar for dollar. Social Security does not count the first $65 of your monthly earnings (plus a $20 general exclusion), and then counts only half of the rest. In 2026, if work is your only other income, your federal SSI check stops only when your earnings pass about $2,073 a month. States that add a state supplement have a higher point.

PASS (Plan to Achieve Self-Support) is not an income limit. It is a written plan, approved by Social Security, to reach a work goal. With an approved PASS, you can set aside income (like wages or SSDI) or savings to pay for things you need for that goal, such as school, training, tools, or starting a business. Social Security does not count the money you set aside, so your SSI check can stay higher. You apply on Form SSA-545-BK. This is a real tool if you're on SSI and want to work. Sources: SSA POMS SI 00870.001, SI 00820.500.

SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance). This is different. SSDI is not income-based like SSI. You don't lose SSDI because you earn too much. You can earn and keep SSDI (up to a "substantial gainful activity" level, which is $1,690/month in 2026 and $1,770/month in 2027 for people who are not blind). SSDI is more work-friendly than SSI.

Medicaid and the Income Limit

Medicaid income limits vary wildly by state. Some states have limits at 133% of federal poverty. Some go higher. After the income limit, you don't qualify.

But there's the marketplace. If you earn too much for Medicaid but not too much for marketplace subsidies, you can buy insurance through the ACA marketplace and get tax credits that make it affordable. The subsidy ramps down as income goes up, but it exists. You don't suddenly go without insurance.

Check what your state's Medicaid limit is. But even if you exceed it, explore marketplace options before assuming you'll be uninsured.

TANF and Work Requirements

TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families) usually has work requirements. You're expected to look for work or participate in work activities. As you earn income, your TANF benefit reduces. But TANF is meant to transition you to work. Many states have "work incentive" provisions that let you earn some money without full benefit reduction.

The details vary by state. Ask your TANF case worker about work incentives in your state. There might be more flexibility than you think.

The "Benefits Cliff" and Why It's Usually a Myth

The "benefits cliff" is the fear that earning just a little more will cause you to lose a bunch of benefits at once, leaving you worse off. You earn $100 more, and suddenly lose $500 in benefits. It sounds real. It's frightening enough to keep people from working.

But in practice, true cliffs are rare. Here's why:

Most benefits phase out gradually. Section 8 doesn't have a cliff — it increases continuously with income. SNAP reduces gradually as your income goes up. SSDI doesn't have a harsh cliff either if you understand the work incentives.

The cliff that does exist is usually Medicaid. Some states have a hard Medicaid income limit. Earn one dollar over it, and you lose Medicaid. That's a real cliff. But it usually happens at a higher income level (often above $1,500-$2,000/month depending on the state and whether you have kids). And when you exceed Medicaid limits, you often qualify for marketplace subsidies.

The math usually works out in your favor. Let's say you're on Section 8 and SNAP. You earn an extra $600/month. Section 8 goes up $180 (30%). SNAP goes down maybe $150 (it reduces at 30% after deductions). Net: you're up $270/month. The fear of the cliff keeps you from earning $600, but earning $600 leaves you better off by $270.

Putting It All Together: A Realistic Example

Current situation:

  • Part-time job: $1,200/month, with two children
  • Section 8 voucher: pays most of the $1,500 rent; your share is $336 (30% of $1,120 adjusted income)
  • SNAP: about $585/month (FY 2027 amounts, if utilities are included in the rent)

You move to full-time work and earn $600/month more ($1,800/month).

  • Work income: up $600
  • Section 8: your share is now $516 (30% of $1,720 adjusted income) — an increase of $180
  • SNAP: might go down by about $150
  • Net change: +$600 − $180 − about $150 = about $270/month more

This is a simplification, but you see the pattern. Your housing cost went up $180 and your SNAP may drop, but you earned $600. Net: you're better off by roughly $270/month. If you get SSI, earnings can lower your SSI check too, so ask Social Security or a benefits counselor what your numbers would be.

Why Earning More Almost Always Works Out

The reason is simple math. Benefits are designed to replace income loss, not to match earned income dollar-for-dollar. They reduce at rates less than 100%. The magic number is often 30%.

Earn an extra $100: Section 8 goes up $30, SNAP goes down maybe $20. You net $50. You're better off.

Earn an extra $1,000: Section 8 goes up $300, SNAP goes down maybe $150. You net $550. You're much better off.

The only time you're worse off is if you lose a benefit entirely (like SSI) and don't have work-around options. Even then, you usually come out ahead with a job because you're adding earned income while benefits only partially reduce.

What to Do Before Taking a Job

Talk to each benefit program before you work. Call your Section 8 PHA, SNAP office, SSI office, and Medicaid agency. Ask specifically: "If I earn $X per month, what happens to my benefits?" Get it in writing or get the name of the person you spoke to.

Ask about work incentives. Many programs have specific provisions for people who are working or trying to work. SSDI has ticket to work. SSI has PASS plans. TANF has work supports. Ask.

Calculate your break-even point. Figure out at what income level benefits reduce enough that you're no longer ahead. Usually this is well above most job wages.

Plan for the transition. If you're about to lose Medicaid, research marketplace insurance. If you might lose SNAP, budget accordingly. Don't be surprised.

Report changes to all programs. When you start working, notify your PHA (for Section 8 recertification), your SNAP office, SSI, Medicaid, and any other programs. They'll recalculate and send you new payment amounts. Don't hide work or earnings.

The Bottom Line

Benefits are designed to help when you're not earning. They're not meant to make you worse off if you do earn. The systems interlock imperfectly, and the paperwork is confusing. But the underlying math works in your favor. Earning more almost always leaves you with more total money. The benefits cliff exists in people's heads more than in reality. Don't let fear of losing benefits keep you from a job. Ask your case workers, do the math, and then decide.

Questions to Ask Before Working

Write down these questions and ask your case workers:

  • What happens to my Section 8 if I earn $X per month?
  • What happens to my SNAP if I earn $X per month?
  • What happens to my SSI/SSDI/TANF if I earn $X per month?
  • Do I have a work incentive plan I can use?
  • What's my income limit before I lose [this benefit]?
  • Can I get marketplace insurance if I exceed Medicaid limits?
  • Who do I report my new job to?
