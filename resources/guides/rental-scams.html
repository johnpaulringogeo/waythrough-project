Rental Scams and Fake Landlords: How to Spot Them and What to Do If You Paid

Matt · Updated November 2026

Rental scams cost people their savings at the worst possible time: when they need a place to live. A scammer copies photos from a real listing, posts it for a low rent, and asks for a deposit or "holding fee" before you can see the inside. Some scammers target people with Section 8 vouchers, or people trying to get on a waiting list. This guide shows the warning signs, how to check who really owns a home, and what to do if you already paid.

Here's the short version: Don't pay anything until you have seen the inside of the home, checked who owns it, and signed a written lease. Never pay by wire transfer, gift card, or crypto. No housing authority charges you to apply for Section 8 or to join a waitlist. If you already paid, call your bank or payment app today, then report it to the police, the FTC, and your state attorney general.

Already sent money? Act today

Call the company you paid through (your bank, the payment app, the wire service, or the gift card company) right away. Say you were scammed and ask them to reverse the payment. The sooner you call, the better your chance. Then follow the steps in What to do if you lost money below.

Red flags of a rental scam

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) lists these warning signs. One alone does not prove a scam, but two or more should make you stop and check.

  • They want money before you see the inside or sign a lease. This includes an application fee, a deposit, a "holding fee," or the first month's rent.
  • They want a hard-to-trace payment. The FTC warns about requests to pay by wire transfer (like Western Union or MoneyGram), gift cards, or cryptocurrency. It says paying this way is "like sending cash," and once it's gone you "probably can't get it back." Payment apps like Zelle, Venmo, and Cash App can be just as hard to undo.
  • The "owner" is out of town. Scammers often "claim to be out of the country" so they never have to show the home. They may offer to mail you a key after you pay.
  • The rent is too good to be true. The FTC warns about rent that is "a lot cheaper than most rents in the area."
  • They rush you. "Other people want it, so pay today" is a common line.
  • They ask for your Social Security number or bank details early, before you have agreed to rent.
  • The listing doesn't match. The same home is listed by a different person or company, or it is actually listed for sale. The FTC calls a for-sale home "a big red flag."

Are holding fees always a scam?

No. Some real landlords charge a holding fee after they accept your application, to hold the unit until you sign the lease. HUD even lets housing authorities help voucher holders pay one (Notice PIH 2022-18). A real holding fee comes with a receipt and written terms. Some states set rules. In Washington State, for example, the landlord must give you a receipt and a written statement of when the fee can be kept, the fee cannot be more than 25% of the first month's rent, and it must be credited to your rent or deposit if you move in (RCW 59.18.253). Before you pay any holding fee, make sure you have seen the unit and checked who owns it.

How to check who owns a property

This takes about 15 minutes and is the best way to catch a fake landlord.

  1. Look up the owner. Search the address on your county assessor or property tax website. Most list the owner's name for free. You can also search the county recorder or register of deeds for the most recent deed. The FTC suggests checking "city or county tax assessment sites" to confirm a private landlord owns the home.
  2. Compare the names. Does the owner's name match the person you are dealing with? If not, ask why. A property manager is normal, but you should be able to confirm that the manager works for the owner.
  3. Call the company yourself. If a management company is listed, find its phone number on its own website and call to ask whether the listing is real. The FTC says to use contact information you already have, not the number from someone who contacted you.
  4. Search the address and the name online. Search the address to see if it is listed elsewhere with a different name or price. Search the landlord's name with words like "scam" or "complaint."
  5. See the inside in person. A video tour or a drive-by is not enough. Ask to see it with the owner or manager, and ask for photo ID.
  6. Pay safely. Pay only after you sign a lease, with a check or card in the name that matches the owner or manager, and get a receipt.

Scams that target Section 8 and voucher holders

Fake waitlist sites and posts

HUD's Office of Inspector General (OIG) warned in an August 1, 2024 fraud bulletin that fraudsters post on social media that Section 8 waitlists are "opening soon." They pretend to represent HUD or a housing agency to get "sensitive personal information or payment" to "apply." Then they disappear. The OIG's advice:

  • "Never pay anyone money so you can be added to an assisted housing waitlist." The OIG says housing authorities and government agencies will never ask you to pay to join a waitlist or to get housing assistance.
  • Don't reply to social media ads that offer a spot on a waitlist. Call your local housing authority at the number on its official website and ask whether the list is open. Our guide on how to find your PHA shows how to find the real one.
  • Don't give your Social Security number or date of birth in response to a social media ad.

HUD does not take Section 8 applications itself. You apply through your local housing authority. Our Section 8 lottery guide explains how real waitlist lotteries work.

Landlords who ask for extra money

If you rent with a voucher, the housing authority approves the rent, and you pay only your share. HUD's rule says the owner "may not demand or accept any rent payment from the tenant in excess of" the approved amount, and must return any extra (24 CFR 982.451(b)(4)(ii)). A landlord who asks for cash "on the side" is breaking the rules. Tell your housing authority in writing.

Fake "Section 8 approved" listings

Some fake listings say "Section 8 welcome" to attract voucher holders. The same checks apply: look up the owner, see the inside, and don't pay before your housing authority has your Request for Tenancy Approval and you have a signed lease.

What to do if you lost money

Do these steps in order, and keep a copy of every text, email, ad, receipt, and screenshot.

  1. Call the payment company right away. The FTC says to report it "immediately" and ask for your money back:
    • Bank transfer or Zelle: call your bank or credit union and ask them to reverse the payment.
    • Payment app (Venmo, Cash App, PayPal): report it in the app and ask them to reverse it.
    • Wire transfer (Western Union, MoneyGram): call the company, say you were tricked, and ask for a refund.
    • Gift card: call the number on the back of the card, say a scammer got the card number, and ask for a refund. Keep the card and receipt.
    • Debit or credit card: call the number on the back of the card and ask for a refund.
  2. File a police report with your local police or sheriff, using the non-emergency number. Ask for a copy or a report number. Banks and housing authorities may ask for it.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  4. Report online scams to the FBI at ic3.gov, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. It is "the central hub for reporting cyber-enabled crime."
  5. Report it to your state attorney general. Find yours at USA.gov. Most have a consumer protection complaint form.
  6. Report the ad to the website or app where you saw it, so it comes down.
  7. If it was a fake waitlist or someone pretending to be HUD or a housing authority, call the HUD OIG Hotline at 1-800-347-3735 or report at hudoig.gov/hotline. You can report anonymously.
  8. If you gave your Social Security number or bank details, watch your accounts. The FTC says that if someone steals your identity, you can report it at IdentityTheft.gov and get "a free personal recovery plan."

If you have a voucher: tell your housing authority in writing

A scam can eat up days on your voucher. Tell your housing authority what happened as soon as you can, and ask for more time if you need it. Under HUD's rules, a housing authority may extend your voucher under its own policy, and it must extend it as a reasonable accommodation if a disability makes your search take longer (24 CFR 982.303(b)). Send your request before your voucher expires. If it has already expired, ask in writing anyway whether the housing authority will reissue it, and call legal aid. Here is a sample:

"To [housing authority], Re: [your name], voucher [number], expiring [date]. On [date], I paid [amount] to a person who said they owned [address]. I learned it was a scam. I filed police report [number], and a copy is attached. I am still searching for a unit. I ask for an extension of my voucher term under your administrative plan. [If it applies: I also ask for this extension as a reasonable accommodation because my disability makes the search take longer.] Please reply in writing. Thank you, [name, phone]."

Keep a copy and write down the date you sent it. If the housing authority says no, ask for the reason in writing, and ask legal aid for help. Our guide to free legal help for tenants shows how to find it.

Watch out for "recovery" scams

After you report a scam, someone may contact you and offer to get your money back for a fee. That is often a second scam. The FBI's IC3 says it "will never directly contact you for information or money," and it does not work with private companies to recover lost funds.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a rental listing is fake?

Watch for rent far below other homes nearby, an owner who says they are out of town or out of the country, pressure to decide fast, and requests to pay by wire transfer, gift card, crypto, or a payment app before you see the inside or sign a lease. The FTC also warns about the same address listed under a different name, or a home that is listed for sale.

How do I check who really owns a rental?

Search the address on your county assessor or property tax website, and on the county recorder or register of deeds website. Compare the owner's name with the name of the person renting it to you. If a company manages it, call the company using a phone number you find yourself, not one from the ad.

Do I have to pay to get on a Section 8 waiting list?

No. HUD's Office of Inspector General says housing authorities and government agencies will never ask you to pay to join a waitlist or to be admitted to a housing assistance program. If a website or social media post asks for money to apply, it is a scam. Report it to the HUD OIG Hotline at 1-800-347-3735.

I paid a fake landlord. Can I get my money back?

Sometimes, if you act fast. Call your bank, payment app, wire transfer company, or gift card company right away, say you were scammed, and ask them to reverse the payment. Then file a police report and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, the FBI at ic3.gov for online scams, and your state attorney general. Money sent by wire, gift card, or crypto is hard to get back.

I have a voucher and lost money to a scam. What should I tell my housing authority?

Tell your housing authority in writing what happened, attach your police report, and ask for more time on your voucher if it is running out. Housing authorities can extend a voucher under their own policy, and they must extend it as a reasonable accommodation if a disability makes the search take longer (24 CFR 982.303(b)).
