How to Find a HUD-Approved Housing Counselor

Matt · Updated April 2026

Housing counselors are trained people who help renters and homeowners deal with the housing system, and HUD-approved counselors are free. They can help you apply for Section 8, improve your credit, make a budget, avoid foreclosure, and learn your rights as a tenant. This guide covers what they do, how to find one near you, what happens at the first session, and when you need a housing attorney instead.

What Do Housing Counselors Do?

HUD-approved housing counselors give free, confidential advice on most housing problems. They work for nonprofits, community groups, and government agencies that HUD has certified to do housing counseling. They can help with:

  • Section 8 and rental assistance applications: Help you understand eligibility, gather documents, fill out applications correctly, and appeal denials
  • Budget planning and financial management: Create a realistic budget, track expenses, manage debt, and prioritize housing costs
  • Credit repair: Understand your credit report, dispute errors, negotiate with creditors, and rebuild your score over time
  • Eviction prevention: Negotiate with landlords, understand your rights, apply for emergency rental assistance, and explore legal defenses
  • Foreclosure prevention: Work with your lender, understand loan modification options, and explore alternatives to foreclosure
  • Homeownership education: Learn about down payments, mortgages, homebuying programs, and home maintenance
  • Tenant rights: Understand your lease, landlord responsibilities, discrimination protections, and how to file complaints
  • Credit counseling: Understand debt, negotiate with creditors, and explore debt management options

Counselors won't judge you, and what you tell them is confidential. They go at your pace. They know a housing problem is usually tangled up with income, health, or family trouble too.

How to Find a HUD-Approved Housing Counselor

HUD's own directory is the easiest place to start, but there are three ways to find one:

Method 1: HUD's Housing Counselor Locator (Easiest)

Visit HUD's Housing Counselor Locator. Enter your zip code and search. You'll see a list of HUD-approved agencies in your area, their specialties, and contact information. Many can start helping you right away by phone or video call, so you don't have to wait for an in-person appointment.

Method 2: Call 211

Dial 211 from any phone and tell them you're looking for free housing counseling. They can connect you with local HUD-approved agencies. This works well if you don't have internet access or would rather talk to someone.

Method 3: Contact Your Local PHA or Nonprofits

Your Public Housing Authority (PHA) often keeps a list of housing counselors it recommends. Local nonprofits that work on affordable housing, homelessness prevention, or tenant rights often partner with HUD-approved counselors. Many public libraries and community centers have resource lists too.

What to Expect in Your First Session

Most first appointments take 30-60 minutes. They usually go like this.

Intake and Confidentiality

The counselor will ask you basic questions about your household size, current housing situation, income, and what you need help with. They'll explain confidentiality: what you share won't be reported to landlords, the PHA, creditors, or other agencies. The exception is if you're in immediate danger, which is rare, and they'll tell you that upfront.

Telling Them What's Going On

Be honest about what's happening, whether it's an eviction, a stuck Section 8 application, a lease you don't understand, or bad credit. Counselors see people in real hardship every day, and they won't judge you.

Creating a Plan

The counselor will help you sort out what to tackle first and make a plan you can actually follow. For example, if you're trying to apply for Section 8 but need to repair your credit first, they'll explain the steps and timeline. They'll tell you what documents you need, what to expect at each stage, and how long things typically take.

Follow-Up Sessions

Most people see a counselor more than once. If you're applying for Section 8, they might review your application before you submit it, answer questions during the process, and help you prepare for your eligibility interview. If you're dealing with eviction, they might help you negotiate with your landlord or apply for emergency rental assistance. You can usually schedule follow-ups as needed.

What Counselors Can Help With: Detailed Examples

Section 8 Application Help

Housing counselors know Section 8 rules and how PHAs work. They'll help you gather the right documents, understand income calculations, claim preferences you might qualify for, and fill out your application correctly. If your PHA denies your application, a counselor can help you request a hearing and prepare your appeal. That help can decide whether you get a voucher or lose out.

Budgeting When Money Is Tight

A counselor will help you see exactly where your money goes and where you can cut without putting your housing at risk. They know rent eats up most of a low-income budget, so they won't tell you to cut groceries. They'll help you put housing first, lower your utility bills, dispute fraudulent debts, and figure out which bills really have to be paid. See our guide on budgeting when money is tight for more.

Credit Repair and Dispute

Counselors can help you get a free copy of your credit report, read it, and dispute errors. They know which negative items matter most to landlords and PHAs (late payments and evictions matter more than medical debt). They can show you how to negotiate with creditors, set up payment plans, and rebuild your score over time.

Foreclosure Prevention

If you're a homeowner facing foreclosure, a counselor can help you contact your lender, understand loan modification options, negotiate a payment plan, and explore alternatives like deed-in-lieu. They can also connect you with legal aid if you need an attorney.

Eviction Prevention

If you're facing eviction, a counselor can help you understand your legal rights, negotiate with your landlord (often more effectively than you could alone), apply for emergency rental assistance, and explore legal defenses. They might also help you find alternative housing quickly if eviction becomes unavoidable.

Housing Counselors vs. Housing Navigators vs. Housing Attorneys

People mix these up. They are different jobs.

Housing Counselors

Free, trained, and certified by HUD. They help with applications, budgeting, credit, and housing decisions. They aren't lawyers. Go to one to sort out your options and get your paperwork in order.

Housing Navigators

Usually paid for by nonprofits or government programs to help people get into specific benefits or housing programs. They might focus on homelessness, veterans, people with disabilities, or emergency rental assistance. Usually free. The work is close to a counselor's, but narrower.

Housing Attorneys

Lawyers who specialize in housing law. They can file formal complaints, represent you in court, negotiate complex legal situations, and provide legal advice. Some provide free services if you qualify for legal aid; others charge fees. See when to contact a housing attorney below.

When to Contact a Housing Attorney Instead

Counselors can handle most situations. These need a lawyer:

  • Eviction is already filed. You have a court date or received an eviction notice. You need legal representation in court.
  • Foreclosure on your home. This requires legal defense, loan modification negotiation, or bankruptcy advice. An attorney can explore options a counselor cannot.
  • Housing discrimination. If you believe you were denied housing based on protected characteristics (race, disability, family status, etc.), you need an attorney to file a discrimination complaint and represent you.
  • Complex lease disputes. If there's a disagreement about lease terms that affects your housing security, a lawyer can review the contract and advise you.
  • Criminal record affecting housing. If you were denied housing based on a criminal record and want to challenge it legally, an attorney can help. They might also advise on expungement options.

Many housing counselors can refer you to legal aid organizations if you qualify. Legal aid provides free legal services to low-income people. Visit Lawhelp.org to find legal aid in your state.

Preparing for Your First Appointment

Bring any documents related to your housing situation:

  • Your most recent lease or rental agreement
  • Recent pay stubs and benefit letters
  • Eviction notices or letters from your landlord (if applicable)
  • Credit report (you can get one free at annualcreditreport.com)
  • List of debts and creditors
  • Section 8 denial letter (if you've been denied)
  • Any court documents or housing-related paperwork

It doesn't have to be organized. Counselors deal with messy paperwork all the time. Bring what you have and they'll help you sort it.

How Counselors Stay Free

HUD-approved housing counselors are paid for by HUD, nonprofits, government grants, and community organizations. They charge you nothing, and there are no hidden fees. If someone calls themselves a "housing counselor" and asks for money, they're not legitimate.

What Happens After Counseling?

Counseling usually isn't a single visit. If you're applying for Section 8, your counselor will help throughout the process. If you're in crisis (facing eviction or foreclosure), they'll work with you until the immediate crisis is resolved. Some people need a few sessions. Others stay with the same counselor for months or years as their situation changes.

A good counselor teaches you enough that you need less help over time. If a new housing problem comes up later, you can go back.
