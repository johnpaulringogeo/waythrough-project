Shared Housing and Roommate Strategies

Matt · Updated April 2026

Sharing a place is one of the cheapest ways to stay housed. This guide covers four ways people do it with Section 8: adding a co-tenant to your voucher, bringing in a live-in aide, living in a seniors housing program, and splitting an apartment with roommates. For each one, it explains what the PHA needs from you and what happens to your rent and household.

Adding Co-Tenants to Your Section 8 Voucher

Your Section 8 voucher belongs to your household, meaning the people listed on your lease, and the PHA sets your rent from that household's income. To add another person (a co-tenant), you have to get the PHA's approval and put them on your lease.

Who Can Be a Co-Tenant?

A co-tenant is anyone who will live in the unit and share expenses. For Section 8, they become part of your household. A co-tenant can be:

  • Family members (adult children, siblings, parents, extended family)
  • Friends or roommates
  • Romantic partners

Whoever it is, they have to be on your lease and in your Section 8 household. The PHA will verify their income and count it when it decides your eligibility and your rent.

How Adding a Co-Tenant Affects Your Rent

When you add a co-tenant, the PHA adds their income to your household's total income. That usually raises your rent, since the rent is now figured on a bigger household income. How much it goes up depends on what they earn:

Scenario 1: Low-Income Co-Tenant

If your co-tenant has little or no income, your rent may not go up much. Say they are unemployed and need a place to live. Their zero income adds nothing to the calculation, so your rent might barely change, or stay the same, depending on how the PHA handles household adjustments.

Scenario 2: Working Co-Tenant

If your co-tenant earns real money, your household income goes up and your rent follows. The PHA adds their income to yours and recalculates your total tenant payment (TTP). For most families that is 30% of monthly adjusted income (income after deductions). The rule is that TTP is the highest of 30% of monthly adjusted income, 10% of monthly income, welfare rent where it applies, or your PHA's minimum rent (24 CFR 5.628). A co-tenant earning $2,000/month adds a lot to your household income and raises your TTP.

When Adding a Co-Tenant Might Make Sense

It can be worth it in these cases:

  • Splitting the rent lowers your cost. If you move from a 1-bedroom to a 2-bedroom with a co-tenant, they pay part of the rent. Your Section 8 rent may go up and you can still come out ahead. For example, your rent goes from $400 (1-bedroom) to $500 (2-bedroom shared with co-tenant). Split 50-50, you pay $250, less than before.
  • Safety or health. You need someone in the home to help with childcare or elderly care, or because you have a disability.
  • The co-tenant needs a stable place. If they are unemployed or low-income, being on your voucher gives them somewhere secure to live.

How to Add a Co-Tenant

Call or write your PHA and ask to add a household member. Then:

  1. Provide the co-tenant's personal information (name, date of birth, Social Security number)
  2. Verify their income with pay stubs, benefit letters, or bank statements
  3. Verify their background (background check may be required)
  4. Update your lease with the landlord to include the co-tenant's name
  5. Have the PHA approve the addition

The PHA will recalculate your rent with the new household and income. Usually that happens at your next recertification, though some PHAs do it sooner.

Live-In Aides and Caregivers

If you or someone in your household is elderly or has a disability, you may be able to add a live-in aide (also called a caregiver) without their income counting toward your rent.

What Is a Live-In Aide?

A live-in aide lives in your unit to care for a household member who is elderly, near-elderly (50 to 61), or has a disability. That can mean help with bathing and hygiene, medical care, meals, medications, getting around, or other care the person needs.

The Aide's Income Doesn't Count

This is the big difference from a co-tenant. A live-in aide's income is left out of your household income, so your rent stays based on what your household already earns, no matter what the aide makes. If you need care and can't afford a higher rent, this matters a lot.

Who Qualifies for a Live-In Aide

To qualify:

  • Someone in your household must be elderly (62+), near-elderly (50 to 61), or have a disability (24 CFR 5.403)
  • The aide must be necessary for you to remain housed and independent
  • The aide must not be part of the original household (you can't count a family member already living with you as a "new" aide to avoid counting their income)
  • The aide's primary purpose must be providing care, not just living there

How to Add a Live-In Aide

Ask your PHA for a reasonable accommodation, and include:

  1. A letter from a doctor or healthcare provider explaining the disability or medical need and why live-in care is necessary
  2. Information about the proposed aide (name, relationship to you, background)
  3. Explanation of the specific care the aide will provide

The PHA has to approve the aide before they move in. After that, you add the aide to your lease, and their income stays out of your household income. Under the Fair Housing Act, this counts as a reasonable accommodation.

Bedroom Size and Occupancy Standards

Your voucher size comes from your PHA's subsidy standards, which each PHA sets in its own policies. HUD requires them to give the smallest number of bedrooms your household needs without overcrowding (24 CFR 982.402). Many PHAs use about two people per bedroom, which looks like this:

  • 1 person: Studio or 1-bedroom
  • 2 people: 1-bedroom
  • 3-4 people: 2-bedroom
  • 5-6 people: 3-bedroom
  • 7-8 people: 4-bedroom
  • And so on (typically one additional bedroom per 2 additional people)

Your PHA's chart may differ, and it can make exceptions for age, health, disability, or other reasons. A live-in aide the PHA approves must be counted when it sets your voucher size (24 CFR 982.402(b)(6)), which often means one more bedroom. How bedrooms are assigned beyond that depends on your PHA's subsidy standards, so check with your PHA before you count on a bedroom.

Seniors Housing and Co-Housing Programs

Many communities have senior housing programs built around sharing. They are meant to cut costs, give people company, and keep older adults from becoming isolated.

Congregate Housing

In congregate housing, seniors rent their own units but share a dining room, kitchen, and living room, and get services like meals, transportation, and activities. You can use a Section 8 voucher there. Your rent is figured the same way as in any other Section 8 unit; the meals, rides, and neighbors come with it.

Co-Housing Communities

In co-housing, several households each keep a private unit and share a common house, often with a kitchen and dining area, plus group meals and activities. Some co-housing communities accept Section 8 vouchers. To find one, ask HUD or a local nonprofit that serves seniors.

Master Leasing Programs

Some nonprofits and community groups master lease a building: they lease the whole thing from the landlord, then rent units to Section 8 voucher holders or other low-income residents. These buildings are set up for low-income tenants, and some come with supportive services.

Splitting Costs with Roommates in LIHTC Housing

Section 8 is where most shared housing questions come up, but many voucher holders live in Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) properties and share a unit with a roommate. Roommates splitting a regular market-rate apartment is common too.

How Cost-Splitting Works

If you and a roommate both have Section 8 vouchers, the rent is split based on your individual rent calculations. For example:

  • Unit rent: $1,200
  • Your TTP (calculated based on your income): $500
  • Roommate's TTP (calculated based on their income): $300
  • Total tenant payments: $800
  • PHA pays the difference: $400 to the landlord

The lease should say how the unit is set up (two separate leases, or one lease with both names) and who pays what. Some roommates put this in writing and some don't. Write it down; it heads off fights later.

When a Roommate Moves Out

If a roommate leaves, here is what can happen:

  • You stay; they leave. You may be able to stay in the unit alone, but you need PHA approval. The PHA might require you to find a smaller unit if the apartment is too big for your household, or let you stay if you can afford the higher rent. Call the PHA and ask.
  • The lease requires both names. If the lease lists both you and your roommate, and they leave, you typically need to sign a new lease with just your name, or find a new roommate.
  • Find a new roommate. If you can't afford the unit without splitting costs, find someone to replace them. The new roommate needs a housing voucher or enough income to pay their share, and you have to tell the landlord and PHA about the change.

What You Have to Report to the PHA

When the people in your household change, you have to tell the PHA. That includes:

  • Adding or removing a household member
  • Someone moving out temporarily (prolonged absence) or permanently
  • A member of your household getting married or divorced
  • A household member's income significantly changing
  • Someone becoming disabled or elderly

How to Report

Tell your PHA as soon as the change happens, not at your annual recertification. When you report right away:

  1. Your rent is calculated correctly based on current household composition
  2. If someone moved out and your rent should go down, the lower rent starts the first of the month after the change you reported. If you report late, the PHA only has to lower it going forward, though it may choose to go back further (24 CFR 982.516(c)(4))
  3. If someone moved in and your rent goes up, the PHA must give you 30 days' notice before the increase starts. If you report late, the PHA must charge the increase back to the month after the change

What Happens If You Don't Report

If you don't report household changes:

  • You might pay more rent than you should, and the PHA won't owe you a refund
  • Your lease might be inconsistent with your PHA household composition, creating compliance issues
  • A guest who stays longer than your PHA's guest rules allow can be treated as an unapproved household member. Anyone who moves in needs PHA approval first (24 CFR 982.551(h)), so check your PHA's guest limits before someone stays long term
  • The PHA could terminate your assistance if you knowingly hide household members or income

Practical Considerations for Shared Housing

Written Agreements with Roommates

Even with a friend, put your arrangement in writing. Cover:

  • How you'll split rent and utilities
  • What happens if someone can't pay their share
  • Notice required if someone wants to move out
  • How shared expenses (groceries, household supplies) are handled
  • Expectations for cleanliness, quiet hours, guests, and shared spaces

Most roommate fights start over something nobody wrote down.

Landlord Requirements

Everyone who lives in the unit has to be on the lease. A resident the landlord doesn't know about can be grounds for eviction. The landlord needs to know who lives there for insurance, liability, and occupancy limits.

Income Verification for Co-Tenants

When you add a co-tenant, the PHA will ask for their income papers. If they don't want to hand them over, tell them:

  • Income verification is standard and required by the PHA
  • Their information is confidential
  • It's necessary for your housing assistance to continue
  • Without it, you might lose your voucher

When Shared Housing Doesn't Work Out

If the arrangement falls apart, it usually looks like one of these:

  • Roommate conflict: You may need to ask someone to move out. Give proper notice (as specified in your roommate agreement). If they refuse, you may need to involve the landlord or, in extreme cases, pursue eviction.
  • Can't afford it alone: If a roommate leaves and you can't afford the full rent, you have several options: find a new roommate quickly, request a lease break from the landlord (though this is not guaranteed), apply to move to a smaller unit, or request emergency rental assistance if you're at risk of eviction.
  • Lease termination: If the shared housing situation is unsustainable, talk to your landlord about early lease termination. Many landlords will work with you rather than deal with conflict or non-payment.

Before Anyone Moves In or Out

Get the PHA's approval first, and put the person on (or take them off) the lease. Report every household change to your PHA when it happens. Put your roommate arrangement in writing. Sharing can lower your costs, but only if the PHA and your landlord know who lives there.
