Free Legal Help for Tenants: How to Find a Lawyer When You Face Eviction

Matt · Updated October 2026

If you rent and need a lawyer, start with your local legal aid office. Legal aid groups are nonprofits that give free legal help to people with low incomes, and eviction is one of the main things they handle. You can find yours with the Legal Services Corporation's find legal aid tool or at LawHelp.org, or by calling 2-1-1. In some cities and states, tenants facing eviction have a right to a free lawyer by law. The list is below. If you already have court papers, call for help today.

Here's the short version: Look up your legal aid office and call it. Have your court papers or notice in front of you. If legal aid is full, ask for advice only, try your state's LawHelp site and your court's self-help center, and still go to every court date. Never pay anyone who promises to "stop" your eviction. Only a court decides an eviction, and real legal aid is free.

If you have court papers, act today

Read the papers right away and find two things: the date you must respond, and the date of your hearing. Some deadlines are only days away. If you miss your hearing, the court can rule against you without hearing your side. Call legal aid the same day, and go to court even if you have not found a lawyer yet. Our guide to landlord notices explains what each kind of paper means.

1. Legal aid: free lawyers for people with low incomes

The Legal Services Corporation (LSC) is the largest funder of civil legal aid in the country. LSC says the groups it funds "provide free legal assistance to low-income individuals and families," and it lists eviction and foreclosure defense among their main work.

  • How to find yours: go to LSC's "I Need Legal Help" page and type your address or city. It shows the legal aid group that serves your area, with its phone number and website.
  • Who qualifies: LSC-funded groups can generally help people with income up to 125% of the federal poverty guidelines. For 2026, that is $19,950 a year for one person, $27,050 for two, $34,150 for three, and $41,250 for four (higher in Alaska and Hawaii). Some groups can make exceptions, and many legal aid offices also have other funding with different limits. If you are close, call anyway.
  • What to expect: legal aid offices get more calls than they can take. When you call, say that you have an eviction case and give your court date first. That helps them move urgent cases to the front.

2. LawHelp.org and your state's LawHelp site

LawHelp.org helps you "find free legal help and information about your legal rights from nonprofit legal aid providers in your state." It is run by the nonprofit Pro Bono Net with legal aid groups. Pick your state, and it sends you to your state's site, such as LawHelpNY or TexasLawHelp.org. State sites list legal aid offices and clinics near you, and many have plain-language guides and court forms for eviction cases. Those forms help if you end up going to court on your own.

3. Call 211

211 connects you with local help. Dial 2-1-1 from most phones. 211.org says "every call is completely confidential." Ask for free legal help with an eviction, and also ask about rent help, since many legal aid offices and rent programs work together. In some places, 211 is the official way in: Maryland and Westchester County, New York, both tell tenants to call 211 to reach their free eviction lawyers.

4. Places where tenants have a right to a free lawyer

Some cities and states have passed Right to Counsel laws. Under these laws, tenants who qualify get a free lawyer for their eviction case. Most have income limits, and some cover only certain ZIP codes or certain kinds of cases. Some depend on yearly funding. 5. Bar association lawyer referral services (low-cost, not free)

A lawyer referral service is run by a state or local bar association. You call, describe your problem, and they match you with a private lawyer who handles that kind of case. The first meeting is usually short and cheap, but it is not free, and after that you pay the lawyer's normal fee unless you agree on something else. Two examples from official bar sites:

  • The Florida Bar: a 30-minute consultation for no more than $25. Call 800-342-8011.
  • Oregon State Bar: a consultation of up to 30 minutes for no more than $50. Its Modest Means Program offers reduced rates for moderate-income Oregonians, and rental housing is one of the areas it covers.

Search for your state or county bar association's "lawyer referral service." This is a good choice if your income is too high for legal aid. Some bar groups also run free clinics or reduced-fee programs, so ask.

Free online answers: the American Bar Association's Free Legal Answers lets people who qualify post a civil legal question "at no cost" for a volunteer lawyer to answer. It covers housing and eviction, but not every state takes part. It is for questions, not for a lawyer to go to court with you.

6. Law school clinics

Many law schools run free clinics where law students, supervised by licensed lawyers, help real clients. Some have housing or eviction clinics. Call the nearest law school and ask whether it has a clinic for tenants. Clinics often follow the school year, so they may not have room during summer or exams. Your state's LawHelp site may list them.

7. Discrimination: fair housing groups and HUD

If you think your landlord is evicting you, refusing to rent, or treating you differently because of your race, color, religion, sex, national origin, disability, or because you have children, that may be illegal under the Fair Housing Act. Many areas have a nonprofit fair housing organization that can test, document, and help with your case for free.

You can also report it to HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity. HUD says to talk with an intake specialist by calling 1-800-669-9777, or report online at hud.gov/reporthousingdiscrimination. There are time limits, so report as soon as you can. Our guide on how to file a housing discrimination complaint walks through it.

8. HUD-approved housing counselors

Housing counselors are not lawyers, but they can help you make a plan, find rent or mortgage help, and talk with your landlord or lender. For homeowners facing foreclosure, they are often the first call. HUD says to find an agency near you, call 800-569-4287 or search HUD's housing counselor locator. Our guide to finding a HUD-approved housing counselor has more.

What to bring when you ask for legal help

Gather what you can, but don't wait to call if you're missing something:

  • Every paper from your landlord or the court: the notice, the summons and complaint, and any hearing notice. Write down the date you got each one.
  • Your lease and any rules or changes you signed.
  • Proof of rent paid: receipts, money order stubs, bank or app records.
  • Texts, emails, and letters between you and your landlord.
  • Photos and repair requests if there are problems like mold, leaks, or no heat.
  • Proof of income for everyone in your home, such as pay stubs or benefit letters. Legal aid will ask about income to see if you qualify.
  • Voucher or subsidy papers if you have Section 8 or live in public or subsidized housing. Special rules apply to you. See our guide to HUD eviction rules.
  • Rent help papers if you applied for emergency rental assistance. A pending application can matter in court.

How to spot an eviction scam

  • No one can sell you a way to "stop" an eviction. Only a court can decide your case. Be careful with anyone who asks for money up front to make your eviction go away.
  • Legal aid is free. Legal aid offices, 211, LawHelp sites, and right-to-counsel programs do not charge you.
  • Check that a lawyer is real. Your state bar's website lets you look up whether a lawyer is licensed. A real lawyer will explain the fee before you pay.
  • Don't sign over your home or your rent money. Pay your rent to your landlord, or into the court if the court tells you to, not to a third party who says they will handle it.
  • Homeowners: the same goes for anyone promising to "save" your house for a fee. Our foreclosure guide covers these scams.

What to do if legal aid can't take your case

Legal aid offices often have to say no because they are full. That does not mean you have no options:

  • Ask for advice only. Many offices can give brief advice or help you fill out a form even if they can't represent you.
  • Ask about a courthouse help desk. Many housing courts have a self-help center or a "lawyer for the day" program on hearing days.
  • Use your state's LawHelp site for court forms and step-by-step guides.
  • Try a lawyer referral service, a law school clinic, or ABA Free Legal Answers (see above).
  • Apply for rent help now if the case is about unpaid rent. Paying what you owe, or showing a pending application, can change how the case ends. See our guide on how to avoid eviction.
  • Go to every court date. Bring your papers, ask the judge for more time if you need to find a lawyer, and ask about mediation.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a free lawyer for my eviction?

Call your local legal aid office. Find it with the Legal Services Corporation's find legal aid tool at lsc.gov, at LawHelp.org, or by calling 211. Say you have an eviction case and give your court date. If you live in a place with a right to counsel law, like New York City, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Washington State, Maryland, or Connecticut, you may have a right to a free lawyer if you qualify.

Who qualifies for legal aid?

Legal aid is for people with low incomes. Groups funded by the Legal Services Corporation can generally help people with income up to 125% of the federal poverty guidelines. In 2026, that is $34,150 a year for a family of three. Some offices have other funding with higher limits, so call even if you are a little over.

What is a right to counsel for tenants?

It is a law that gives tenants who qualify a free lawyer in their eviction case. Each place sets its own rules, such as income limits or covered ZIP codes. Places with these laws include New York City, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Cleveland, Boulder, Washington State, Maryland, and Connecticut.

Is a bar association lawyer referral service free?

Usually not. A lawyer referral service matches you with a private lawyer, and the first short meeting costs a small fee. For example, the Florida Bar charges no more than $25 for 30 minutes, and the Oregon State Bar no more than $50. After that, you pay the lawyer's fee unless you agree on something else.

Can someone stop my eviction if I pay them?

No one can promise that. Only a court can decide an eviction case. Be careful with anyone who asks for money up front to stop an eviction. Legal aid and right to counsel programs are free, and you can check whether a lawyer is licensed on your state bar's website.

What if legal aid says no?

Ask for brief advice or help with forms. Use your state's LawHelp site for court forms, try your court's self-help center, a lawyer referral service, a law school clinic, or ABA Free Legal Answers. Apply for rent help if you owe rent. And go to every court date, even without a lawyer.
