How to Submit Your Section 8 Application

Matt · Updated August 2026

Step 4 of the Section 8 application process. This page covers one step in depth — for the whole process end to end, see How to Apply for Section 8.

Submitting a Section 8 application is not a form you fill out at leisure. Waiting lists open on the housing authority's schedule, sometimes for a window of only a few days, and a submission that is late, incomplete, or unprovable is functionally the same as not applying. This guide covers how applications are actually submitted — the three channels PHAs use, what to have ready for each, how to prove you submitted, and the specific mistakes that get applications thrown out.

Here's the short version: Most Public Housing Authorities now take applications through an online portal, some still take them in person, and a few accept mail. Apply the moment a list opens, fill out every field, list every household member and every source of income, keep proof of submission, and apply to as many housing authorities as you're willing to move to. You are allowed to be on multiple waiting lists at once, and you should be.

First: You're Probably Filling Out a Pre-Application

Almost nobody's first form is the real application. What opens when a "waiting list opens" is usually a short pre-application — name, household size, income range, contact information, and which preferences you're claiming. It exists to establish your place in line, and it typically takes ten to twenty minutes.

The full application comes years later, when your name approaches the top of the list. That is the one that requires documents, verification, and an interview. Understanding this ordering saves a lot of anxiety: you do not need every document in hand to get on a waiting list, and waiting until you've assembled a perfect file is how people miss the window entirely.

The one thing to get exactly right on a pre-application is contact information, because everything downstream depends on the PHA being able to reach you — and the gap between the two forms can be years long.

Apply Immediately — The First Days Decide a Lot

When a large PHA opens its list, it can take tens of thousands of applications in the first week. How that volume is handled determines how much your speed matters:

  • First-come, first-served lists rank applicants by submission timestamp. Here speed is everything. Applying an hour after opening rather than three days after can be the difference of thousands of places.
  • Lottery lists accept applications during a fixed window and then randomly draw a set number of applicants onto the list. Speed matters less, but the deadline is absolute — and a lottery does not mean you can relax, because preferences are usually applied on top of the draw.
  • Some PHAs cap the list and close it the moment they reach a set number of applications, regardless of the published window.

You will not always be told which method is in use before you apply. Treat every opening as first-come, first-served. If a list has literally just opened and you need the fast version of this, read you found an open waiting list — now what, which is written for exactly that moment.

The Three Ways to Apply

Online (most common)

Most PHAs now use a web portal, often run by a third-party vendor rather than the housing authority itself. Practical preparation:

  • Create the account before the list opens if the portal allows it. Registration is often available ahead of the application window, and it removes a step from the critical few minutes.
  • Use an email address you will still have in five years, and one you actually check. A work email you lose with the job is the single most common way people fall off a list.
  • Have documents already photographed or scanned if the portal accepts uploads. Photos from a phone are usually fine; make sure all four corners and the text are legible.
  • Expect the site to be slow. Opening day traffic overwhelms these portals routinely. Do not assume a timeout means you failed — go back and check whether the submission registered before starting over, or you may end up with a duplicate.
  • Screenshot the confirmation screen before you close the tab.

In person

Some PHAs still require an in-person submission, and some hold in-person sessions alongside an online option for people without reliable internet access. If you're going in person: arrive early, bring photo ID for yourself, bring the Social Security numbers and dates of birth for every household member written down, bring a pen, and be prepared to wait for hours. Ask for a stamped or signed copy of what you submitted before you leave.

By mail

Mail submission is increasingly rare but still used by smaller housing authorities. If you're mailing an application, use certified mail with return receipt. The receipt is your proof of both submission and date, and in a first-come, first-served process the postmark may determine your position. Keep a photocopy of the completed application before you send it — you will want to know what you told them when the full application arrives years later.

Filling It Out: The Fields People Get Wrong

Completeness matters more than polish. An application with a blank field can be rejected outright, and at a PHA processing thousands of submissions nobody is going to call you to clarify.

  1. List every household member who will live in the unit, including children, including anyone not related to you, and including a member with no income. Household size drives your income limit and your voucher size; understating it can leave you with a voucher too small for your family.
  2. List every source of income — wages, self-employment, SSI, SSDI, Social Security, TANF, unemployment, pensions, child support, regular contributions from family. Irregular and small amounts still count. Income you don't report will surface during verification, and that is a much worse conversation than reporting it now.
  3. Claim every preference you qualify for. Preferences are usually claimed on the pre-application and often cannot be added later without losing your original date. This is the highest-leverage field on the entire form — see our guide to waiting list preferences before you submit.
  4. Give a mailing address you can maintain, or the address of someone who will reliably reach you. If you're currently homeless, ask the PHA how to handle this — shelters, outreach programs, and general-delivery arrangements are all commonly accepted, and having no fixed address does not disqualify you.
  5. Answer background questions honestly. A record is rarely disqualifying on its own; a discrepancy discovered later is treated as misrepresentation and is far more damaging. Our eligibility guide explains what actually triggers a denial.
  6. Write "N/A" rather than leaving fields blank where something doesn't apply, so it's clear you didn't skip it.

Submit once. Duplicate applications from the same household are a common reason for disqualification — some PHAs void all of them rather than picking one. If you're unsure whether a submission went through, check your account or call before submitting again.

Apply to More Than One Housing Authority

This is the highest-return decision in the entire process, and it is completely permitted. You can be on as many waiting lists as you want, at the same time. Housing authorities do not penalize you for it, and being selected by one does not remove you from the others.

The strategy that works: apply to the large urban PHA everyone else is applying to, and then also apply to the smaller suburban and county authorities within commuting distance. Smaller PHAs are dramatically less oversubscribed, and their lists open more often and move faster. The trade-off is a residency preference — many PHAs prioritize applicants who live or work in their jurisdiction, so an out-of-area application may rank below local ones. It is still worth submitting.

Once you have a voucher, portability lets you move it to another jurisdiction after an initial period, so getting a voucher anywhere is a route to living somewhere else later. Our voucher transfer guide covers how that works, and waiting list strategies goes deeper on multi-PHA tactics.

To find the authorities near you, start with our guide to finding your PHA, or browse the housing authority directory for the specific portals and waitlist status of individual authorities.

Keep Proof That You Applied

Years pass between the pre-application and the interview. Records get lost — sometimes yours, sometimes theirs. Before you walk away from the submission, capture:

  • The confirmation or application number, written down somewhere that isn't only in your email.
  • A screenshot or photo of the confirmation screen, including the date.
  • The confirmation email, kept in a folder you don't clear out.
  • For mail: the certified mail receipt and return receipt.
  • For in person: the stamped copy or receipt the office gives you.
  • The date you applied and which preferences you claimed, in your own notes.

If a PHA later has no record of your application, that documentation is the entire basis for getting your original date restored. Without it, you start over.

If You Need Help Applying

Two rights are worth knowing about, because they are rarely advertised on the application page:

Reasonable accommodation applies to the application process itself. If a disability makes it hard to complete an online form, get to an office, or meet a deadline, you can request an accommodation — a paper application, help completing it, extra time, or an alternative format. Ask before the deadline, not after. Our guide on requesting a reasonable accommodation covers how to make the request in writing.

Language assistance is required. Housing authorities receive federal funding and must provide meaningful access to people with limited English proficiency, which includes interpretation and translated vital documents. Ask for it directly.

Beyond that, HUD-approved housing counseling agencies help with applications for free. Our guide to finding a housing counselor explains how to locate one near you.

What Happens Right After You Submit

You should receive a confirmation, and in many cases a position number or application number. Then, in most places, a long quiet stretch. Three things keep you on the list through it:

  • Update your contact information every time it changes — with every PHA you applied to, in writing, and keep proof you sent it. Being unreachable is the leading cause of removal from waiting lists.
  • Respond fast when they do reach out. Response windows can be as short as ten business days, and letters get lost.
  • Check your status periodically. Many PHAs run purges that require you to confirm continued interest; miss one and you're off the list. Every six months is a reasonable rhythm.

For a realistic sense of the timeline ahead, see how long Section 8 actually takes.

Why Applications Get Rejected

The most common reasons have nothing to do with eligibility:

  • Missing or blank required fields
  • Submitted after the window closed, or after the application cap was reached
  • Duplicate applications from the same household
  • Household composition or income that doesn't match what verification finds
  • A preference claimed but never documented when asked
  • Unreachable contact information at the time of selection

If your application is rejected, ask for the reason in writing and request an informal review. Rejections based on clerical errors and mismatched records are common and reversible. See how to appeal a housing denial.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I apply for Section 8 in more than one city or county?

Yes. You can be on any number of waiting lists simultaneously, and applying to several is the single most effective way to shorten your wait. Note that many housing authorities give preference to applicants who live or work in their jurisdiction, so out-of-area applications may rank lower.

Can I apply for Section 8 online?

Most housing authorities now accept applications through an online portal, frequently operated by a third-party vendor. Some also offer in-person sessions, and a few smaller PHAs still accept mailed applications. The PHA's waiting list opening notice will say which channels are available.

What if I don't have an address to put on the application?

Not having a fixed address does not disqualify you. Ask the housing authority how they handle it — shelter addresses, outreach or case management programs, and general delivery are commonly accepted. Homelessness is also a preference at many PHAs, which can move you up the list.

How long does it take to hear back after applying?

You should get a confirmation within days, but the wait for your name to come up is usually measured in years rather than months. The confirmation only means you're on the list.

What happens if I submit the application twice by accident?

Contact the housing authority as soon as you notice. Duplicate applications are a common disqualification reason, and some PHAs void every copy rather than keeping one. Flagging it yourself is far safer than leaving it.
