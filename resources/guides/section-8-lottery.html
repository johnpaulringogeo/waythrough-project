Section 8 Lotteries in 2026–2027: How They Work and Which Housing Authorities Use Them

Matt · Updated October 2026

There is no national Section 8 lottery. Each local housing authority runs its own Housing Choice Voucher waiting list, and many of them use a lottery: a random computer drawing that decides who gets on the list, or in what order. In a lottery, it does not matter when you apply, only that you apply before the window closes. As of October 15, 2026, the Dallas and Seattle housing authorities take lottery applications all year. Denver's 2027 lottery closed on September 18, 2026, and its results are expected in January 2027. Chicago says it will use a lottery the next time its voucher list opens, but it has not set a date.

Here's the short version: Find out whether your housing authority uses a lottery, and sign up for its email alerts if it has them. When a window opens, apply once, with a mailing address and email you will keep checking. Never pay anyone to apply. Then apply to every other list you can, because most people who enter a lottery are not picked.

How a Section 8 lottery works

A Section 8 lottery, also called a housing choice voucher lottery or a waiting list lottery, follows the same basic steps almost everywhere:

  • The housing authority announces it. Federal rules say that when a housing authority opens its voucher waiting list, it must give public notice "by publication in a local newspaper of general circulation, and also by minority media and other suitable means." The notice must say where and when to apply, and any limits on who may apply.
  • A short window opens. You fill out a short pre-application, usually online. Windows are often just days or a couple of weeks. Some authorities, like Dallas and Seattle, keep the window open all year.
  • A computer draws names at random. Some authorities draw once to choose who goes on the waiting list. Others keep everyone in a pool and draw more names each time vouchers free up.
  • You are told the result. This may come by email, mail, or an online portal. Each authority does it differently.
  • Being picked is not a voucher yet. If your name is drawn, you fill out a full application, prove your income and household, and go through screening. Only then can you get a voucher.

The federal rule behind all this is short. A housing authority "must select participants from the waiting list in accordance with admission policies in the PHA Administrative Plan" (24 CFR 982.204). For people with the same preference, it may sort them by "date and time of application" or by "a drawing or other random choice technique" (24 CFR 982.207). That second option is the lottery.

Lottery or first-come, first-served: why housing authorities use lotteries

Housing authorities choose between two ways to order their lists. With first-come, first-served, the people who apply first get the best spots, so lines form and websites crash. With a lottery, everyone who applies in the window has the same chance.

HUD's guidance to housing authorities, Notice PIH 2012-34, explains why many pick a lottery:

  • When demand is far higher than supply. HUD says a lottery "would be a better choice for a PHA whose demand for housing assistance typically exceeds availability."
  • Timing stops mattering. Under a random method, HUD says, "the order in which an applicant submits an application has no bearing on how soon the applicant will receive assistance."
  • Safety. HUD "cautions against opening the waiting list and accepting applications for limited periods, such as a single day," because it can lead to "disorderly and unsafe" crowds.
  • Fairness for people with disabilities. HUD says first-come lists can hurt applicants with disabilities, and a lottery removes most of that problem.

If a housing authority uses a lottery to decide who gets on the list, HUD says the opening notice "must clearly state that this system will be used." So read the notice. It tells you whether speed matters.

Not every list is a lottery. For example, the Los Angeles County Development Authority's public housing lists, which were open from August 17 to September 16, 2026 and are now closed, picked people "in order of preferences and then by date and time registered." On a list like that, applying early does matter.

Section 8 lottery application: how to apply

Most lottery applications are online. Dallas and Seattle say theirs take 20 to 30 minutes, and both ask for about the same things:

  • An email address you check, and a phone number
  • A mailing address where you can get mail
  • Names, birth dates, and Social Security numbers for the people in your household
  • Your household's total yearly income

Apply only once per household. Denver says, "Only one computer entry can be submitted per household." Do not let two adults in your home each send an entry to the same housing authority.

Write down your confirmation number. You may need it to check your result.

No computer? Public libraries have free computers. Denver also offered computers at two of its Opportunity Centers. If a disability makes the online form hard to use, you can ask the housing authority for a reasonable accommodation, such as a paper form or help over the phone.

Apply to more than one housing authority. You can be on many lists at once. Federal rules say a housing authority may not refuse to put you on its list just because you applied for other housing help (24 CFR 982.205). Our guide to what to do when you find an open waiting list walks through the application step by step.

Section 8 lottery results: how to find out if you were picked

Each housing authority sends results its own way. Here is how some of them do it:

  • Denver: check DHA's lottery status page with the Social Security number you entered. If your number is drawn, DHA says you will be told "through the mail within 120 days after the date of the drawing."
  • Dallas: "If you are selected from the waiting list, you will be notified via email and asked to complete a full application."
  • Home Forward: by mail, email, and your RentCafe account, in late 2026.
  • King County: a lottery number search on KCHA's lottery page.
  • Memphis: "When your name reaches the top of the waiting list, MHA will notify you by first-class mail."

If you are picked, answer by the deadline in the letter or email. You will usually fill out a full application, turn in proof of income and identity, and have an interview. The authority then checks that you qualify.

If you are not picked, you did nothing wrong, and there is no penalty. Apply again next time. In Denver, lottery numbers expire on December 31 each year, so you must enter again every year.

Keep your contact information up to date with every housing authority you applied to. Federal rules let a housing authority remove you if you do not answer its requests for information (24 CFR 982.204). If a disability kept you from answering, it must put you back in your old place.

Preferences: who gets picked first

A lottery is not always one big drawing. Many housing authorities first sort people into preference groups, and then use the lottery inside each group. Federal rules let housing authorities set local preferences, as long as they are written in their Administrative Plan (24 CFR 982.207). Common ones are:

  • People who live or work in the city or county. A housing authority may give this as a preference, but it may not require it.
  • Working families, people with disabilities, and older adults
  • People who are homeless
  • Survivors of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking
  • Veterans

For example, New Jersey's statewide lottery gave preference to veterans, people who are homeless, people with disabilities, domestic violence survivors, and local residents, with veterans first. Seattle gives preference to households with very low incomes or who are homeless. Our guide to Section 8 waiting list preferences explains how to claim one.

Why most people are not selected

There are far more people who need help than there are vouchers. The numbers from housing authorities' own websites show how big the gap is:

  • Los Angeles, 2017: HACLA got 187,804 lottery applications and picked up to 20,000 for its waiting list.
  • Houston, 2026: about 56,000 people applied for public housing lists, and about 10,000 were expected to be added.
  • King County: KCHA drew 2,500 names in March 2020. By the end of 2024 it had called the first 1,800, and it says it is not pulling any more names from that list in 2025 or 2026.
  • Denver: DHA picked no names from its 2025 or 2026 lotteries because of limited federal funding.

Voucher funding comes from Congress through HUD. When funding is flat or cut, housing authorities can only help as many families as they can pay for, and the wait gets longer. This is why getting on a list does not mean you will get a voucher.

What you can do: apply to as many lists as you can, including nearby cities and counties. Look at public housing and project-based lists too. In Chicago, for example, CHA says those lists "are always open." Our guide to navigating housing waiting lists has more ideas.

How to avoid Section 8 lottery scams

Scammers post fake lottery and waitlist openings on social media and build websites that look official. HUD's Office of Inspector General warns that they ask for "sensitive personal information or payment to 'apply' for assisted housing," then disappear.

  • Never pay to apply. HUD's Inspector General says "housing authorities and government agencies will never ask you to pay for admission to housing assistance programs or to join a waitlist." Denver's lottery page says it in capital letters: "NEVER pay money to apply for housing!"
  • Do not answer social media ads that offer to put you on a list. Go straight to the housing authority's own website instead, or call the number listed there.
  • Do not give your Social Security number or birth date to anyone who contacts you on Facebook or other social media.
  • Check the website address. Real lottery forms are on the housing authority's own site or a portal it links to from its own site.

To report a scam, call the HUD OIG Hotline at 1-800-347-3735 or go to hudoig.gov/hotline. You can report without giving your name.

Frequently asked questions

When is the next Section 8 lottery in 2026?

There is no single national date, because each housing authority runs its own lottery. As of October 15, 2026, the Dallas and Seattle housing authorities take lottery applications all year. Denver's 2027 lottery closed on September 18, 2026, and Denver holds one lottery each year. To find your next chance, check your local housing authority's website or our Section 8 waitlist tracker.

How do I check my Section 8 lottery results?

Use the method your housing authority gave you, which is usually an online status page, an email, or a letter. Keep your confirmation number. Denver says results for its September 2026 lottery are expected to be available in January 2027 on its lottery status page.

Does winning the Section 8 lottery mean I get a voucher?

No. Being picked puts you on the waiting list or lets you apply in full. The housing authority still checks your income, household, and background, and it can only give out as many vouchers as its funding allows.

Does applying early help in a Section 8 lottery?

No. In a lottery, everyone who applies during the window has the same chance. HUD says that under a random method, the order in which you apply has no bearing on how soon you get help. Just make sure you apply before the window closes.

Can I enter more than one Section 8 lottery?

Yes. You can apply to as many housing authorities as you want. But send only one entry per household to each housing authority. Denver, for example, says only one entry can be submitted per household.

Do I have to pay to enter a Section 8 lottery?

No. Housing authorities never charge to apply or to join a waiting list. If anyone asks you for money, it is a scam. Report it to the HUD OIG Hotline at 1-800-347-3735.
