How to Avoid Eviction (Step by Step)

Matt · Updated May 2026

Most evictions can be stopped if you move early. Below are the warning signs, the rights you have, and where to get help while there's still time to use it.

Talk to your landlord right away, apply for Emergency Rental Assistance to cover back rent, call legal aid for a free lawyer, and know your rights, including your right to show up in court and ask for more time. Timing matters: most programs can't help once a judgment is entered.

Step 1: Catch the warning signs early

It's much easier to fix a rent problem before it turns into a formal notice. Watch for these:

  • You're late on rent month after month. Even a week or two late, over and over, means the rent is more than you can carry right now.
  • You're 1-2 months behind, even if the landlord hasn't said anything yet.
  • The landlord's tone changes. Repair requests go unanswered, new notices show up, or friendly texts turn into formal letters.
  • You get a "pay or quit" notice. This is the first formal step in an eviction. You have a set number of days (usually 3-5) to pay, or the landlord can file in court.
  • Your lease is ending and the landlord won't renew. Some landlords use a "no-cause" eviction when the lease runs out, especially if they know you're behind.
  • Your income dropped because of a job loss, fewer hours, or a cut in benefits.

If any of these fit, go to Step 2 today.

Step 2: Communicate with Your Landlord in Writing

A lot of landlords will work with a tenant who talks to them early. Some ways to handle that conversation:

  • Reach out before they reach out to you. Don't wait for a formal notice. If you know you'll be short next month, send an email or text immediately.
  • Be honest about the situation: "I've had reduced hours at work and I'm going to be short $500 next month. I want to work with you on this."
  • Propose a solution: Ask for a payment plan: "Can I pay $300 by the 5th and $300 by the 15th?" Or ask if you can defer payment while you apply for assistance.
  • Keep all communication in writing. Use email or text, not phone calls. Written records matter if this goes to court.
  • Be realistic about what you can pay: Don't promise more than you can deliver. It's better to propose half the rent now and half later than to promise full rent and default.

Some landlords will work with you, especially if you've been a good tenant. Some won't. Either way, you now have a written record that you tried, and that matters if the case goes to court.

Step 3: Apply for Emergency Rental Assistance (ERA) Immediately

Emergency Rental Assistance programs pay your landlord directly for back rent and sometimes future rent. Many are still taking applications. To apply:

  • Find your program: Search "[your state] emergency rental assistance" or call 211 to find your local program. You can also visit 211.org and enter your zip code.
  • Check eligibility: Most ERA programs require you to earn 50-80% of your area's median income. If you're behind on rent due to hardship (job loss, medical issues, etc.), you likely qualify.
  • Gather documents: You'll need proof of income (or lack of it), proof of rent burden (lease and past rent notices), and sometimes ID. If you're unemployed, bring bank statements showing no income.
  • Apply even if you think you might not qualify. Let the program decide. Plenty of people who expected a no end up qualifying.
  • Apply to more than one program if you can. Some states and counties have several ERA providers, and each application is another chance.
  • Follow up after applying. ERA programs can be slow. Call every 2 weeks to check your status. Submitting an application doesn't mean anyone is working on it.

Even if ERA takes 30-60 days to process, it can stop an eviction when your landlord knows the application is in.

Step 4: Get a lawyer or tenant advocate

If you get a formal eviction notice, get legal help the same day. Timelines are short: in many states you have only 3-5 days to respond to a first "pay or quit" notice. Most of these are free:

  • Legal aid. Find your state's legal aid office at lawhelp.org. They give free legal representation to low-income tenants facing eviction.
  • Tenant groups. Many cities have tenant unions or housing justice organizations that give free advice. Search "tenant rights + [your city]."
  • 211. Call or text 211 or visit 211.org for local eviction prevention programs, legal aid, and rent help.
  • Legal clinics. Many law schools, libraries, and nonprofits hold free clinics where you can talk to a lawyer.

A lawyer or advocate knows defenses you don't, can negotiate with your landlord's attorney, and can help you present your case in court.

Step 5: Know how the court case works

If the landlord files anyway, this is the usual order of things:

  • You get a court summons. It tells you when and where to appear, and it's legally binding. If you don't show up, you lose by default.
  • The hearing. You and the landlord each tell your side to a judge, usually 1-4 weeks after you're served.
  • You can ask for a continuance. If you need more time (to save money, finish an ERA application), ask the judge to push the hearing back. Judges often grant one.
  • The judge decides. If the landlord wins, you usually get 3-10 days to move before a sheriff can physically remove you.
  • Losing isn't always the end. Some states allow appeals. Ask your lawyer.

Step 6: Prepare Your Defense

If the case goes to court, bring these and plan how you'll present yourself:

  • Bring all written communication: Emails, texts, or letters showing you've tried to work with the landlord or that you've applied for assistance.
  • Bring proof of income hardship: Termination letters, pay stubs showing reduced hours, medical bills, or bank statements showing zero income.
  • Bring ERA approval letters: If you're approved or pending for emergency rental assistance, bring all documentation. Even pending ERA can influence a judge's decision.
  • Bring proof of partial payments: If you've paid any portion of rent, bring receipts or bank records showing these payments.
  • Dress neatly and be respectful. Answer the judge's questions directly and skip the excuses. "I lost my job and couldn't pay, but I've applied for assistance" is honest, and judges respond to that.
  • Have a witness if possible: A social worker, doctor, or advocate who can speak to your hardship strengthens your case.

Step 7: If You Lose, Protect Yourself

Losing the case is hard, but you still have moves to make, and you need to make them right away:

  • Ask the judge for more time to move. Some judges will grant 10-30 days extra if you ask respectfully and explain your situation.
  • Contact emergency housing programs immediately. Call 211 or visit your city's housing authority to find emergency shelter or rapid rehousing programs.
  • Start moving as early as possible. Once the judgment is final, the clock is running. Even if you disagree with the ruling, get your belongings somewhere safe.
  • Negotiate a move-out date with your landlord. Some landlords will agree to a later move-out date if you contact them after the judgment, since it saves them costly sheriff involvement.
  • Request an appeal if there are legal grounds. Your legal aid lawyer can advise whether an appeal is viable in your state.
  • Get proof of the eviction judgment. For future housing, you'll need to be honest about the eviction. But having documentation of the judgment (and proof you tried to resolve it) helps explain your situation to future landlords.
