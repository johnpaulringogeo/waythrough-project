How to Apply for Section 8 (Step by Step)

Matt · Updated July 2026

Applying for Section 8 can feel like a lot, especially when every housing authority seems to have slightly different rules. This guide covers the whole process, from finding an open waiting list to what happens after you apply. Take the steps in order.

In one paragraph: find a Public Housing Authority (PHA) with an open waiting list, gather your income and identity documents, submit your application, and wait for your name to come up. You can apply to several PHAs at once, which improves your odds. Each step is explained below.

Step 1: Find Public Housing Authorities with Open Waiting Lists

Section 8 is run locally by Public Housing Authorities (PHAs). Each PHA keeps its own waiting list, and the lists open and close on their own schedules. So the first job is finding out which PHAs near you are taking applications right now. If the waits near you are long, our guide on how to handle housing waiting lists has ways to improve your odds and shorten the wait.

Where to look:

Start with the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's (HUD) PHA Contact Directory to find the housing authorities in your area. Then check each PHA's website, or call, and ask whether the waiting list is open. You can also search online for "Section 8 waiting list open [your state]", since several nonprofits keep updated lists of open waiting lists nationwide.

Important: Don't stop at one PHA. You can apply to several housing authorities at the same time, and you should. Some smaller suburban PHAs have much shorter waits than the big urban ones.

Already know which authority you need? Our housing authority directory covers individual authorities in depth: current waitlist status, phone number, and the exact portal to apply through. Popular ones: Houston (HHA), Philadelphia (PHA), New York (NYCHA), Los Angeles (HACLA), Chicago (CHA), Milwaukee (HACM), Phoenix, Oklahoma City (OCHA), Raleigh (RHA), Jacksonville, and Boston (BHA).

Full guide to this step: Find Your Local PHA. It covers using HUD's locator, what to ask when you call, and how to check whether a waiting list is open.

Step 2: Check Your Eligibility

Before you apply, check that you meet the basic requirements:

Income. Your household income usually must be at or below 50% of the Area Median Income (AMI) for the PHA's jurisdiction. In limited cases, such as families who already get federal housing help, the limit is 80% of AMI. By law, 75% of new vouchers go to households at or below 30% of AMI (extremely low income). Use HUD's Income Limits tool to look up the specific dollar amounts for your area and household size. For a detailed explanation of how income limits work, what counts as income, and deductions that can help you qualify, see our Section 8 income limits guide. For a realistic timeline of the full process, see how long Section 8 actually takes.

Citizenship or eligible immigration status. At least one member of your household must be a U.S. citizen or have eligible immigration status. Mixed-status households may receive prorated assistance.

Background. PHAs run background checks. In most cases a criminal history doesn't automatically disqualify you, but certain offenses (especially drug-related criminal activity or sex offenses) can affect eligibility, depending on the PHA's policies. If you have a record, ask the PHA for its screening criteria before you decide you're ineligible.

Full guide to this step: Section 8 Eligibility Requirements. It covers income and asset limits, citizenship and immigration status, background screening, and what doesn't disqualify you.

Step 3: Gather Your Documents

Get your documents together before the waiting list opens so you can apply fast. Most PHAs ask for:

  1. Proof of identity for all household members: government-issued photo ID for adults, birth certificates or Social Security cards for children
  2. Social Security cards or numbers for every household member
  3. Proof of income: pay stubs (last 4-8 weeks), benefit award letters (SSI, SSDI, Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), etc.), pension statements, child support documentation
  4. Proof of assets: bank statements (last 3 months), investment account statements if applicable
  5. Verification of any deductions you're claiming: disability documentation, medical expense receipts, childcare expense receipts, dependent documentation
  6. Current lease or proof of housing situation: especially if you're homeless, in a shelter, or facing eviction
  7. Veteran documentation (DD-214) if you're claiming veteran preference

Make copies of everything. Keep the originals somewhere safe and bring copies to appointments. If you're missing a document, call the PHA. They may be able to help you get it or verify the information another way.

Full guide to this step: Housing Application Document Checklist. It covers every document a PHA can ask for, organized by category, with a printable version.

Step 4: Submit Your Application

When a waiting list opens, apply right away. Popular PHAs can get thousands of applications in the first few days. Some lists are first-come, first-served; others use a lottery.

Most PHAs now take online applications through a portal where you fill in your information, upload documents, and submit. Have your documents scanned or photographed ahead of time.

Some PHAs require in-person applications. If yours does, get there early, because lines can be long the day a list opens. Bring all your documents and expect to wait.

Mail-in applications are sometimes an option. If you mail yours, send it certified with return receipt so you can prove you applied.

Fill out every part of the application, and get it right. Incomplete applications can be rejected or delayed. If a question confuses you, call the PHA and ask instead of leaving it blank.

Full guide to this step: How to Submit Your Section 8 Application. It covers applying online, in person or by mail, the fields people get wrong, and how to prove you applied.

Step 5: Claim All Applicable Preferences

Most PHAs move certain kinds of applicants ahead on the waiting list. Common preferences include:

  • Homelessness: currently homeless or living in a shelter
  • Veteran status: you or a family member served in the U.S. military
  • Disability: a household member has a qualifying disability
  • Elderly: head of household or spouse is 62 or older
  • Local residency: you currently live or work in the PHA's jurisdiction
  • Domestic violence survivor: you're fleeing or have fled domestic violence
  • Involuntary displacement: you've been displaced by a natural disaster, government action, or other involuntary circumstance

Every PHA has its own set, so find out which ones yours offers and send proof for each one you claim. A preference can move you a long way up the list.

Full guide to this step: Section 8 Waiting List Preferences. It covers what each preference means, the documents that prove it, and how to claim one without losing your date.

Step 6: Wait (And Stay Reachable)

Once you apply, you'll get a confirmation with your position or application number. Then comes the hard part, which is waiting.

Keep your contact information current. If you move, change your phone number, or get a new email address, tell every PHA where you have an active application right away. If the PHA tries to reach you and can't, you will be removed from the list.

Answer anything the PHA sends you quickly. When your name comes up, you may have as little as 10 business days to respond, so check your mail, email, and voicemail often.

And check in every 6 months, by phone or online. Some PHAs make you update your application now and then to stay active on the list. Don't take silence as a good sign; confirm you're still on it.

Step 7: Attend Your Eligibility Interview

When your name reaches the top of the list, the PHA will contact you to determine eligibility. Usually that means an in-person interview where they verify everything on your application.

Bring updated copies of all your documents: pay stubs, bank statements, identification, and anything else the PHA asks for. If your income or household has changed since you applied, say so. The PHA will recalculate your eligibility using current information.

If you're approved, you'll get your Housing Choice Voucher along with the rules for your housing search: how long you have to find a unit, the payment standard for your area, and what you're required to do.

Step 8: Find Your Housing

With your voucher in hand, you have a set time (usually 60-120 days) to find a unit that meets two requirements: the rent must be within the PHA's payment standard, and the unit must pass a Housing Quality Standards (HQS) inspection.

Start looking the day you get it. Call landlords, check listings, look at LIHTC properties, and ask your PHA for its landlord list. If you run into trouble finding a landlord who accepts vouchers, read our guide on what to do when a landlord rejects your voucher. In many states and cities, discrimination based on voucher status is illegal. Learn about source of income protections in your area.

Once you find a unit and the landlord agrees, the PHA will schedule an inspection. If the unit passes, you sign a lease with the landlord and the PHA signs a Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) contract. Once the PHA approves the tenancy, your voucher covers its portion of the rent from the first day of the lease term. The PHA can't pay the landlord until the HAP contract is signed, which has to happen within 60 days of the lease start; then it pays back to that first day (24 CFR 982.305(c)).
