How to Transfer Your Voucher to Another Area (Portability)

Matt · Updated November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

What Is Voucher Portability?

Portability is one of the best-kept secrets in housing assistance. It's your right to move your Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher to a different city, county, or region and use it there. Your voucher isn't locked to one PHA — it can travel with you.

Think of it like this: if you have a voucher from your home city and you need to move to another state for work, family, or safety reasons, you don't have to give up your housing assistance. You can port your voucher to a receiving PHA in your new location and keep your subsidy while you find an apartment there.

This is a life-changing benefit for people who need to relocate, and it's far easier than you might think.

When Can You Port Your Voucher?

Most voucher holders have a right to port. Under 24 CFR 982.353(b), a family with a voucher can use it to rent outside the initial PHA's area, "anywhere in the United States," wherever a PHA runs a voucher program. There is no general waiting period. Only one group has to wait, and even then the PHA can say yes early.

The 12-month rule applies only to "nonresident" applicants

Under 24 CFR 982.353(c), the limit applies only if neither the head of household nor the spouse had a legal residence (a "domicile") in the initial PHA's jurisdiction when the family first applied to that PHA. If you lived there when you applied, this rule does not apply to you, and you can ask to port right away.

If you were a nonresident applicant:

  • The limit lasts 12 months from your admission. Admission is the start date of your first HAP contract, which is the first day of your first assisted lease (24 CFR 982.4). For example, if your first assisted lease started March 1, 2026, the limit ends March 1, 2027.
  • During those 12 months, you can rent anywhere in the initial PHA's jurisdiction, but you do not have a right to port.
  • The PHA can waive it. The rule says the initial PHA "may choose to allow portability during this period." Ask in writing and explain why you need to move now.
  • After the 12 months end, the limit is gone. Send your written portability request to the PHA. You don't need to give a special reason.

Exception for survivors: The 12-month limit does not apply if you or a family member is or has been a victim of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking, and the move is needed to protect someone's health or safety (982.353(c)(4)). See the VAWA section below.

Asking the PHA to let you port early

Federal rules do not list "good cause" reasons for an early move. Each PHA decides. Reasons that may help your request include:

  • Employment: A job offer or job in the new area
  • Medical: Medical care you need in another area, with a letter from your provider
  • Disability: A move related to a disability, such as an accessible unit or nearby services. You can also ask for this as a reasonable accommodation (see below).
  • Family: Moving near a caregiver or a child
  • Housing: You can't find a unit that fits your family in the PHA's area

If the PHA says no, ask for the decision in writing and ask what its Administrative Plan says about early portability.

Other move limits that apply to everyone

Separate from the 12-month rule, a PHA may adopt policies that stop a family from moving during the initial lease term or more than once in any one-year period (24 CFR 982.354(c)(2)). These limits don't apply to the VAWA moves described below. Ask your PHA if it has these policies. You also need a lawful way to end your current lease, such as the lease ending or a notice you have a right to give (982.354(b)).

How Portability Works: The Basic Process

Portability involves two PHAs: your current one (the "initial PHA") and the one in your destination area (the "receiving PHA"). They communicate with each other to transfer your voucher.

Here's the typical timeline:

  1. You notify your current PHA in writing that you want to port
  2. Your current PHA approves the port (usually takes 1-2 weeks if you meet the requirements)
  3. Your current PHA contacts the receiving PHA with your information
  4. The receiving PHA accepts or acknowledges the port (can take 1-4 weeks)
  5. You're officially ported and can begin searching for housing in the receiving jurisdiction
  6. You find and lease an apartment using your voucher with a private landlord in the receiving area
  7. The receiving PHA processes your lease and calculates your new rent portion

Total process: 30-60 days is typical, though it can sometimes be faster or slower depending on how responsive the PHAs are.

The Absorption vs. Billing Question

Here's where it gets a bit technical, but it matters. When you port, the receiving PHA has two options for how to pay for your voucher. The PHAs make this choice, not you. Under 24 CFR 982.355(c)(3), your current PHA must contact the receiving PHA before it approves your move to find out whether the voucher "will be absorbed or billed," and the receiving PHA must answer in writing.

Absorption

The receiving PHA "absorbs" your voucher, paying for it with its own funding. You become one of its regular voucher holders. Once a receiving PHA says it will absorb, it cannot change its mind later without your original PHA's consent.

Billing

The receiving PHA runs your voucher but bills your original PHA for it. The rule says the original PHA "must promptly reimburse the receiving PHA for the full amount of the housing assistance payments," plus part of the administrative fee. Day to day, you deal only with the receiving PHA: its policies apply to you, it does your inspections and recertifications, and it decides your unit size under its own subsidy standards (982.355(c)(10) and (c)(12)).

From your side, billing usually works the same as absorption. You can't negotiate absorption, and you don't need to. What matters is that a receiving PHA that is "not absorbing" still has to serve you. The next section explains how.

When the Receiving PHA Isn't Absorbing: Billing and Your Rights

Updated November 4, 2026. Some families are told, "That housing authority isn't absorbing right now," as if that ends the move. It usually doesn't.

"Not absorbing" is not a reason to turn you away

Under 24 CFR 982.355(a) and (b), the receiving PHA "must administer assistance" for a family that ports into an area it serves, and it "cannot refuse to assist incoming portable families or direct them to another neighboring PHA." A PHA that isn't absorbing bills your original PHA instead. HUD can excuse a PHA in rare cases, such as a declared disaster area, but the PHA must have HUD's approval in writing first. If more than one PHA serves the area you're moving to, the rule lets you pick which one.

When your current PHA can say no for lack of funding

Billing costs your current PHA money, so the rules let it deny a move for "insufficient funding," but only in narrow cases (24 CFR 982.354(e)(1) and 982.355(c)(5)). HUD Notice PIH 2016-09 says a PHA may deny a move for funding only if all of these are true:

  1. You are moving to a higher-cost area, where the subsidy would be bigger (for example, higher payment standards or a bigger voucher size);
  2. The receiving PHA is not absorbing you; and
  3. Without the denial, the PHA could not avoid ending help for current families this calendar year and still stay in its budget.

HUD's notice also says the PHA cannot deny the move for funding if the receiving PHA has confirmed it will absorb you, or if the area is not higher-cost. It can't deny your move so it can admit more families from its waiting list. And once it starts denying moves, it may not admit families from the waiting list until open move requests are processed.

The PHA must tell HUD. Under 982.354(e)(1), the PHA must give written notice to its local HUD office within 10 business days of deciding it has to deny moves for lack of funding. HUD's notice says that notice must include a financial analysis and a statement that the PHA has stopped issuing vouchers. If HUD finds the PHA had no grounds, the PHA must immediately process the family's move.

How long a denial lasts: the PHA's Administrative Plan must say how long your request stays open and how it will tell you when money is available. HUD's notice says that should be no later than January 1 of the next year. If that date passes and you haven't heard, write to the PHA again and ask for a decision. If you still get no answer, contact your local HUD field office. Already ported under billing? HUD says your original PHA cannot end your voucher for lack of funding, and cannot cap what it pays for you.

How to ask both PHAs in writing

Ask early, by email or letter, and keep copies. Two short requests:

To the receiving PHA

"I have a Housing Choice Voucher from [initial PHA] and want to move to your area under portability. Please tell me in writing: (1) Will you absorb or bill for an incoming family like mine? (2) What are your payment standard and the voucher size you would give a household of [number]? (3) What are your procedures for incoming portable families? I understand that under 24 CFR 982.355(b) you must assist incoming portable families even if you are billing."

To your current (initial) PHA

"I request portability of my voucher to [city/county, state]. Please tell me in writing whether you will approve the move. If you plan to deny it for insufficient funding under 24 CFR 982.354(e)(1), please give me the reason in writing, confirm that the receiving PHA is not absorbing and that the area is higher-cost, tell me the date you notified the HUD field office, and send me your policy on how long my request stays open. I also request an informal hearing if the move is denied."

Moving as a reasonable accommodation

If someone in your household has a disability and the move is related to it, such as being near medical care, an accessible unit, or a caregiver, you can ask for the move as a reasonable accommodation under fair housing law (24 CFR 100.204). HUD Notice PIH 2016-09 says PHAs must consider these requests "even if a family might otherwise be restricted from moving," such as under a one-move-per-year policy or because of insufficient funding. If the limit is the PHA's own policy, the PHA must grant the accommodation unless it would be an undue financial and administrative burden. If the limit is a federal rule, the PHA must first assess the burden and, if there is none, ask HUD for a waiver. For a funding denial, the PHA must still consider your request, and its decision can be reviewed by HUD. You can also ask for more search time on your voucher as an accommodation. Use our reasonable accommodation request template and our reasonable accommodation guide.

Initiating the Portability Process

Here's exactly what you need to do:

Step 1: Contact Your Current PHA in Writing

Send a letter or email to your current PHA requesting portability. Include:

  • Your full name and voucher number
  • The receiving jurisdiction (city/county) where you want to move
  • The reason you're moving (employment, family, safety, etc.) if you are a nonresident applicant asking to port within your first 12 months
  • Your contact information
  • The date you want the port to be effective

Sample: "I request portability of my Housing Choice Voucher to [City/County], [State], effective [date]. The reason for my move is [employment/family/medical/safety]. Please contact me at [phone] to process this request."

Send this certified mail (keep the receipt) so you have proof of delivery.

Step 2: Follow Up

If you don't hear back within a week, call your PHA's housing assistance office and confirm they received your request. Ask what else they need from you.

Step 3: Provide Documentation (If You're Asking for an Early Move)

If the 12-month nonresident limit applies to you and you're asking the PHA to waive it, send papers that support your reason:

  • Employment: Job offer letter or employment verification
  • Medical: Letter from doctor on letterhead stating medical necessity
  • Family: Documentation showing family ties in the receiving area
  • Safety: A VAWA move doesn't need a waiver. See the VAWA section below for what the PHA can ask for.

Step 4: Contact the Receiving PHA

Once your current PHA approves the port, you can reach out to the receiving PHA. However, let your current PHA contact them first if possible — this creates an official record. If the receiving PHA doesn't reach out to you within 2 weeks of your current PHA approving the port, call them directly and confirm they have your file.

Know the Receiving PHA's Contact Info

Use the HUD PHA locator (https://www.hud.gov/contactus/public-housing-contacts) to find the receiving PHA's address and phone number. Have this information before you port.

What Changes When You Port?

Moving your voucher means several things change. Be prepared for these:

Payment Standard

Each PHA sets its own "payment standard," the maximum monthly subsidy for a unit of your size (24 CFR 982.505(a)). After you port, the receiving PHA's payment standard is used, not your old PHA's. The rule says your assistance is figured "in the same manner as for other families in the receiving PHA program" (24 CFR 982.355(e)(2)), under the receiving PHA's policies (982.355(c)(10)). The receiving PHA also decides your voucher size under its own subsidy standards (982.355(c)(12)). In a higher-cost area the payment standard can be higher. In a lower-cost area it can be lower.

Your Portion of Rent May Change

Your total tenant payment (TTP) is the highest of these: 30% of your monthly adjusted income (income after allowed deductions), 10% of your monthly gross income, a welfare rent if your welfare agency sets one for housing, or your PHA's minimum rent (24 CFR 5.628). For most families, that's 30% of adjusted income.

The PHA pays the owner a housing assistance payment (HAP). Under 24 CFR 982.505(b), the HAP is the lower of the payment standard or the gross rent, minus your TTP. "Gross rent" is the rent to the owner plus any utility allowance (982.4). You pay the rest.

Example. These numbers are made up to show the math. Assumptions:

  • Monthly adjusted income: $2,000. Monthly gross income: $2,400.
  • TTP: 30% of $2,000 = $600. That's higher than 10% of gross ($240) and the minimum rent, so TTP is $600.
  • The rent includes all utilities, so there's no utility allowance and gross rent equals the rent.
  • Same 2-bedroom voucher size at both PHAs.
  • Before the move (initial PHA): Payment standard $1,100. Gross rent $1,000. The lower of the two is $1,000. HAP = $1,000 − $600 = $400. You pay $600.
  • After the move (receiving PHA, higher-cost area): The receiving PHA's payment standard is $1,500. Gross rent $1,450. The lower is $1,450. HAP = $1,450 − $600 = $850. You still pay $600.
  • Same area, unit above the payment standard: Gross rent $1,700. The lower is the $1,500 payment standard. HAP = $1,500 − $600 = $900. You pay $1,700 − $900 = $800.

The 40% cap at move-in. When the gross rent is above the payment standard, your share at move-in can't be more than 40% of your monthly adjusted income (24 CFR 982.508). In this example, 40% of $2,000 is $800, so $1,700 is the highest gross rent the 40% cap allows at move-in. Before you apply for a unit, ask the receiving PHA for its payment standard for your voucher size, and its utility allowance.

Recertification Responsibility

Once you're ported, the receiving PHA handles your annual recertification. You'll report income changes to them, not your original PHA. This is actually cleaner — one PHA, one recertification process.

Timeline Expectations

Here's a realistic timeline for porting:

  • Your request to current PHA approval: 1-2 weeks (longer if they're slow or you need to provide documentation)
  • Current PHA to receiving PHA communication: 1-2 weeks
  • Receiving PHA acknowledges port: 1-4 weeks (some PHAs are fast, some are slow)
  • Your housing search and lease: 2-8 weeks (depends on the housing market and your availability)
  • Receiving PHA approval of lease: 1-2 weeks

Total: usually 30-60 days from your initial request to having an approved lease in the new area. Some people get it done in 4 weeks; others take 3 months. Stay on top of both PHAs to keep things moving.

Tips for a Smooth Port

Start Your Housing Search Early

Don't wait for the port to be officially processed to start looking for apartments in the receiving area. Go online, attend apartment showings, and identify places you might want. Once the port is approved, you'll be ready to move fast when you find something.

Contact the receiving PHA before you move: Call them and introduce yourself. Tell them you're porting there. Ask about local rent ranges, which landlords accept vouchers, and any special requirements they have. Building a relationship makes the process smoother.

Bring your documentation with you: When you move, take a copy of your lease, your approval letter for the port, and your current PHA documentation. You may need these when you contact the receiving PHA.

Be flexible about where you look: Some neighborhoods have more landlords willing to accept vouchers than others. Ask the receiving PHA which areas have good voucher participation.

Don't give up if the first PHA is slow: Some PHAs move slowly, especially if they're understaffed. Stay persistent. Keep calling and sending documentation. For most voucher holders, portability is a right under 24 CFR 982.353(b), and the PHA has to act on your request.

Special Situation: VAWA and Safety-Based Portability

If you or a family member is or has been a victim of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking, and the move is needed to protect someone's health or safety, VAWA (Violence Against Women Act) rules give you extra protection:

  • No 12-month nonresident limit. It does not apply to these moves (24 CFR 982.353(c)(4)).
  • No PHA move limits. A PHA's limits on moving during the initial lease term or more than once a year don't apply (24 CFR 982.354(c)(2)(iii)).
  • Breaking the lease. If you had to leave in violation of the lease to stay safe, and you've met your other program obligations, you can still get a voucher from your PHA and move to another area (982.353(b)).
  • Documentation. The PHA may ask in writing for proof. You choose what to send: HUD's certification form, a letter from a victim service provider, lawyer, or medical or mental health professional, or a police or court record (24 CFR 5.2007). You have 14 business days from the day you get the written request. If you need more time, ask before that deadline. The PHA can extend it. If the deadline passes and you haven't sent anything, VAWA no longer stops the PHA from denying the move, so contact legal aid or a victim advocate right away and send the documents as soon as you can.

If you're in a safety situation, contact your local domestic violence shelter or legal aid office. They can help you navigate portability with VAWA protections and may be able to advocate with your PHA to speed up the process.

What If Your Port Is Denied?

PHAs rarely deny legitimate portability requests, but it happens. If your port is denied or delayed unreasonably, you have options:

  • Request the reason in writing from your current PHA
  • Request an informal hearing if you believe the denial is wrong
  • File a complaint with HUD if you believe the denial is discriminatory or violates your rights
  • Contact a legal aid office for help challenging the denial

After You're Ported: Life with Your Voucher in a New Area

Once you've successfully ported and leased an apartment in your new location, life continues pretty normally. You'll:

  • Pay your tenant portion of rent to your landlord each month
  • Attend annual recertification with the receiving PHA
  • Report income changes to the receiving PHA
  • Let the receiving PHA know if you move again (you might be able to port a second time, though this gets complex)

Your voucher is now tied to the receiving PHA. If you decide to move again in the future, you'd need to port again, and the receiving PHA would handle that process.

Moving Forward

Portability is one of the most powerful tools you have as a voucher holder. Whether you need to move for a job, family, safety, or better opportunities, your housing assistance doesn't have to stay behind. Reach out to your current PHA today if you're considering a move.
