Resource Guide

Benefits & Financial Support

There are a lot of benefit programs, and each has its own rules. They are also a big part of what keeps people housed: benefits replace or add to the income you use for rent, utilities, food, and healthcare. This guide covers the major programs, how they affect each other, and how they affect your housing assistance, so you can work out the combination that fits your situation.

SSI vs. SSDI: What's the Difference?

Both SSI (Supplemental Security Income) and SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) are federal disability benefits, but they work very differently.

SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance). SSDI is based on your own (or your spouse's) Social Security work history. To qualify, you must have worked enough to earn "work credits" and now be unable to work due to a disability. The amount depends on your work history: the more you worked and earned, the higher the check. SSDI has no income limit, so you can get it even if you have other income or assets. After two years on SSDI you also get Medicare (health insurance), regardless of income.

SSI (Supplemental Security Income). SSI is a needs-based program for people who are disabled, blind, or age 65+ and have little income or assets. You don't need a work history to qualify. The program is there to cover basic living costs for people who have almost nothing else. The trade-off is strict income and asset limits: currently $967/month in income (plus certain disregards) and $2,000 in countable assets ($3,000 for couples). If you exceed these limits, you lose SSI. SSI recipients automatically receive Medicaid in most states.

Which one qualifies you for housing programs? Both SSI and SSDI recipients are priority applicants for most housing programs. Section 8, public housing, and disability-specific programs (Section 811) all prioritize people receiving disability benefits.

How to apply. Both programs are administered by the Social Security Administration (SSA). Apply online at ssa.gov, by phone at 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778), or in person at your local Social Security office. The application process can take several months, and most initial applications are denied. If you are denied, you have the right to appeal, and a disability advocate or attorney can help you do it.

SNAP (Food Stamps)

SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program), commonly called food stamps, helps low-income people buy food. The amount you receive is based on your income and household size, and benefits can range from $50/month to over $1,000/month for a large household.

Who qualifies: SNAP is available to people with low income, including people working part-time, unemployed, or receiving SSI/SSDI. Assets limits vary by state but are typically around $2,250 for individuals.

How it works with housing: SNAP is separate from housing assistance. Receiving SNAP doesn't affect housing subsidies or vice versa. Your SNAP amount is based on your income and household, but housing costs don't reduce SNAP benefits (unlike some other programs). You can get both at the same time.

How to apply: Apply through your state's SNAP office. Visit SNAP's state directory to find your local office or apply online in many states.

Medicaid and Healthcare Coverage

Medicaid is health insurance for people with low income. Who qualifies and what is covered depends a lot on your state. Some states are generous; others are strict.

Who qualifies: Medicaid is available to people with low income, some disabled individuals, pregnant people, children, and parents of minor children (in many states). Many people receiving SSI or SSDI automatically qualify for Medicaid. If you don't automatically qualify, you may still be eligible based on income alone.

Why this matters for housing: Medicaid covers mental health treatment, substance use treatment, and medical care, all services that support housing stability. Many medical and mental health providers only take Medicaid, or are easier to get into if you have it. Some housing programs also prioritize or are available only to people with Medicaid coverage.

How to apply: Apply through your state's Medicaid office or health department. Many states now allow online application.

TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families)

TANF is a cash assistance program for families with low income. It provides monthly cash payments, though the amount varies dramatically by state (from a few hundred dollars to over $1,000/month depending on family size and state). TANF also often includes job training, childcare support, and other services.

Who qualifies: TANF is primarily for families with children, though some states have programs for individuals or couples. You must meet income limits and work requirements. Most states require that you be working, in job training, or searching for work to receive TANF.

Time limits: TANF has a federal time limit of 60 months (5 years) of assistance. Some states have shorter limits or count periods of receipt differently. Don't plan on TANF for the long term. It is meant to carry you for a while as you work.

How to apply: Apply through your state's social services or welfare department.

LIHEAP (Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program)

LIHEAP helps low-income people pay their heating and cooling bills. In some states, it can also help with utility deposits or assistance during emergencies when utilities are about to be shut off.

Why this matters for housing: If your rent is affordable but your utility bills are not, LIHEAP lowers your total monthly costs, and that can be what keeps you from falling behind. In some Section 811 or other subsidized housing, utility costs can be significant.

How much you get: Typically $500–$2,000 per year, but amounts vary a lot by state, household size, and available funding. Check with your state's LIHEAP office for current amounts in your area. (Note: LIHEAP funding and benefit amounts change annually. Last reviewed March 2026.)

How to apply: LIHEAP is administered by state and local agencies. Visit ACF's LIHEAP directory to find your state program.

How Benefits Interact With Each Other

Each benefit can change what you get from the others, sometimes in ways people don't expect. These are the ones to watch:

SSI asset limits. If you receive SSI, you must keep your countable assets under $2,000 ($3,000 for couples). Receiving other benefits that increase your assets (like a lump sum payment, an inheritance, or a settlement) can push you over the limit and cause you to lose SSI. Some assets don't count: your home, one vehicle, household items, and a few others are exempt. A benefits planner can tell you what counts in your case.

Income counting. Each program counts your income its own way. SNAP counts income. SSI counts income but has monthly disregards ($65 earned income disregard plus 50% of remaining earned income; unearned income is counted dollar-for-dollar). Housing subsidies count income at 30%. Some earned income is disregarded in different ways by different programs. So one extra dollar of wages can change three programs by three different amounts.

Medical expense deductions. In housing subsidies and some benefits, if you're elderly or disabled, unreimbursed medical expenses above 3% of your income can be deducted, which lowers your calculated income and so your rent. High medical bills can lower your rent. Keep receipts for doctor visits, prescriptions, medical equipment, and health insurance premiums.

Working while receiving benefits. Many programs have work incentives so you can start a job without losing your benefits right away. SSDI has the Ticket to Work program, SSI has work incentives like impairment-related work expenses. SNAP and housing subsidies have earned income disregards. Before you take a job, ask a benefits counselor what it will do to your benefits.

Benefits and Housing Subsidies

Housing programs usually count benefit payments as income. That affects you in three ways:

Rent calculation. Your rent in Section 8, public housing, or other subsidized programs is 30% of your "adjusted income." If you receive $900/month in SSI and $200 in SNAP (though SNAP is often excluded), your adjusted income is roughly $900–$1,100 depending on program rules. Your rent would be $270–$330.

How housing subsidies help. Your share is about 30% of adjusted income, and the subsidy covers the rest. Your landlord receives the subsidy portion directly. So if you receive $900/month in SSI and your rent is calculated at $270, the housing program pays the difference between $270 and fair market rent (could be $500–$1,500 depending on location). For someone on a low, fixed income, that gap is the reason Section 8 and public housing matter so much.

When benefits increase. If you get a job and your income increases, your rent in subsidized housing increases automatically. However, Section 8's Earned Income Disallowance (EID) cushions this: if you were previously unemployed or receiving welfare assistance, 100% of your increased earned income is excluded from rent calculations for the first 12 months, and 50% is excluded for months 13-24. You keep more of your paycheck while you settle into the job.

Benefits.gov: Screen for Many Programs at Once

Benefits.gov is a federal website where you can screen for multiple benefits at once. You answer questions about your income, household, and situation, and the tool tells you which benefits you likely qualify for.

Why use it: You might be eligible for benefits you have never heard of. Plenty of people qualify for several programs and only apply for one.

How to use it: Go to benefits.gov, answer the pre-screening questions honestly, and the tool will tell you which programs you might qualify for. Then you get information on how to apply.

Putting Your Benefits Together

Most people don't live on one benefit. They put several together, usually at least one for each of these needs:

Income: SSI, SSDI, TANF, wages from employment, child support, or other income sources.

Housing: Section 8, public housing, Section 811, or rapid rehousing subsidies.

Food: SNAP supplements your income so you can afford food.

Healthcare: Medicaid or Medicare keeps a medical bill from costing you your housing.

Utilities: LIHEAP helps with energy costs in some states.

Childcare (if applicable): TANF or state childcare subsidies help you work or train.

Together they make a safety net. The hard part is learning each program's rules, how they affect each other, and how to apply.

Getting Help With Benefits

If the rules don't make sense to you, ask someone who works with them every day. Places to start:

211. Dial 211 or visit 211.org to be connected to local social services and benefits assistance programs.

Legal aid. Many legal aid offices have benefits specialists who can go over your options and help with applications. Find one at LawHelp.org.

Disability advocates or centers for independent living. These organizations often have benefits counselors who can explain how benefits will affect your specific situation.

Your local Social Security office. Social Security staff can answer questions about SSI, SSDI, Medicaid, and work incentives. Call 1-800-772-1213.

Work Incentives Planning and Assistance (WIPA). If you're on SSI or SSDI and thinking about working, WIPA projects provide free, expert benefits counseling. Find one at choosework.ssa.gov.

Worked Examples: SSI, SNAP, Work, and Section 8

The rules are easier to follow with numbers attached. These examples show how the math works out in a few common cases.

Start with a common case. Say you receive $967/month in SSI and $250 in SNAP. You're on Section 8 and your rent is calculated at 30% of adjusted income. Your adjusted income is roughly $967 (SNAP is often excluded from income calculations), so your rent contribution is about $290/month. Your housing authority pays the difference between that and fair market rent. If fair market rent in your area is $1,200, the subsidy covers $910/month. You pay $290 for stable housing and the program covers $910. Without Section 8, you couldn't afford housing at all.

Now say you have the voucher and pick up part-time work earning $600/month. Under Section 8's Earned Income Disallowance (EID), if you were previously unemployed or on welfare, 100% of your new earned income is excluded from rent calculations for the first 12 months. This means your income for rent calculation purposes is still roughly $967 (your SSI), not $1,567. Your rent stays at about $290 while you keep the full $600 from working. After 12 months, 50% of your earnings are excluded for another 12 months. The point of the rule is to let you start working without your rent jumping right away.

Benefits don't all count the same way toward rent. Unearned income (SSI, SSDI, child support) counts toward rent at 100%. Earned income (wages, self-employment) is also counted but with disregards. Medical expense deductions can reduce your calculated income if you're elderly or disabled and have significant unreimbursed medical costs. Someone on SSI, SSDI, SNAP, LIHEAP, and a housing subsidy has five programs that all depend on each other. When one changes, check what it does to the other four.

State-Specific Benefit Variations

Benefits are not the same from state to state; the United States has no uniform system. The same disability doesn't qualify for the same level of support everywhere. SSI is federal, so the federal benefit is identical in all states ($967/month as of 2025), but many states add a state supplement on top of that. Some add $50/month, others add $200+. Those differences matter when you're budgeting.

TANF is even more varied. A family of three can receive $500/month in one state and $1,200/month in another for the exact same circumstances. Some states have earned income disregards (you can earn money without losing benefits), others don't. Some have time limits shorter than the federal 60-month limit. Learn your own state's rules before you plan around any of this. A benefits counselor in one state might give you advice that doesn't apply in another.

Many states also run General Assistance programs, which provide cash assistance for people who don't qualify for federal programs. Some states run large programs; others have phased them out entirely. Some states fund their own childcare assistance, and others rely on federal money. To find your state's specific benefit rules, contact your state's social services department, call 211 for local referral, or visit benefits.gov which shows results for your state. Don't assume what you heard about benefits from someone in another state applies to you.
