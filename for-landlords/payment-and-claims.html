For Landlords

Payment and Claims

Waythrough Project

If you have a voucher lease, you need to know how Housing Assistance Payments (HAP) work. This page covers the payment schedule, how the tenant and the PHA split the rent, damage claim procedures, and the protections you have as a landlord.

How HAP Payments Work

In a Housing Choice Voucher lease, the rent comes from two places: the Public Housing Authority (PHA) and the tenant.

The PHA's portion, the HAP, is a fixed amount paid directly to you every month. It is guaranteed and is not affected by changes in the tenant's job or income.

The tenant pays the difference between the PHA's payment and the full rent. That's typically about 30% of their adjusted income (income after certain deductions), with the PHA picking up the remainder (up to the fair market rent for your area).

You and the PHA work out the total rent when you negotiate the lease. It must not exceed the PHA's Fair Market Rent (FMR) for your area, and it must be reasonable next to similar units in your market. Your local PHA can give you the FMR for your area and tell you what counts as reasonable rent.

Fair Market Rent (FMR)

Fair Market Rent is set annually by HUD based on local rental market surveys. It represents the average rent for standard, adequate rental units in your area. The PHA will not exceed FMR when calculating HAP payments, regardless of what rent you might charge in the private market.

That keeps the program from overpaying and keeps the tenant's rent reasonable. For you, it means your rent has to be reasonable for your market and can't go above FMR. Most areas have different FMRs for different unit sizes (studio, 1-bed, 2-bed, etc.).

Payment Schedule and Timing

HAP payments are typically made monthly, directly to you via ACH deposit, check, or money order. The PHA's portion is paid on the 1st through the 5th of each month, depending on your local PHA's processing schedule.

The tenant is responsible for paying their portion to you. You set the due date in your lease (typically the 1st of the month), and the tenant pays their portion by then. The PHA portion arrives separately, so you may get two payments for one unit.

Some landlords set up two separate accounts or payment arrangements so tenant rent and PHA rent never get mixed up. Others just track payments by hand. Use whatever fits your bookkeeping.

If a HAP Payment Is Late

HAP payments are generally reliable, but now and then one is delayed by a processing error, a banking problem, or an administrative mix-up. If your HAP payment doesn't arrive on the expected date:

  1. Contact your PHA's rent manager or payment department within a few days to inquire about the status
  2. Check that they have the correct banking information for you
  3. Ask if there are any lease issues or paperwork problems that might delay payment
  4. Document the delay and follow up in writing if it persists beyond 5 days

Most delays get fixed quickly once you report them. If a delay is long or keeps happening, document it and take it to PHA management. For chronic payment problems, you can also contact your local housing advocacy organization or legal aid office.

What If a Tenant Doesn't Pay Their Portion?

When a voucher tenant doesn't pay their portion of the rent, you still receive the PHA's HAP payment. The unpaid portion is the tenant's debt to you, and you can collect it or evict the same as with any tenant.

The PHA's portion is guaranteed. If a tenant pays nothing, you lose only the tenant's portion, not the PHA subsidy. That is a lot less financial risk than you carry with a private-market tenant.

If a tenant is consistently late or non-paying:

  • Send a written notice of non-payment in accordance with your lease and local law
  • Notify the PHA in writing of the non-payment issue
  • Follow local eviction procedures if payment is not received
  • The PHA may require you to issue notices to the tenant, or may conduct a briefing with the tenant about their lease obligations

Most of the time, the prospect of eviction gets the rent paid. If you do have to evict, the PHA must keep paying HAP while the family still lives in the unit, until you get a court judgment allowing the eviction (24 CFR 982.311(b)), which cuts your loss further.

Damage Claims and Lease Enforcement

If a tenant damages the property beyond normal wear and tear, you can make a damage claim through the HAP lease process.

What Counts as Damage?

Damage that goes beyond normal wear and tear and results from tenant negligence or misuse can be claimed. Examples include:

  • Holes in walls from nails or items thrown
  • Broken windows from negligence
  • Stains or damage to carpet or flooring
  • Broken fixtures from misuse
  • Damage from unauthorized occupants or activities
  • Pet damage (if not disclosed or in violation of lease)

Normal wear and tear (such as small scuffs on walls, worn carpet from walking, or faded paint) is the landlord's responsibility, not the tenant's.

Filing a Damage Claim

The usual process looks like this:

  1. Document the damage: Take photos/video of any damage immediately. Include close-ups and wide shots showing context.
  2. Notify the tenant in writing: Inform the tenant of the damage and your expectations for repair or payment.
  3. Get repair estimates: Obtain quotes from contractors for the cost to repair. Get at least two estimates for significant damage.
  4. Notify the PHA: Provide the PHA with documentation of the damage, photos, estimates, and your claim for the cost.
  5. Follow local law: Many jurisdictions require landlords to provide itemized lists of damages and offer the tenant an opportunity to dispute. Follow your state/local procedures.

Collecting Damage Claims

The PHA can't do much to enforce a damage claim, but it can:

  • Require the tenant to pay from their own funds
  • Deduct from future vouchers if the tenant remains in the program
  • In some cases, condition continued assistance on payment

If the tenant doesn't pay voluntarily, you may pursue a small claims lawsuit or collection action under state law. The PHA can provide documentation of the claim but typically cannot enforce it directly.

Lease Termination and HAP Continuation

If you or the tenant terminate the lease, HAP continues through the end of the notice period in most cases. For example:

  • If you give 30-day notice to terminate, the PHA pays HAP for 30 more days
  • If the tenant gives 30-day notice to move, the PHA pays for 30 more days
  • If you evict for non-payment, the PHA typically pays through the lease termination date

That protection matters. Whether you evict or the tenant leaves on their own, you have income through the notice period, which covers your administrative costs and, typically, any repairs between tenants.

Rent Increase Procedures

If you want to increase rent during a lease, you must follow the HAP lease procedures:

  • Give written notice to the tenant (typically 30 days)
  • The new rent cannot exceed the Fair Market Rent for your area
  • Notify the PHA of the rent increase
  • The PHA will re-calculate HAP based on the new rent amount

An increase that's reasonable and doesn't exceed FMR is typically approved. The tenant still has to agree to it, though, and can end the lease rather than accept a big increase. Check your local PHA's own procedures for rent increases.

When Fair Market Rent (FMR) Changes

FMR is updated annually, typically on October 1st. If FMR goes down, the PHA's HAP payment may go down. If FMR goes up, the PHA can raise payments up to the new FMR level.

Your PHA will tell you if FMR changes. You can't control these changes, but they come on a predictable yearly schedule, so build your budget knowing HAP may adjust each October based on the new FMR data.

Taxes and Income Reporting

HAP is rental income and must be reported on your taxes. The PHA should provide you with a Form 1098-T or other documentation of annual payments for tax purposes. Keep records of all HAP payments received for your tax records.

Tenant payments are also rental income and should be reported. Many landlords find it easier to track voucher income because the PHA portion is very consistent and clearly documented.

The Protections Built Into a Voucher Lease

A Housing Choice Voucher lease gives you these protections as a landlord:

  • The HAP payment is guaranteed and doesn't depend on the tenant's behavior or circumstances.
  • Payment continues through lease termination: you get paid through the notice period even if you evict.
  • The PHA can pressure the tenant to pay or follow the lease.
  • The HAP lease spells out who is responsible for what.
  • The PHA has a formal process for documenting tenant-caused damage.
  • The PHA oversees compliance with fair housing laws.

Add guaranteed income to that list and a voucher lease is relatively low-risk next to a private-market rental.
