For Landlords

Payment and Claims

Waythrough Project

If you have a voucher lease, you need to know how Housing Assistance Payments (HAP) work. This page covers the payment schedule, how the tenant and the PHA split the rent, what to do about tenant damage, and the protections you have as a landlord.

How HAP Payments Work

In a Housing Choice Voucher lease, the rent comes from two places: the Public Housing Authority (PHA) and the tenant.

The PHA's portion, the HAP, is paid directly to you every month. It is not a fixed amount. The PHA recalculates it when the family's income or household changes at a reexamination, and when the rent or the payment standard changes. If the tenant loses a job and their income drops, their share goes down and the PHA's share goes up to cover it (24 CFR 982.505(b), 982.451(b)(1)).

The tenant pays the difference between the PHA's payment and the full rent. That's typically about 30% of their adjusted income (income after certain deductions). The PHA picks up the rest, up to its payment standard for the unit size.

You propose the rent and the PHA has to approve it. It must be reasonable next to similar unassisted units in your market (24 CFR 982.507). It can be higher than the PHA's payment standard, but then the tenant pays the extra, and when the family first moves in, their total share can't be more than 40% of their adjusted monthly income (24 CFR 982.508). Your local PHA can tell you its payment standards and how it decides what counts as reasonable rent.

Fair Market Rent (FMR)

HUD sets Fair Market Rents every year for each area. An FMR is HUD's estimate of rent plus utilities at the 40th percentile for standard-quality units in the area (24 CFR 888.113), so it sits a bit below the middle of the market. The PHA uses the FMR to set its payment standards, generally between 90% and 110% of FMR (24 CFR 982.503). The payment standard, not the FMR, is the most the PHA's share can be, and some PHAs use higher exception payment standards in expensive neighborhoods.

So the FMR doesn't cap your rent. Your rent has to be reasonable for your market, and the PHA's share stops at the payment standard. Most areas have different FMRs and payment standards for different unit sizes (studio, 1-bed, 2-bed, etc.).

Payment Schedule and Timing

HAP payments are made monthly, directly to you, usually by direct deposit (some PHAs still mail checks). There's no federal payment day. The HUD HAP contract has the PHA pay at the beginning of each month and promptly when due, and most PHAs pay at the start of the month. Your PHA's payment schedule sets the exact date.

The tenant is responsible for paying their portion to you. You set the due date in your lease (typically the 1st of the month), and the tenant pays their portion by then. The PHA portion arrives separately, so you may get two payments for one unit.

Some landlords set up two separate accounts or payment arrangements so tenant rent and PHA rent never get mixed up. Others just track payments by hand. Use whatever fits your bookkeeping.

One more thing to know: if the PHA pays you money you weren't entitled to (say, HAP for a month after the family moved out), the HAP contract lets it take that overpayment out of later payments it owes you, including payments on your other Section 8 contracts (24 CFR 982.311(d); form HUD-52641).

If a HAP Payment Is Late

HAP payments are generally reliable, but now and then one is delayed by a processing error, a banking problem, or an administrative mix-up. If your HAP payment doesn't arrive on the expected date:

  1. Contact your PHA's rent manager or payment department within a few days to inquire about the status
  2. Check that they have the correct banking information for you
  3. Ask if there are any lease issues or paperwork problems that might delay payment
  4. Document the delay and follow up in writing if it persists beyond 5 days

Most delays get fixed quickly once you report them. If a delay is long or keeps happening, document it and take it to PHA management. After the first two months of the contract, the HAP contract makes the PHA pay you a late penalty if late fees are normal in your market, you charge them to all your tenants, and you charge this tenant for late rent too (24 CFR 982.451(b)(5) and the HAP contract, form HUD-52641). For chronic payment problems, you can also contact your local housing advocacy organization or legal aid office.

What If a Tenant Doesn't Pay Their Portion?

When a voucher tenant doesn't pay their portion of the rent, you still receive the PHA's HAP payment. The unpaid portion is the tenant's debt to you, and you can collect it or evict the same as with any tenant.

The PHA's portion doesn't depend on the tenant paying theirs. If a tenant pays nothing, you lose only the tenant's portion, not the PHA subsidy. That is a lot less financial risk than you carry with a private-market tenant.

If a tenant is consistently late or non-paying:

  • Send a written notice of non-payment in accordance with your lease and local law
  • Notify the PHA in writing of the non-payment issue
  • Follow local eviction procedures if payment is not received
  • The PHA may require you to issue notices to the tenant, or may conduct a briefing with the tenant about their lease obligations

Most of the time, the prospect of eviction gets the rent paid. If you do have to evict, the PHA must keep paying HAP while the family still lives in the unit, until you get a court judgment allowing the eviction (24 CFR 982.311(b)), which cuts your loss further.

Tenant Damage and Lease Enforcement

If a tenant damages the property beyond normal wear and tear, the tenant owes you for it, the same as any renter. The PHA does not pay damage claims. HUD ended the old owner-claim system for tenant-based vouchers in 1995, and the HAP contract says the PHA won't pay any other claim you have against the family. Your cushion is the security deposit and, where one exists, a local landlord mitigation fund.

What Counts as Damage?

Damage that goes beyond normal wear and tear and results from tenant negligence or misuse is the tenant's responsibility. Examples include:

  • Holes in walls from nails or items thrown
  • Broken windows from negligence
  • Stains or damage to carpet or flooring
  • Broken fixtures from misuse
  • Damage from unauthorized occupants or activities
  • Pet damage (if not disclosed or in violation of lease)

Normal wear and tear (such as small scuffs on walls, worn carpet from walking, or faded paint) is the landlord's responsibility, not the tenant's.

Documenting Damage

The usual process looks like this:

  1. Document the damage: Take photos/video of any damage immediately. Include close-ups and wide shots showing context.
  2. Notify the tenant in writing: Inform the tenant of the damage and your expectations for repair or payment.
  3. Get repair estimates: Obtain quotes from contractors for the cost to repair. Get at least two estimates for significant damage.
  4. Tell the PHA: Send the PHA copies of your photos and notes. It won't pay for the damage, but damage the family caused that fails the inspection standard is a breach of the family's program obligations, and the PHA can act on that.
  5. Follow local law: Many jurisdictions require landlords to provide itemized lists of damages and offer the tenant an opportunity to dispute. Follow your state/local procedures.

Collecting What You're Owed

When the tenant moves out, you can apply the security deposit to damage and unpaid rent, subject to state law, and you must give the tenant a written list of each charge (24 CFR 982.313). If the deposit doesn't cover it, you can go after the balance yourself through small claims court or a collection action under state law. The PHA can't collect it for you, but it can:

  • Waive your duty to fix an inspection failure the family caused (beyond ordinary use), so your HAP isn't held back over it (24 CFR 982.404(a)(4))
  • End the family's assistance for a family-caused inspection failure or for serious or repeated lease violations (24 CFR 982.404(b), 982.551, 982.552)

Ask whether your PHA, city, or state runs a landlord mitigation or risk-reduction fund. Some reimburse tenant-caused damage above the deposit, up to a cap.

Lease Termination and HAP Continuation

If you or the tenant terminate the lease, HAP continues through the end of the notice period in most cases. For example:

  • If you give 30-day notice to terminate, the PHA pays HAP for 30 more days
  • If the tenant gives 30-day notice to move, the PHA pays for 30 more days
  • If you evict for non-payment, the PHA typically pays through the lease termination date

That protection matters. Whether you evict or the tenant leaves on their own, you have income through the notice period, which covers your administrative costs and, typically, any repairs between tenants.

Rent Increase Procedures

If you want to raise the rent, you must follow the HAP contract and lease rules:

  • You can't raise the rent during the initial lease term (usually the first year)
  • After that, give the tenant written notice as your lease and state law require
  • Notify the PHA in writing at least 60 days before the new rent takes effect (24 CFR 982.308(g)(4))
  • The PHA checks rent reasonableness again before any increase. The new rent can't be more than the reasonable rent, or more than you charge for comparable unassisted units
  • The PHA recalculates the HAP based on the new rent. If the new rent is above the payment standard, the tenant pays the difference

An increase that holds up next to comparable units is typically approved. The tenant can still decide to move rather than pay more. Check your local PHA's own procedures for rent increases.

When Fair Market Rent (FMR) Changes

FMR is updated annually, typically on October 1st. When it changes, your PHA reviews its payment standards, and it must bring them back within 90% to 110% of the new FMR within three months if needed (24 CFR 982.503(c)(3)). A higher payment standard gets applied to your tenant's HAP no later than their next regular or interim reexamination, or one year after the increase, whichever comes first. If the payment standard goes down, the PHA can keep using the old one for a family that stays in your unit. If it does lower it, the cut can't start until two years after the decrease, and the family gets 12 months' written notice (24 CFR 982.505(c)).

Ask your PHA when it updates its payment standards. You can't control these changes, but they come on a predictable yearly cycle, so build your budget knowing the HAP can shift after new FMRs come out.

Taxes and Income Reporting

HAP is rental income and must be reported on your taxes. PHAs generally report the HAP they paid you for the year on Form 1099-MISC, in box 1 (Rents). Keep records of all HAP payments received for your tax records.

Tenant payments are also rental income and should be reported. Many landlords find it easier to track voucher income because the PHA portion is very consistent and clearly documented.

The Protections Built Into a Voucher Lease

A Housing Choice Voucher lease gives you these protections as a landlord:

  • The PHA keeps paying its share even when the tenant doesn't pay theirs, and if the tenant's income drops, the PHA's share goes up.
  • Payment continues through lease termination: you get paid through the notice period even if you evict.
  • The PHA can pressure the tenant to pay or follow the lease.
  • The HAP lease spells out who is responsible for what.
  • The PHA can hold the family responsible for damage that fails inspection, up to ending their assistance.
  • The PHA oversees compliance with fair housing laws.

Add a dependable government-backed payment to that list and a voucher lease is relatively low-risk next to a private-market rental.
