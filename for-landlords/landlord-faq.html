For Landlords

Landlord FAQ

Waythrough Project

These are the questions landlords ask most about the Housing Choice Voucher program. If yours isn't here, your local Public Housing Authority can answer it for their program.

About Voucher Tenants and Screening

Q: Can I screen voucher tenants differently than private market tenants?

A: You can use the same screening criteria for everyone. What you can't do, in places with source of income protections (currently 19 states plus D.C. and many cities), is discriminate based on source of income, meaning you can't turn someone down just because they use a voucher. Credit history, rental history, criminal background, and income verification are all fair game, the same as with a private-pay tenant. Your standards just have to apply equally to every applicant, however they pay the rent. Some landlords find the PHA's own screening (employment, income verification) means they need less private background checking.

Q: What happens if a voucher tenant has poor rental history?

A: You can review rental history and deny an applicant for legitimate reasons (evictions, a history of non-payment, property damage). Before you deny a voucher applicant, make sure you would also deny a private-market applicant with the same record. Write down your screening criteria and your reason for each denial. Some voucher tenants may have moved around a lot because of past circumstances, so judge each application on its own merits.

Q: Can I require a co-signer for a voucher tenant?

A: Generally, no. Co-signers aren't part of the Housing Choice Voucher lease structure. The PHA is in effect the guarantee, and asking for a co-signer on top of that isn't standard practice. Your lease should follow the PHA's HAP lease template, which has no co-signer provision. If something about a tenant worries you, deal with it in your normal screening instead.

Q: How does the PHA screen voucher tenants?

A: The PHA verifies income and employment and runs background checks on every voucher applicant. It confirms the applicant meets the program's eligibility rules (income limits, citizenship status, etc.). How far the background check goes depends on the PHA, but most look at criminal background and rental history. You can ask the PHA how it screened a particular applicant.

Q: Are voucher tenants required to be employed?

A: No. You don't need a job to hold a voucher. Vouchers generally go to households at or below 50% of Area Median Income, and that includes people who are unemployed, seniors on fixed incomes, people with disabilities who aren't working, and others. Their income may come from public benefits, disability, a job, or other sources, and some families have no income at all. The PHA verifies where the tenant's income comes from.

About Lease Terms and Occupancy

Q: How long does a voucher lease typically last?

A: Most Housing Choice Voucher leases run one year, though they can be shorter or longer. You negotiate the term with the tenant and the PHA. One year is the norm because 24 CFR 982.309 generally requires an initial lease term of at least one year, unless the PHA approves a shorter one. After that, you and the tenant can agree to renew for more time. Once you both sign, the lease is binding for that term.

Q: Can I require a security deposit from a voucher tenant?

A: Yes, the same as for any tenant. Keep the amount reasonable and in line with what you normally charge. Your state's security deposit law controls how much you can charge, how you hold the money, and how you return it or take deductions. Under voucher rules, the PHA may bar deposits that are higher than local market practice or higher than what you charge unassisted tenants (24 CFR 982.313(b)), and state law may set other limits.

Q: Can I limit the number of occupants in the unit?

A: Yes, within legal limits. Local housing codes and state law set occupancy limits, and fair housing law bars limits that unfairly shut out families with children. HUD's general rule of thumb is two people per bedroom, adjusted for things like bedroom size and the layout of the unit. For the voucher program, the unit must have at least one bedroom or living/sleeping room for every two people (24 CFR 5.703(d)(5)), and the PHA's own subsidy standards decide what size voucher the family gets. Whatever standard you use, apply it equally to every applicant, voucher or private pay.

Q: Can I prohibit pets in a voucher unit?

A: Yes. A no-pet policy works the same as in a private-market rental, as long as it follows fair housing law on assistance animals. A trained service animal has to be allowed as a reasonable accommodation no matter what your pet policy says. Emotional support animals are where the rules have shifted. HUD rescinded its earlier assistance-animal guidance in 2025, and in May 2026 it said it will bring fair housing cases only over animals trained to do disability-related work, not untrained emotional support animals. But the Fair Housing Act itself didn't change, tenants can still sue in court, and your state's law may protect emotional support animals. Treat every animal request as a reasonable accommodation request and get legal advice before you turn one down. Apply your pet rules equally to all tenants, voucher or private.

Q: What if a voucher tenant wants to add an occupant (roommate, partner) during the lease?

A: Any change to who lives in the household needs PHA approval. The new occupant has to pass the same screening and income verification the original tenant did. Tell the PHA about the request and it will run its approval process. The lease may need an amendment. Don't let anyone move in without telling the PHA; occupancy changes go through the proper channels.

About Payment and Rent

Q: When do HAP payments start?

A: HAP payments typically begin the month after the lease is executed and the property passes HQS inspection. Sign a lease in March, pass inspection in early March, and HAP would begin in April. The exact timing depends on how fast your local PHA processes paperwork, so ask about their usual start date while you're going through the application.

Q: What if the tenant doesn't pay their portion of rent?

A: You still receive the PHA's portion. The tenant's portion is the tenant's obligation. If they don't pay, follow your lease and local law on non-payment: you can serve a notice to cure (pay the rent or move) and go ahead with eviction if needed. A tenant who breaks the lease may lose their voucher. Either way, the guaranteed HAP income keeps coming even when the tenant doesn't pay their share.

Q: Can I increase rent during the lease?

A: Not during the initial lease term. The HAP contract bars rent increases then. After that, you can raise the rent at renewal or as your lease allows. Give the tenant the written notice your lease and state law require, and notify the PHA at least 60 days before the new rent takes effect (24 CFR 982.308(g)(4)). The PHA checks that the new rent is still reasonable next to comparable unassisted units. It doesn't have to stay under Fair Market Rent, but if it's above the PHA's payment standard, the tenant pays the difference. The tenant can accept the increase or move.

Q: What if HAP payments are late?

A: Call your PHA's rent manager right away if a payment doesn't arrive on the expected date. Most delays come from processing errors or banking problems and get fixed quickly. If it keeps happening, take it up with PHA management. That said, a delay of a few days now and then happens in any government payment system. Keep a record of the delays and follow up in writing if they continue.

About Inspections and Maintenance

Q: How often must my property be inspected?

A: After the initial HQS inspection, the property has to keep passing periodic HQS inspections. At least every two years (some PHAs do it every year), on the PHA's schedule, an inspector comes back to check that it still meets Housing Quality Standards. HAP payments continue only if you keep passing. If the property fails a periodic inspection, you have 30 days to make repairs and request a re-inspection.

Q: What if I need major repairs between inspections?

A: You're responsible for keeping the property up to HQS standards for the whole lease. If a major system fails (furnace, water heater, plumbing), fix it promptly so the unit stays habitable. The tenant can ask for a repair, and if you don't make it, they can contact the PHA, which may delay or stop HAP payments until the work is done. Fixing big problems fast protects you as much as the tenant.

About Termination and Eviction

Q: Can I evict a voucher tenant for lease violations?

A: Yes, the same as with any tenant, as long as you follow fair housing law and your state's eviction procedures. The PHA would rather you resolve problems without eviction if you can, and many PHAs offer mediation. If you do evict, the PHA must keep paying HAP while the family still lives in the unit, until you get a court judgment allowing the eviction (24 CFR 982.311(b)). So the money doesn't stop during the eviction. You can't evict for the PHA's share if the PHA is late paying it; the tenant owes only their own share (24 CFR 982.310(b)). Give proper notice and follow your state's procedures.

Q: What happens to the voucher if I evict a tenant?

A: The voucher belongs to the tenant, not the property. An evicted tenant loses their placement at your property, but they can move to another rental and port their voucher there. Once they move out, your unit is no longer a voucher unit (unless the PHA places another voucher holder there).

Q: Can I refuse to renew a voucher lease?

A: Yes, you can choose not to renew when the lease expires. You must give proper notice (typically 30 days, depending on local law), but you don't need a reason. What you can't do is refuse because of source of income (voucher use) or any other fair housing-protected class. If you don't renew, the tenant must be out by the lease end date, and the PHA stops payments. This is different from evicting someone mid-lease. At renewal you're simply choosing not to continue.

About Source of Income Protections

Q: What is source of income protection, and how does it affect me?

A: In many places, source of income (SOI) is a protected class under fair housing law. That means you can't deny housing to someone, or treat them worse, just because they use a Housing Choice Voucher. You can still screen, as long as voucher and non-voucher applicants face the same standards. Some states and cities have explicit SOI protections and others don't, so check your local fair housing law. Staying within SOI rules is simple: use the same screening criteria for every applicant, wherever their rent money comes from.

General Program Questions

Q: How do I get started accepting vouchers?

A: Call your local Public Housing Authority and tell them you want to be a voucher landlord. They'll explain their program, what participation requires, and how to apply. You'll need a property that meets Housing Quality Standards, and you'll sign the HAP lease agreement. The PHA schedules an initial HQS inspection, and once the property passes, you can take a voucher tenant. From first contact to a signed lease usually takes 4-8 weeks.

Q: Is there a maximum number of voucher units I can own or manage?

A: There's no federal limit. Some local PHAs may have policies capping the percentage of an owner's portfolio that can be voucher units. This varies by PHA, so ask yours. Plenty of institutions and large landlords run large numbers of voucher units without trouble.

Q: Can I accept multiple voucher tenants in different units of the same building?

A: Yes. You can have voucher leases in several units of the same building or portfolio. Each unit needs to pass its own HQS inspection and have its own HAP lease. Some landlords find that running several voucher units is efficient and steadies income across their portfolio.
