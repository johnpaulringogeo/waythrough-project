Project-Based vs. Tenant-Based Vouchers: What's the Difference?

Matt · August 12, 2026 · Updated November 4, 2026 · 11 min read

Tenant-based vouchers (Housing Choice Vouchers) go with you wherever you move. Project-based vouchers are tied to a specific apartment — if you leave, the subsidy stays behind. Tenant-based vouchers offer more flexibility and choice, while project-based vouchers often have shorter waiting lists and guarantee a specific unit. After one year in a project-based unit, you can request a tenant-based voucher to move.

Tenant-Based Vouchers (HCV)

This is what most people mean when they say "Section 8." A tenant-based Housing Choice Voucher is issued to you, and you find your own apartment in the private market. The voucher pays the difference between your rent portion (usually about 30% of your adjusted income) and the approved rent.

Pros: You choose where to live. You can move and take the voucher with you. You can port to another PHA's jurisdiction. You have more control over your housing decisions.

Cons: You have to find a landlord who accepts vouchers. The housing search can be stressful and time-consuming. Waiting lists are often very long — years in many areas.

Project-Based Vouchers (PBV)

Project-based vouchers are attached to specific units in specific buildings. The subsidy is tied to the apartment, not to you. When a PBV unit becomes available, it's filled from the PHA's waiting list (usually a separate list from the HCV list).

Pros: No housing search — you move into a specific unit. Waiting lists are often shorter. The building is already approved and inspected. Some PBV properties offer on-site services (case management, job training).

Cons: You can't choose which apartment or neighborhood. If you want to move, the subsidy doesn't come with you (though you can request an HCV after one year). The building management controls more of your experience.

The One-Year Conversion Right

Here's something many people don't know: if you've lived in a project-based unit for at least one year, you have the right to request a tenant-based voucher and move. HUD's rule, 24 CFR 983.261, says the PHA "must offer the family the opportunity for continued tenant-based rental assistance." That is usually a regular voucher, but the PHA's Administrative Plan can say it will offer "other comparable tenant-based rental assistance" instead.

This makes PBV a potential stepping stone. Get into stable housing faster through a PBV unit, live there for a year, then convert to a tenant-based voucher if you want to choose your own apartment.

How to Use the One-Year Right, Step by Step

Updated November 4, 2026: this section is new. It explains how to ask, how the wait works, and the different rules for buildings converted under RAD.

1. Ask the PHA first, in writing, before you tell your landlord

The order matters. The rule says that before you give notice to end your lease, you "must contact the PHA to request a voucher" if you want to move with help. Send a dated letter or email to the PHA that runs your project-based voucher (not just the building office). Keep a copy. The PHA's Administrative Plan must explain how you contact it and how it keeps track of families waiting, so ask for that part of the plan too.

2. Don't give notice until you have the voucher

When the PHA gives you the voucher, you then give your landlord advance written notice that you will move out, following your lease, with a copy to the PHA. If you move out before you finish one year of PBV help, you give up this right (unless the safety exception below applies). If you give notice before the PHA has a voucher for you, you could end up with no unit and no voucher.

3. If no voucher is free yet, you get priority for the next one

If a voucher is not available right away, the rule says the PHA "must give the family priority to receive the next available opportunity" for tenant-based help. That means you go ahead of new families from the waiting list, but you still wait for a voucher to come open. Ask the PHA in writing for the date it logged your request and where you stand.

4. Know that some PHAs limit how many turnover vouchers go to these moves

Vouchers open up when other families leave the program. How long you wait depends on how often that happens and on the PHA's funding. Your PHA's Administrative Plan explains how it handles these requests. For buildings converted under RAD (below), HUD lets PHAs cap these moves:

  • RAD project-based voucher (PBV) buildings: if PBV units are more than 20% of the PHA's voucher program, the PHA does not have to give more than three-quarters of its turnover vouchers in a year to these requests.
  • RAD Project-Based Rental Assistance (PBRA) buildings: the PHA does not have to give more than one-third of its turnover vouchers in a year to these families, and the PHA and owner can limit moves to 15% of the units in one building per year.

HUD says a PHA that uses a cap must keep a priority list in the order requests came in. HUD also says families in regular, non-RAD PBV units are not counted in these caps.

5. RAD conversions: one year for PBV, two years for PBRA

If your building used to be public housing and converted under the Rental Assistance Demonstration (RAD), HUD calls this right "Choice Mobility." HUD's RAD resident fact sheet says:

  • If your building uses PBV: you can ask for a tenant-based voucher after living there 1 year after it became RAD/Section 8.
  • If your building uses PBRA: you can ask after living there 2 years after it became RAD/Section 8. HUD's guidebook adds that a PBRA family must also qualify for the PHA's voucher program, such as its income limits.

HUD's guidebook counts the time from the later of the date the building converted or the date you moved in. HUD says you get priority for the next available voucher, you do not need a full new Section 8 application, and you can ask "even if the Section 8 waiting list is closed." The right "does not expire" as long as you still live in the property. A few PHAs have no voucher program, so their RAD buildings have no Choice Mobility. Not sure which kind your building is? Ask the property office or PHA in writing.

6. What to do while you wait

  • Stay put and stay in good standing. Keep paying your rent share, go to recertifications, and report changes on time. Problems can lead to a denial.
  • Check in every 60 to 90 days in writing, and ask for your place on the priority list each time.
  • Get ready to search. Look at rents, gather documents, and read our voucher housing search tips. When the voucher comes, its search time has an end date. If you need more time, ask in writing for an extension before that date. The rule says that if the search time runs out, the PHA gives the voucher to the next family, so ask the PHA in writing whether you can make a new request.
  • Disability? You can ask for a reasonable accommodation, such as more search time. See how to request a reasonable accommodation.
  • Safety emergency? If you or a household member is a victim of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking and you need to move to stay safe, the rule says you don't have to give the owner advance notice or contact the PHA first. If you had at least one year of PBV help, the PHA must still offer you continued tenant-based help. With less than a year, ask about the PHA's Emergency Transfer Plan.

Dear [PHA name],

I live at [address, unit] with project-based voucher assistance. I have received this assistance since [date], which is more than [one year / for RAD PBRA: two years]. Under 24 CFR 983.261 [or: the RAD Choice Mobility rules], I am asking for a tenant-based voucher so I can move with continued assistance.

I will not give my landlord notice until you issue the voucher. If a voucher is not available now, please confirm in writing that I have priority for the next one, the date you logged this request, and where I am on your list. Please also send me the part of your Administrative Plan that covers these requests.

Thank you, [Name, phone, email, date]

If the PHA says no or doesn't answer, ask for the reason in writing and whether you can ask for an informal review. Legal aid can help; see our guide to free legal help for tenants. For RAD questions, HUD's RAD office takes email at rad@hud.gov.

Which Should You Apply For?

Apply for both. There's no penalty for being on multiple lists, and each increases your chances of getting housed sooner. If you're in urgent need of housing, project-based units may get you housed faster.

Consider PBV if: you need housing quickly, you're comfortable in the specific location and building, the property offers services you need, or the HCV waiting list is extremely long in your area.

Consider holding out for HCV if: you have a specific area or school district you need to be in, you have a good housing situation that can last while you wait, or mobility and choice are priorities for you.

How to Find Project-Based Units

Your PHA's website should list project-based properties. Affordablehousing.com also lists these units. Call the management office of PBV properties directly — they maintain their own waitlists and some may have immediate availability.
