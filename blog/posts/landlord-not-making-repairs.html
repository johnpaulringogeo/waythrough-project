Your Landlord Won't Fix Things? Here's What to Do

Matt · June 3, 2026 · 8 min read

If your landlord won't make repairs, you have legal options. Start by documenting the issue in writing (email or letter). If they still don't respond, you can file a complaint with your local code enforcement or health department, contact legal aid, withhold rent in some states, or use rent escrow. Section 8 tenants have the additional protection of PHA inspections that can force repairs.

Do this first: put it in writing.

Copy our free repair request letter, or fill in the letter generator and print it. Send it, keep a copy, and if the deadline passes with no repair, find who to call in your city.

When repair requests go nowhere

Requests get ignored, promised repairs never happen, and some landlords stop answering the phone the moment you report a problem. That happens a lot, and you have more options than it feels like.

Every state has habitability laws that require landlords to keep rental properties safe and livable. When a landlord doesn't, the law gives you ways to force the repair.

Step 1: Document Everything in Writing

Before you go over the landlord's head, build a paper trail. Send a written repair request. Email works, and a letter by certified mail is better.

Say what the problem is, when it started, and how it affects your health or safety. Add photos if you can, and give a reasonable deadline for the repair (usually 14-30 days depending on urgency).

Keep copies of everything, including screenshots of text messages and saved emails. If this goes further, that's your evidence.

Step 2: Know What Your Landlord Must Fix

Landlords are legally required to maintain:

Structural integrity: roof, walls, floors, foundation, stairs, and railings in good repair.

Plumbing: working hot and cold water, no leaks, functioning toilets and drains.

Heating: a working heating system (AC requirements vary by location).

Electrical: safe, working electrical systems with no exposed wiring.

Pest control: addressing infestations of roaches, rats, bed bugs, or other vermin.

Common areas: hallways, stairwells, and shared spaces must be safe and clean.

Locks and security: working locks on all exterior doors and ground-floor windows.

Step 3: Escalate If They Don't Respond

Contact code enforcement. Every city and county has a code enforcement or building inspection department. File a complaint. An inspector will visit your unit and can issue violations against the landlord with deadlines and fines.

Contact the health department. For issues like mold, pest infestations, sewage problems, or no hot water, the health department can intervene and order repairs.

Contact legal aid. Free legal aid organizations handle tenant rights cases. They can write demand letters, represent you in housing court, and help you sort out your options. Find your local legal aid at lawhelp.org.

If You're on Section 8

Section 8 tenants have an extra tool: your PHA. Call your housing authority and report the conditions. The PHA can:

Conduct a special inspection outside the regular inspection cycle to document the violations.

Abate the landlord's HAP payments: stop paying the landlord's portion of the rent until repairs are made. Landlords tend to move quickly once that money stops.

Terminate the HAP contract entirely if violations are severe and the landlord refuses to act. In this case, you'd get a new voucher to move.

Your landlord cannot retaliate against you for contacting the PHA about repairs. Federal law protects you here, and so does state law in most states.

Rent Withholding and Escrow

Some states allow tenants to withhold rent or pay into an escrow account when the landlord fails to make necessary repairs. It can work, but it's risky. If you don't follow your state's exact procedures, you could face eviction.

Generally, the process looks like: written notice to landlord → waiting period → tenant deposits rent with the court instead of the landlord → landlord must make repairs to get the money released.

Do not try rent withholding until you know your state's specific rules. Legal aid can walk you through them.

If you're worried about retaliation

Many tenants don't report repair issues because they're afraid of retaliation, such as rent increases, eviction notices, or harassment. Most states have anti-retaliation laws that protect tenants who exercise their legal rights.

If your landlord tries to evict you, raise your rent, or cut services within a certain period (usually 6-12 months) after you filed a complaint, that's presumed to be retaliation and is illegal.

Document the timeline: when you reported the issue, when the landlord took retaliatory action. This sequence is your strongest evidence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who do I report my landlord to for not fixing things?

For a regular private rental, report it to your city or county code enforcement office. Some places call it housing inspection, building inspection, or the health department. In New York, Chicago, Houston, Philadelphia, San Antonio, Dallas, Austin, Columbus, Charlotte, Denver, El Paso, Boston, Memphis, Atlanta and Baltimore you start with 311. If you have a Section 8 voucher, also call your housing authority and ask for a special inspection: it can stop paying the landlord its share of the rent until the repairs are made. Send the landlord a written repair request first, so you can prove you asked.
