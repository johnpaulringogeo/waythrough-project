What Happens When Your Section 8 Voucher Expires

Matt · April 14, 2026 · Updated November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Yes, Section 8 vouchers expire. Most PHAs give you 60 days to find a unit, some give 90 or 120, and many grant extensions if you ask in time. If you don't find an apartment and complete the lease-up process within your search period, the voucher expires and you're back to square one.

This is one of the most stressful parts of the Section 8 process, and it happens more often than people realize. Here's exactly what happens when your voucher expires, how to prevent it, and what your options are if time is running out.

How Long Do You Have?

Most PHAs issue vouchers with an initial search period of 60 days. Some give 90 or 120 days upfront. The search period starts the day the voucher is issued — not the day you start looking, not the day you have your briefing. The clock begins immediately.

During this time, you need to find a landlord who accepts vouchers, submit a Request for Tenancy Approval (RFTA) to your PHA, pass the housing quality inspection, and sign your lease. That's a lot of steps crammed into a short window, especially in a tight rental market.

Can You Get an Extension?

Yes — and this is the most important thing to know. Most PHAs will grant at least one extension if you ask before the voucher expires. Here's how:

  • Ask early. Don't wait until the last week. Contact your PHA 2-3 weeks before expiration and request an extension in writing (email is ideal so you have a record). Our housing authority directory has the phone number and portal for each authority we cover; HUD's PHA contact directory covers the rest.
  • Show you've been searching. Keep a log of every landlord you contacted, every listing you responded to, every apartment you viewed. PHAs are much more likely to extend if you can prove you've been actively looking.
  • Explain barriers. If landlords in your area are refusing vouchers, if you have a disability that makes the search harder, or if the rental market is exceptionally tight, explain this. These are legitimate reasons for an extension.
  • If a disability is part of the problem, ask for the extension as a reasonable accommodation. This is the one kind of extension that isn't up to the PHA. Under 24 CFR 982.303(b)(2), if a family member with a disability needs more time and you ask for it, the PHA must extend the voucher for as long as is reasonably needed for that purpose. In your request, say how the disability makes the search take longer, for example you need a unit with no stairs, a unit near your treatment, or help from a caseworker to tour units.

Most extensions add 30-60 days. Some PHAs allow multiple extensions up to a total of 120 or even 180 days. Ask your PHA what their policy is. Extensions are the PHA's call, not HUD's — HUD's search-and-leasing guidance (PDF) sets the 60-day floor and leaves the rest to each agency's written policy. The exception is a reasonable accommodation extension, described above, which the PHA must grant when a disability makes it necessary.

The Clock Stops While the PHA Reviews Your Unit

Many renters never hear about this rule, and it isn't optional for the PHA. Under 24 CFR 982.303(c), your PHA must suspend (pause) your voucher's term from the day you turn in a Request for Tenancy Approval (RFTA) until the day the PHA tells you in writing whether it approved or denied the unit. Housing staff sometimes call this "tolling."

That means the days the PHA spends scheduling the inspection, waiting on the landlord, or reviewing the rent shouldn't count against your search time. Example: your 60-day voucher was issued March 1, so it ends April 30. You turn in an RFTA on April 1. The PHA sends you a written denial on April 20. Your clock was paused while the PHA had your RFTA, so your end date moves back by about those 19 days, to around May 19. Each PHA's written plan says exactly how it counts the days, so always ask for the new date in writing.

How to protect yourself:

  • Get proof of the date you turned in the RFTA. Ask for a date-stamped copy if you hand it in, or send it by email or another way that gives you a receipt.
  • Keep every written notice about the unit, including the approval or denial letter and the date on it.
  • Ask for your new expiration date in writing each time a unit is approved or denied. A short email works: "Please confirm my voucher's new expiration date, counting the suspension from [RFTA date] to [decision date] under 24 CFR 982.303(c)."

What Happens If It Actually Expires

If your voucher expires without a successful lease-up, the voucher is returned to the PHA's pool. Here's what that means for you:

  • You do not keep your place on the waiting list. Your position is gone. To get another voucher, you'd need to reapply the next time the waiting list opens.
  • The PHA reissues the voucher to someone else. The funding doesn't disappear — it goes to the next person on the list.
  • There's usually no penalty for trying again. An expired voucher doesn't disqualify you from future applications. But you'll be starting from the bottom of a waiting list that could be years long.

If It Expired Because of PHA Delay or Missing Documents

An expired voucher isn't always the end. If the PHA's own delay, or a document problem you couldn't control, used up your time, ask the PHA in writing to reinstate the voucher or recalculate its end date. Send the request as soon as you can, ideally within a few days of the expiration date. The longer you wait, the more likely the voucher has gone to the next family on the list.

  1. Build a timeline. Write down the date the voucher was issued, each date you turned in an RFTA, each inspection date (including ones the PHA rescheduled or never set), and the date of each written decision. If missing documents (like a birth certificate or ID) held things up, write down when you asked for them and when they came.
  2. Apply the suspension rule. If the PHA didn't pause the clock while it had your RFTA, your voucher may not have actually expired yet. Say so, and show the math.
  3. Send a written request. Ask for (a) a recalculated expiration date under 24 CFR 982.303(c), or (b) reinstatement and an extension because of the delay. Attach your timeline and copies of your papers. If a disability played a part, say so and ask for the extension as a reasonable accommodation under 24 CFR 982.303(b)(2).
  4. Ask for the answer in writing, with the reason, and ask what review the PHA's administrative plan offers if you disagree.

If they say no: Federal rules don't require the PHA to give you an informal review or hearing just because it refused a regular extension (24 CFR 982.554(c)(4) for applicants, 982.555(b)(4) for families already in the program). But the suspension rule and the accommodation rule are requirements, not favors. If the PHA won't follow them, ask for a supervisor in writing. Then call HUD's Public and Indian Housing Customer Service Center at 1-800-955-2232 (weekdays, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Eastern) and ask how to reach the HUD office that oversees your PHA. If a disability accommodation was refused, you can also file a complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing (1-800-669-9777). HUD has time limits for complaints, so file as soon as you can. A free legal aid office can help you write the request.

Why Vouchers Expire (The Real Reasons)

In my experience working with voucher holders, these are the most common reasons vouchers expire unused:

  • Landlord discrimination. In states without source of income protections, landlords can legally refuse vouchers. In competitive markets, many do — they'd rather take a market-rate tenant who can start immediately without inspections.
  • Payment standard too low. If the PHA's payment standard hasn't kept up with market rents, there may literally be no apartments available at the voucher amount. You can rent above the standard, but your out-of-pocket share increases — and it's capped at 40% of your income at initial lease-up.
  • Failed inspections. You find a willing landlord, but the unit fails its Housing Quality Standards (HQS) inspection. The landlord doesn't want to make repairs. You're back to searching with less time on the clock.
  • Not knowing the process. Some people receive their voucher and don't fully understand the steps — the RFTA form, the inspection, the approval timeline. Days slip away before they realize how urgent it is.

How to Avoid Losing Your Voucher

Start your search before you even have the voucher in hand. The moment you know your name is coming up on the list, begin identifying landlords who accept vouchers and units that might work. The day you get the voucher, you should already have a list of places to contact.

Other tips that make a real difference:

  • Ask your PHA for a landlord list. Many PHAs maintain lists of landlords who have previously accepted vouchers. This is your best starting point.
  • Use targeted search terms. On Craigslist, Zillow, and Apartments.com, search for "Section 8 accepted" or "vouchers welcome."
  • Cast a wide geographic net. If your area is too expensive, remember that many vouchers are portable — you can use them in a different city or county with a lower cost of living.
  • Have your documents ready. When a landlord says yes, don't lose days gathering paperwork. Have your RFTA form, ID, income verification, and references ready to submit immediately.
  • Walk the unit with the inspection checklist before committing. Don't waste your limited time on a unit that's going to fail inspection.

If You Lost Your Voucher — What Now

If your voucher has already expired, don't give up. Here's what to do:

  • Reapply to every PHA with an open waiting list. Use our open waiting list guide to find PHAs currently accepting applications. Apply to multiple — there's no limit.
  • Look into LIHTC housing. Tax credit apartments offer below-market rents without needing a voucher. Many have shorter waiting lists.
  • Contact 211. Call or text 211 for local housing assistance, emergency rental help, and other programs that don't require a voucher.
