Section 8 Changes in 2027: What Voucher Holders Should Know

Matt · December 2, 2026 · 9 min read

Starting January 1, 2027, most housing authorities must use HUD's new income and asset rules, from a 2016 law called HOTMA, for every Section 8 and public housing rent calculation. The biggest changes for families already on the program: a limit of $109,797 on net assets in 2027, a rule against owning a home you could live in, new deductions, and new rules for reporting income changes during the year.

Many common savings, like retirement accounts, college savings, and tax refunds, do not count toward the asset limit. And your housing authority (PHA) can choose not to enforce the limit for families already on the program. But the rules will change how your rent is figured at your next recertification. Here is what changes, when, and what to do before your next recertification.

When Do the New Section 8 Rules Start?

HUD's Notice PIH 2026-15, issued May 14, 2026, says HUD will enforce the new rules starting January 1, 2027. Any annual or interim recertification that takes effect on or after that date must follow them. That covers Housing Choice Vouchers, project-based vouchers, and public housing.

  • Your PHA may already be using them. PHAs that start annual recertifications 120 days early had to begin using the new rules to collect and check family income in September 2026. So the paperwork you fill out this fall may already follow the new rules.
  • Some PHAs switch later. PHAs in HUD's Moving to Work program, and PHAs that only use HUD's Family Reporting Software, will switch on a date HUD has not announced yet.
  • Some changes already happened. No new families could start the Earned Income Disregard after January 1, 2024. New income exclusions and definitions took effect by July 1, 2025.

The fastest way to know which rules apply to you is to ask your caseworker: "Will my next recertification use the HOTMA rules, and what is its effective date?"

Is There a Savings Limit on Section 8 Now?

Yes. Under the new rules, a PHA cannot give assistance, when you first apply or at a recertification, to a family whose net assets are over $109,797 in 2027. HUD adjusts that amount every year for inflation.

"Net assets" means the cash value of things like bank accounts, stocks, bonds and real estate, minus what it would cost to sell them. These do not count:

  • Retirement accounts the IRS recognizes, like a 401(k), an IRA, or a pension plan
  • ABLE accounts, 529 college plans, Coverdell education accounts, and government "baby bond" accounts
  • Your Family Self-Sufficiency (FSS) escrow account
  • Federal tax refunds and refundable tax credits, for 12 months after you get them
  • Necessary personal property, such as a car you use to get around every day
  • Other personal property, like jewelry or collectibles, if it is worth $54,898 or less in total in 2027
  • Money from a lawsuit or settlement over an injury that caused a family member's disability

Also know: if you give away assets or sell them for less than they are worth, the PHA counts that value for two years. A sale or transfer in a divorce settlement does not count against you this way if you get something of value in return, even if it is not money.

Can You Lose Your Voucher for Having Too Much Savings?

It is possible, but there is room for your PHA to protect current families. HUD's rule at 24 CFR 5.618 says that when a PHA recertifies a family already on the program, it may:

  • Choose not to enforce the asset limit at all, or
  • Make exceptions based on things like age, disability, income, or how hard it would be to find other housing, and
  • Wait up to 6 months before starting to end assistance or evict a family that is over the limit.

A PHA can only do this through a written policy, usually in its Administrative Plan (for vouchers) or its admissions and occupancy policy (for public housing). Ask your PHA for the section on the asset limit. If you are close to the limit, find out the policy now, not at your recertification.

What If You Own a House?

The new rules also stop assistance for a family that owns a home it has the legal right to live in and the power to sell, if the home is suitable for the family to live in. There are exceptions. The rule does not apply to:

  • A home you bought through the voucher homeownership program
  • A home owned with someone outside your household who lives there
  • A survivor of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking (see our guide to Section 8 protections for survivors)
  • A home that is for sale

A home is also not "suitable" if you show it does not meet a family member's disability needs, is too small for your family, is so far away that it would be a hardship (for example, a very long trip to work or school), is unsafe and cannot be easily fixed, or is a place local law does not let you live. The same PHA policy that covers the asset limit applies here too.

How Assets Will Change Your Rent

If your net assets are $54,898 or less in 2027, the PHA can accept your signed statement about your assets instead of asking for bank statements. You still have to list any income those assets earn, like interest, and that income counts.

If your net assets are over $54,898, the PHA counts the actual income they earn. When it cannot figure out the actual income from an asset, it uses HUD's passbook rate, which is 0.38% for 2027. If your assets are $54,898 or less, the PHA cannot add made-up income on top of what your assets really earn.

New Deductions in 2027

Your rent is about 30% of your adjusted income, so deductions matter. Under the new rules for 2027:

  • Dependents: $525 per dependent each year, up from $480.
  • Elderly or disabled family: $575 each year, up from $400.
  • Medical and disability expenses: only count above 10% of your income, up from 3%. If you were already getting this deduction on January 1, 2024, you get phased-in relief: a 5% threshold for 12 months, then 7.5% for 12 months, then 10%. Elderly and disabled families, and families with a member who has a disability, can also ask for hardship relief at 5% for 90 days, which the PHA can extend.
  • Child care: if your child care deduction is ending but you still need the care, you can ask to keep it for up to 90 days as a hardship exemption, and the PHA can extend it.

Our guide on how your rent is calculated walks through the full formula with examples under both the old and new rules. You can also check your numbers with our Know Your Numbers calculator.

Reporting Income Changes During the Year

The new rules also change interim recertifications, the rent changes between your yearly reviews:

  • A raise at work generally does not count until your next annual recertification, unless you got an interim rent decrease earlier in the same year.
  • Other income increases that raise your adjusted income by 10% or more trigger an interim recertification. The PHA can skip an interim in the last three months before your annual review.
  • If your income drops, the PHA can decline to lower your rent if your adjusted income falls by less than 10%, unless it sets a lower threshold. A decrease takes effect the first of the month after the change you reported.
  • If your rent goes up and you reported on time, you get 30 days' notice. If you did not report on time, the increase can be charged back to the month after the change.

Always follow your PHA's reporting rules. Read what happens when your income goes up for more.

What to Do Before Your Next Recertification

  1. Find your recertification date. Look at your last notice or ask your caseworker if the new rules apply to it.
  2. List your assets. Write down every bank account, cash app balance, investment and property, and what each one earns. Leave out the ones that do not count.
  3. Gather proof of your deductions. Keep receipts for medical bills, insurance premiums, disability-related costs and child care.
  4. Ask for the policies. Ask your PHA for the parts of its Administrative Plan on the asset limit, property ownership, and hardship exemptions. Our tips on dealing with your housing authority can help.
  5. Ask for a hardship exemption in writing if your rent goes up because of the new medical or child care rules and you cannot pay.
  6. Check the math. Compare your new rent notice to your own numbers. If you think the PHA got your income wrong, HUD's rules give voucher holders the right to an informal hearing on how their income and rent were figured. Ask for it in writing and keep a copy.

Frequently Asked Questions

When do the new Section 8 rules start?

HUD will enforce the new HOTMA income and asset rules for most housing authorities starting January 1, 2027. Any annual or interim recertification that takes effect on or after that date must follow them. Housing authorities in the Moving to Work program, and those that only use HUD's Family Reporting Software, will switch later, on a date HUD has not announced yet.

How much money can you have in the bank on Section 8 in 2027?

Under the new rules, a family generally cannot get or keep assistance if its net assets are over $109,797 in 2027. Retirement accounts, ABLE accounts, 529 college savings, Family Self-Sufficiency escrow, and federal tax refunds for 12 months after you get them do not count. For families already on the program, your housing authority can choose not to enforce the limit or can make exceptions, but only under a written policy.

Can I lose my Section 8 voucher if I own a house?

You can if you own a home you could legally live in and have the right to sell, unless an exception applies. Exceptions include a home bought through the voucher homeownership program, a home that is for sale, a home a co-owner lives in, and survivors of domestic violence. A home that does not fit your family's disability needs, is too small, is too far away, or is unsafe does not count.

Do I have to report a raise at work right away?

Follow your housing authority's reporting rules. Under the new rules, the housing authority generally does not count a raise in earned income until your next annual recertification, unless you got an interim rent decrease earlier in the same year. Other income increases that raise your adjusted income by 10% or more trigger an interim recertification.
