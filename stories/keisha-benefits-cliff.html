Keisha: Escaping the Benefits Cliff

Waythrough Project

Names and identifying details have been changed. These stories are based on real experiences shared with Waythrough Project.

The Trap

Keisha had been on Section 8 for four years. She worked full-time as a dental hygienist at a community health center, earned about $38,000 annually, and received a Section 8 housing voucher that covered most of her rent. Her share of the rent was set at about 30% of her adjusted income, about $950 per month. The voucher covered the rest. It made life manageable: she could pay for childcare for her two kids, maintain reliable transportation, and put small amounts into a savings account.

In early 2025, she was offered a promotion: senior dental hygienist, managing operations at the clinic. The new salary would be $47,000: a $9,000 annual raise, about $700 extra per month before taxes. It was exactly what she needed to keep advancing, maybe eventually go back to school for dental school prerequisites. She said yes immediately.

Then the clinic's HR person mentioned something casually: "Just so you know, when your income goes up, your Section 8 subsidy will go down. You might want to check with your PHA about how much."

The Shock

Keisha called the PHA to understand how the raise would affect her voucher. The calculation was brutal. Under Section 8 rules, her tenant contribution stays about 30% of her adjusted income, so it rises when her income rises. The raise would push her income to $47,000. At that level, her share of the rent would go up by roughly $225 per month, and her subsidy would go down by the same amount. There is no set income cutoff for keeping a voucher once you have one. Help ends only if her share grows so large that the housing authority's payment drops to $0. If that lasts 180 days, the voucher contract ends (24 CFR 982.455). A $47,000 salary was nowhere near that point.

"I was celebrating one minute and panicking the next," Keisha remembers. "The raise was supposed to help me, but it was going to take away my housing subsidy. The math didn't work."

The so-called "benefits cliff" is real. When housing subsidies or other benefits are designed to phase out as income rises, it can feel like earning more costs more. In Keisha's case, accepting the promotion would mean her rent would rise from $950 to about $1,175. After taxes and that $225 rent increase, much of the raise seemed to disappear.

Finding a Solution

Keisha almost turned down the promotion. But before making that decision, she called the PHA and asked if there were programs designed specifically for this situation. The case worker mentioned something: the Family Self-Sufficiency (FSS) program.

The FSS program is a federal initiative built specifically to address the benefits cliff. Here's how it works: when you enroll, the PHA records your current income and rent. If your earnings go up during the program (usually five years), your rent contribution still rises as normal, but the PHA puts an amount equal to that increase into an escrow account in your name each month. When you complete the program, you get that escrow money, an incentive for actually getting ahead. If you leave without completing it, you usually lose the escrow (24 CFR 984.305).

Keisha had never heard of FSS. The PHA's FSS coordinator, an older woman named Diane, explained the program in concrete terms: "You take the promotion. Your income goes up. Your rent contribution goes up too, to about $1,175. The voucher still covers the rest. But the PHA puts an amount equal to your rent increase (about $225 a month) into your escrow account. In five years, if you've met your goals and stayed compliant, you get that accumulated escrow. It's yours."

The FSS Agreement

Keisha enrolled in FSS. The program required a "Five-Year Self-Sufficiency Plan." Basically, what were her employment and financial goals? With Diane's help, Keisha outlined them: stay employed full-time, maintain stable housing, save $12,000 over five years, and pursue dental school prerequisites through evening community college classes.

In exchange, Keisha agreed to regular check-ins with Diane (quarterly) to update on her progress, to stay employed or seek employment if she lost a job, and to maintain her apartment in good condition. FSS wasn't charity. It was a partnership. She had to do the work; the program removed the perverse incentive that punished earning more.

Diane walked her through the numbers: "You take the promotion at $47,000. Your contribution goes up about $225. That same $225 a month goes into escrow, about $2,700 per year. Over five years, if your income stays stable and you don't lose the voucher, that's roughly $13,500 in escrow, plus interest, because the escrow account earns interest."

Life After FSS

Keisha took the promotion. Her rent went up to about $1,175, and about $225 a month went into her escrow account. She attended evening classes at the community college, completing prerequisites for dental school applications. She passed the Dental Admission Test (DAT) in early 2026. Three dental schools have already invited her to interview. Her five-year FSS term will end in early 2030, but she expects to be in dental school by then, no longer needing Section 8.

The escrow account now holds approximately $5,400 (she's two years in). If she meets her FSS goals before the five years are up, the PHA can pay out the escrow early. The PHA can also choose to release part of it for school costs once she meets interim goals. That money could become a down payment for a place of her own, or help with school expenses. But more importantly, she accepted the promotion guilt-free. She earned more, and it actually helped her, not hurt her.

"The benefits cliff is real," Keisha says, "but FSS is the fix. I wish everyone knew about it. If you're on Section 8 and you're worried about what happens when you get a better job, ask your PHA about FSS. It's the program that makes getting ahead actually possible."

Key Takeaways

  • The benefits cliff is real but not permanent: When your income increases, Section 8 subsidies decrease. It can feel like earning more costs you money. But programs exist to address this.
  • Family Self-Sufficiency (FSS) is a lifeline: Your rent contribution still rises with your income, but the PHA puts an amount equal to the increase into an escrow account you get when you complete the program. Not all PHAs run FSS, but ask. It's a federal program and many do.
  • FSS has real requirements: You need to commit to work, check in regularly with a case manager, and maintain your housing. But it's a partnership, not a hand-out. You're investing in yourself.
  • Your self-sufficiency plan matters: Think beyond just work. Include goals like education, savings, building credit. FSS can include support for job training, childcare help, and financial counseling.
  • Take the promotion: If you're worried about benefits, explore FSS first. But don't turn down career advancement because you're scared of losing housing. The system is designed to help you move up, not trap you.
  • Stay in touch with your PHA: Report income changes. Check in with your case manager. The system works better when you engage with it actively.
